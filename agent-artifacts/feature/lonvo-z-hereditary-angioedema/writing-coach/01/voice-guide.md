# Voice guide: feature/lonvo-z-hereditary-angioedema (01)

## How this piece should sound

The readers can follow a derivation and have built systems, and they have no
medical training. The register that suits them is exact about the thing, plain
about the words, and at ease saying what is not known. Derek Lowe and Scott Alexander
both write this way about drugs for people who are not clinicians. Ruxandra
Teslo does it with a more formal hand and more numbers.

Terms get their gloss at the moment they are needed, and the gloss is short.
Scott Alexander stops on "positive allosteric modulator", says what it means in
one sentence, and ties it to drugs the reader has heard of. Lowe spells out
PCSK9 once, in full, and then uses the abbreviation. The cascade in this piece
(prekallikrein, kallikrein, bradykinin, the places where each drug acts) can be
handled the same way: the practitioners' names stay, each introduced once with
what it does. An analogy from the reader's own field is allowed where it makes
the cascade easier to follow than a definition would. None of the three writers
uses one in the passages below, so it needs to earn its place.

Every figure can arrive with the things a reader needs to weigh it. Look at how
Scott Alexander reports a side effect: two people, about 2% of the study
population, and what the researchers made of it. Look at how he calls the first
study "open-label (ie no placebo) and very small (only four women)" in the same
breath as its result. Teslo puts a dose beside the dose that came before it, so
that "remarkably small" can be checked. Lowe says a trial will enroll 40
patients in stated rounds. This piece's central work is putting endpoints from
different trials on one footing, so the denominator, the period and the
comparator belong in the sentence that carries the number.

Where the data stop, a plain clause can say what is missing and what would
show it. Lowe's "We'll see how that goes" is followed at once by the three
things that would be seen. That is a different thing from a sentence rating the
writer's own caution, which `spec/slop.md` cuts.

A little wit fits and none is required. Lowe's "(technical term there)" is a
parenthesis that costs three words, and Scott Alexander's jokes are built from
the nouns of the subject. This piece reports a death in a sister program, and
that passage should stay level. The brief and the commission decide that, and
the exemplars cannot.

The person on the page shows in what gets picked out and checked. Scott
Alexander notices that lower doses beat higher ones, says that is sometimes a
red flag, and says why he does not think it is one here. Lowe notes which
patients a disease affects and when the heart attacks start. The first-person
voice and the running jokes belong to those two writers and are not part of what
to take. The paper's own voice reaches the same exactness without borrowing
either persona.

The brief asks for a five-minute read built around visuals, so the prose
beside each figure is short and says how to read it. Teslo's account of the 2011
first-in-human result shows what a few sentences can hold: the dose, the
comparison, the measured change and the duration.

## Derek Lowe, "More CRISPR In Human Subjects"

Source: https://www.science.org/content/blog-post/more-crispr-human-subjects

> "Familial hypercholesterolemia, for its part, is pretty much what the name implies: an inherited genetic disorder that leads to high levels of cholesterol, specifically high LDL levels. That's famously associated with notably poor cardiovascular outcomes with aging, but in HeFH patients the heart attacks start showing up in the 20s and 30s."

Checked: https://gwern.net/doc/www/www.science.org/2ffe4c81525bbefc146eac56d559ad1d31b9254f.html (a mirror of the Science page; science.org itself returned 403 to curl), retrieved 2026-11-14
Lowe defines a disease by reading its name back to the reader, then gives the age at which the consequence appears. The abbreviation HeFH is introduced without ceremony, and the sentence about heart attacks says who it happens to and when.

> "That whacking (technical term there) is being done in Verve's case by using CRISPR base-editing technology to make a specific A-to-G change in the PCSK9 gene. That site was picked because it's not similar to anything else in the genome (so the guide RNA should take the machinery only to the place where it's being targeted) ..."

Checked: https://gwern.net/doc/www/www.science.org/2ffe4c81525bbefc146eac56d559ad1d31b9254f.html, retrieved 2026-11-14
The mechanism is stated with the exact edit (an A-to-G change) and the reason that site was chosen. The jokes are three words long, and a parenthesis explains what the guide RNA is for.

> "The trial is supposed to enroll 40 patients, with a first round of ascending-dose to look for the best effects, followed by a round of treatment at the selected dose, and perhaps another second dose for patients who got the lower doses the first time around or did not respond well. We'll see how that goes - how efficient the CRISPR base editing really is in humans, how beneficial it is if it does work, and what other things may happen along the way."

Checked: https://gwern.net/doc/www/www.science.org/2ffe4c81525bbefc146eac56d559ad1d31b9254f.html, retrieved 2026-11-14
Lowe gives the design of an unfinished trial in one sentence with its size. He then lists the three things not yet known, so the uncertainty comes with contents.

## Scott Alexander, "Zounds! It's Zulresso and Zuranolone!"

Source: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/zounds-its-zulresso-and-zuranolone

> "GABA is the main inhibitory neurotransmitter; it’s usually associated with relaxation and sedation. A positive allosteric modulator is a chemical that makes receptors respond more strongly to their targets. So “a positive allosteric modulator of GABA” means a chemical that makes the brain respond stronger to relaxation/sedation signals. Sounds pretty useful!"

Checked: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/zounds-its-zulresso-and-zuranolone, retrieved 2026-11-14
He defines the term, then restates the phrase in the reader's words, and the whole thing takes four short sentences. The "Sounds pretty useful!" is the person: he has just explained something and reacts to it the way a reader would.

> "Still, Kanes et al tried this in 2017. The study was open-label (ie no placebo) and very small (only four women) but appeared to work extraordinarily well. Four post-partum women who qualified as “severely depressed” when they started the infusion progressed to “completely recovered” within twelve hours. Nothing else except maybe ketamine had produced results like this before."

Checked: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/zounds-its-zulresso-and-zuranolone, retrieved 2026-11-14
The design caveats (open-label, four women) sit in the same sentence as the result. The size of the effect is then placed against the one other drug the reader might know that behaved that way.

> "Both studies also evaluated side effects. These were generally mild, but two people (about 2% of the study population) lost consciousness. Nothing seemed wrong with them, and researchers mostly attributed this to allopregnanolone being a sedating drug. If you sedate people too hard, they pass out."

Checked: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/zounds-its-zulresso-and-zuranolone, retrieved 2026-11-14
A safety finding is given as a count and a percentage of the population, followed by who explained it and how. The last sentence is a plain statement of the mechanism behind the explanation, in the register of everyday speech.

## Ruxandra Teslo, "Clinic-in-the-Loop"

Source: https://www.asimov.press/p/clinic-loop

> "CAR-T therapy works by turning a patient’s own immune cells into living drugs. Doctors collect T cells from the blood, genetically reprogram them to recognize a protein on cancer cells, and reinfuse the modified T-cells into the patient. These engineered cells expand inside the body, move to tumor sites, and destroy malignant cells."

Checked: https://www.asimov.press/p/clinic-loop, retrieved 2026-11-14
A therapy's mechanism is told as a sequence of four verbs in the order they happen to the patient's cells. No term is left undefined, and the paragraph stops when the sequence stops.

> "In laboratory experiments, these first-generation CAR-Ts were formidable, displaying antigen recognition and potent killing power against tumor cell lines. Yet, this in vitro prowess vanished in patients and did not yield durable clinical responses. Understanding why this happened, though, was not simple. The failure could have been caused by a breakdown in in vivo antigen recognition, poor signaling strength, or other defects that only emerged after the cells were injected into the body."

Checked: https://www.asimov.press/p/clinic-loop, retrieved 2026-11-14
She keeps the field's own terms (in vitro, in vivo, antigen recognition) and lists the candidate explanations for a failure by name. The contrast between the bench result and the patient result is a single sentence.

> "They also administered a dose that was remarkably small by cell therapy standards at that time: just 1.5 × 10⁵ CAR-T cells per kilogram of body weight. (A first-generation CAR-T trial targeting renal cell carcinoma, published in 2013, used a dose more than 100-times higher.) What followed was unprecedented. The CAR-T cells multiplied more than a thousandfold in patients, at their peak comprising a large fraction of immune cells in the blood. The CAR-T cells also persisted for months."

Checked: https://www.asimov.press/p/clinic-loop, retrieved 2026-11-14
The dose is given with a figure for comparison, in a parenthesis, so that "remarkably small" can be checked by the reader. The result then follows in short sentences with a multiplier and a duration.
