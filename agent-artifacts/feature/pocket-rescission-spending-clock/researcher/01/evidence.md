# Evidence: pocket-rescission spending clock

Research current through **September 27, 2026**. The documentary record supports a narrower and more exact frame than the White House headline: the September 25 special message proposes 11 rescissions totaling **$809,501,022** from specifically identified Treasury appropriation fund symbols (TAFS), all of which the message identifies as ending in FY2026. The five calendar days from the announcement to September 30 are not the Impoundment Control Act (ICA) clock. Both chambers were adjourned from September 24 to September 28, those intervening days are excluded from “continuous session,” and the public record reviewed here does not establish when both chambers received the message. Nor does September 30 itself enact a rescission: absent legislation, expiring unobligated balances become unavailable for new obligations under their enacted availability terms and move into expired-account status; they are not immediately canceled by presidential action.

## Contradictions

1. **“Nearly $1 billion” versus the document total.** The exact proposals total **$809,501,022**. That is **$190,498,978 short of $1 billion** and equals **80.95% of $1 billion**. The rounded public bullets total $810 million, consistent with the special message's rounded cover total but materially below the headline phrase.

2. **Proposal versus completed cancellation.** The White House speaks of eliminating spending and calls the move a pocket rescission. The legal document is a proposal under §683(a). Unless Congress enacts a rescission, September 30 can end new-obligation availability under the appropriations acts; it does not convert the President's proposal into a congressionally enacted cancellation.

3. **Expiration versus cancellation.** An expiring TAFS becomes unavailable for new obligations after its availability period. Under 31 U.S.C. §1552 it then remains in expired status for existing-obligation accounting, generally for five years, before the remaining balance is canceled. “Lapsed,” “expired,” “rescinded,” and “canceled” are not interchangeable.

4. **The “five-day clock” versus the statutory clock.** September 25 to September 30 is five elapsed days after the announcement. It is not five continuous-session days. September 25-27 were within both chambers' longer adjournments, and the statutory start depends on congressional receipt. At most three countable session days preceded September 30 on the facts publicly established here.

5. **Account names versus the targeted TAFS.** FY2026 Housing Counseling Assistance remains available through September 30, 2027, but R26-10 targets FY2025 HCA, TAFS 2025/2026. “Education for the Disadvantaged” also contains advance-funded FY2026 money available through 2027, while R26-4 identifies a 2026/2026 slice. A broad statement that all money in all 11 named accounts expires September 30 would be false.

6. **GAO versus OMB on the core legal question.** GAO reads “shall be made available” to require release early enough for prudent obligation and rejects withholding through expiration. OMB reads the lack of a transmission deadline and the 45-day framework as authority to continue withholding even if the enacted period ends first; it points to the express cross-year restriction on deferrals that Congress omitted from the rescission provision. This is a live interpretive conflict, not a neutral fact settled by the statute's title.

7. **The 2025 Supreme Court stay versus claims of a merits victory.** The order credited the government's preliminary showing concerning private APA enforcement and mandamus and balanced the equities. It expressly said the ruling was not a final merits determination. It does not establish that every pocket rescission is legal.

8. **Public posting versus statutory receipt.** OMB posted a September 25 package and the cover letter is addressed to the Speaker. The ICA requires same-day transmission to both chambers and permits officer delivery during adjournment. Without receipt records from both chambers, the legal clock's start date should remain an open field in the story.

9. **Current congressional charge versus verified package scope.** Senator Murray calls the move illegal and alleges additional problematic withholding beyond the special message. Her “over $800 million” description matches the package's scale. The broader allegations are not part of the $809,501,022 package and need their own account-level records before inclusion.

## Limits

- **Most important limit:** No public House-and-Senate receipt record for the September 25, 2026 message was located by the September 27 cutoff. The first day of the 45-day period therefore cannot be stated as a verified date. The defensible formulation is that September 25-27 did not count and that no more than three countable session days could precede September 30 if both chambers received the message on September 25.
- The package states that the proposed amounts are unobligated balances, but this research did not locate public apportionment/reapportionment schedules or agency ledger data independently proving each live balance at transmission or tracking obligations between September 25 and September 30.
- The message's TAFS and the enacted availability clauses establish when the identified budget authority may incur new obligations. They do not by themselves establish whether all programmatic prerequisites could have been completed in the remaining time. GAO's “prudent obligation” standard is program-specific.
- The P.L. 117-328 debt-relief locator is the enrolled bill's Division K, title III, PDF page 533. Before quoting the clause in published copy, re-open that page in the official PDF and retain the page image with the reporting file.
- CourtListener is a secondary docket mirror. Recheck PACER or the official court docket for any new merits order immediately before publication. The Supreme Court docket and order are sufficient to say the September 26, 2025 action was interim and explicitly not a final merits ruling.
- CRS LSB11374 predates the Supreme Court's September 26, 2025 order. Use it for the structure of the competing legal arguments, not for the litigation's current procedural posture.
- GAO's opinions are central and directly analogous, but they are not Article III holdings. Senator Murray's statement is a primary record of a congressional objection, not proof of illegality.
- No inference should be drawn from a same-named FY2026 account to a different source-year TAFS. The clearest trap is Housing Counseling Assistance: R26-10 is 2025/2026 even though the FY2026 account lasts into 2027.

## Discarded

- Secondary news reports summarizing the September 25 package: displaced by the White House announcement and the special message.
- Any arithmetic that treats “nearly $1 billion” as the package's exact value: contradicted by the 11 proposal sheets.
- The White House's political descriptions of recipients and program effects as proof of waste, misconduct, or legal ineligibility: the cited announcement supplies assertions, not audits or adjudications.
- The FY2026 Housing Counseling Assistance end date as evidence for R26-10: wrong source year and wrong TAFS.
- A blanket statement that “all 11 accounts expire September 30”: too broad. Only the 11 identified balances are shown to end then; same-named accounts can contain later-available money.
- A blanket statement that the funds were “canceled” October 1: inconsistent with 31 U.S.C. §1552's expired-account regime.
- Partisan congressional releases as legal authority: retained only where needed to identify a current objection and exact title; the legal analysis rests on statutes, enacted laws, GAO, court filings, and the Supreme Court order.
- Wikipedia, Justia, advocacy fact sheets, and unsourced docket summaries: useful discovery leads, superseded by official documents or, for current status, the clearly labeled CourtListener mirror.
- Treating CRS LSB11374's pre-order litigation account as current: retained only for its balanced map of GAO and OMB arguments.
- Treating the Supreme Court's 2025 stay as a final holding that pocket rescissions are lawful: directly contradicted by the order's limiting language.
