# Voice guide: feature/redistricting-ballot-measures-2026 (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a results-and-mechanism piece for readers who build models for a living and do not follow election law. Its register is a careful elections analyst explaining a count to a smart colleague: plain declarative sentences, state names and measure numbers in every line, and the procedural vocabulary used exactly (veto referendum, legislatively referred constitutional amendment, independent commission, certified versus unofficial canvass). David Nir's tracker shows how far this goes: one parenthesis explains why a California map change needs a constitutional amendment at all, and the reader never has to ask. Where a measure works by an odd legal route, the material may call for one sentence like that, placed where the measure first appears.

Treat the reader as somebody who will check the arithmetic. Jonathan Rodden's passage on competitiveness names the assumption behind a count, the single 2024 presidential result, before giving his own. A net seat figure for the House rests on the same kind of choice: which map is the baseline, which seats count as changed, whose rating says a seat moved. If the piece reaches a net figure, the reader should see which baseline it is computed from and who owns any partisan projection, in the way Rodden attributes the Times and Politico method before departing from it. If the sources do not support a net figure, say what they do support and stop. That is a finding too.

Bolts shows the fair-treatment brief in practice. Each position is given in the words of a named person holding it (Levitt, Pildes), and the explanatory sentences around the quotations stay neutral and short. Proponents' and opponents' cases for each measure can sit in that form, attributed, with the legal effect described separately in the paper's own voice. Keep any wit inside the subject's nouns, as Nir does when he describes delegations "effectively maxed out," and keep the five-minute budget in mind: the table and the drawn figure do the comparing, and the prose says how to read them.

## Jonathan Rodden, "Did Mid-Decade Redistricting Make Congressional Districts Less Competitive?"

Source: https://jonathanrodden1.substack.com/p/did-mid-decade-redistricting-make

> "Who is right? The answer depends on how much weight one places on the geography of the 2024 Presidential Election when predicting future elections. The strategy of Republican map-drawers, and the classification scheme adopted by the Times and Politico, assumes that the conditions of the 2024 election will be replicated in 2026 and beyond. A district is “safe” if the margin of victory of Donald Trump or Kamala Harris was more than 10 percentage points in 2024."

Checked: https://jonathanrodden1.substack.com/p/did-mid-decade-redistricting-make (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-09
The question is answered at once, and the answer is an assumption the reader can inspect, ending on the exact threshold. Rodden is a political geographer, and his habit of locating a disagreement in the data choice rather than in the people is what makes him visible here.

> "However, when evaluating redistricting plans, specialists typically examine results of all statewide elections held over several cycles. The idea is to avoid drawing conclusions based on a single election that might be idiosyncratic."

Checked: https://jonathanrodden1.substack.com/p/did-mid-decade-redistricting-make (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-09
Two sentences give the field's standard practice and the reason for it, in words a non-specialist can follow. He speaks as someone who does this work, without credentials on display.
