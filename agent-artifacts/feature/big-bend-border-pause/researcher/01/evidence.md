# Evidence record: Big Bend border projects and Sept. 30 filing

Prepared for the feature assignment. This is a reporting record, not article copy. Sources below were opened and checked at the locations listed unless explicitly marked unavailable. “Paragraph” references use the numbered paragraphs in legal filings; Federal Register references give printed pages and section headings. URLs are direct source links.

## Reporting takeaways and limits

- “Big Bend pause” covers separate project areas and has changed over time. The Sept. 30 coverage describes the pause on the Big Bend National Park work ending Sept. 30, with CBP considering changes before a final decision in the first week of October. It separately says ground-disturbing work on the 61-mile Big Bend 2 project in Presidio County may resume. Do not report a single region-wide restart or cancellation.
- Big Bend 2 is the roughly 61-mile line from Ruidosa toward Redford, mostly on private property according to the government as quoted in Sept. 30 local coverage. Big Bend 4 is a different CBP contract/project, with about 17 miles of noncontiguous vehicle barriers and a much larger mileage of possible patrol-road work and/or detection technology in and near the National Park. Contract-level totals do not establish that all “system attributes” will be built inside the park.
- Big Bend Ranch State Park is separate from Big Bend National Park. In March, Border Patrol told Marfa Public Radio that it had no plans for wall construction in the state park; the agency map then still showed a wall option, and the local account explicitly said whether that change was final remained unclear. CBP’s August statement said it would not build a 30-foot wall or stadium lighting in either park. The Sept. 30 filings as described in press reports concern the national-park project and the Presidio County line, not a newly announced state-park alignment.
- The June 9 Federal Register notice gives DHS’s legal rationale and its claimed enforcement basis. It is an agency determination, not an independent finding that Big Bend has “high illegal entry,” and it is not a court ruling that the waivers are lawful. The People of La Junta complaint states plaintiffs’ allegations and requested relief; neither allegation nor complaint language establishes environmental damage as a completed fact.
- The accessible primary records establish the waiver’s scope, project concepts and parties’ legal positions. They do not establish a finalized park design, exact alignment, a final scope reduction, environmental impact findings, or a final decision on Oct. 1–7.

## Sources opened and checked

### 1. DHS amended Big Bend waiver, Federal Register, June 9, 2026 (primary)

URL: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-06-09/pdf/2026-11473.pdf

Location: printed Federal Register pp. 34831–34833; “Determination and Waiver,” section 1 and section 2, especially pp. 34832–34833. The preamble at printed p. 34832 (PDF page 2) describes IIRIRA §102(a)–(c). Section 1 says DHS considers the Big Bend Sector an area of high illegal entry and cites FY2021–FY2025 apprehensions and seizures. Section 2 gives the corrected project-area endpoints and enumerates covered work: access, staging, earthwork, excavation, fill, barriers, roads, drainage, erosion controls, lighting, cameras and sensors. The last portion of the notice lists the statutes waived and says previous waivers remain in force.

Establishes: what the agency formally determined and waived; its asserted border-security rationale; covered activity and the corrected corridor description. The notice itself says the May 15 project-area description was incorrect and this June 9 republication corrects it (p. 34832, summary). The waiver is effective June 9.

Limits: this is the government’s legal determination and factual assertion, not an adjudicated finding on border activity, ecological effects, or legality. It defines a waiver area, not a construction alignment or parcel-level footprint. Do not equate the entire broad waiver corridor with the 61-mile Big Bend 2 or 17-mile Big Bend 4 contract.

### 2. Plaintiffs’ original complaint, *People of La Junta for Preservation et al. v. DHS et al.*, filed Aug. 14, 2026 (primary court record; plaintiffs’ account)

URL: https://www.texastribune.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/People-of-La-Junta-BigBend-suit.pdf

Location: PDF pp. 2–4, ¶¶7–12; PDF pp. 13–14, ¶¶49–56; PDF pp. 20–22, ¶¶82–87. ¶¶7–8 frame plaintiffs’ narrower theory: the complaint contests permanent development in a national park under the NPS statute, not the importance of border security in general. ¶¶49–56 summarize the waiver and its endpoints, and distinguish legal authority to waive requirements from whether a project is otherwise authorized. ¶¶82–87 set out plaintiffs’ claims about excavation, roads, lighting, archaeological context, habitat, scenery and recreation.

Establishes: what the People of La Junta, Far Flung Outdoor Center and individual plaintiffs allege and the legal question they ask the court to decide. It gives a useful statement of the advocates’ strongest documented case: Congress assigned administration, protection and development of Big Bend National Park to Interior/NPS, while the plaintiffs say the waiver did not remove that statutory protection (pp. 2–3, ¶¶7–8).

Limits: allegations and requested relief are not judicial findings. Environmental consequences are described as predicted harms, not measured impacts. For the waiver’s exact terms and the government’s own rationale, cite the Federal Register notice instead.

### 3. *Friends of the Ruidosa Church v. Mullin*, complaint, filed Apr. 16, 2026 (primary court record; separate case)

URL: https://clearinghouse-umich-production.s3.amazonaws.com/media/doc/170086.pdf

Location: PDF pp. 2–4, ¶¶3–11; pp. 20–25, ¶¶54–57; pp. 35–36, ¶94. The pleading describes the broader “Big Bend Wall Project” as spanning over 250 miles and says final construction plans were not publicly available and CBP had revised plans multiple times (pp. 23–24, ¶56 and n.6). It separately presents plaintiffs’ environmental and cultural-resource claims (pp. 24–25, ¶57).

Establishes: an earlier, distinct lawsuit challenging broad statutory waivers, plaintiffs’ pleaded theory and the state of information available to those plaintiffs in April. It documents why project labels and proposed infrastructure varied.

Limits: it predates the June 9 corrected waiver, August contracts and Sept. 29 filings. Its projected footprint and damage claims should not be treated as current final plans or court findings.

### 4. *Presidio Municipal Development District v. DHS*, memorandum opinion, D.D.C., Aug. 2, 2026 (primary court ruling)

URL: https://docs.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/district-of-columbia/dcdce/1:2026cv02146/293621/20

Location: PDF pp. 17–19, especially p. 18 (court text lines 136–141); opinion section “Likelihood of Success on the Merits.” The court says DHS’s July 2 waiver did not eliminate subject-matter jurisdiction over PMDD’s pre-waiver claims, but held that the waiver then precluded the plaintiff’s APA and ultra vires claims on the merits while it remained in effect. The opinion also recites the plaintiff’s allegations about contracts, contractor mobilization, land access and clearing (p. 18), and the parties’ dispute over the Rivers and Harbors Act (pp. 18–19).

Establishes: procedural posture and what the court actually decided in the PMDD case as of Aug. 2. The ruling distinguishes jurisdiction over pre-waiver actions from success on claims after the waiver.

Limits: this is a ruling in the PMDD/D.D.C. matter, not the People of La Junta/W.D. Tex. case; it is not a final judgment on every challenge to the project, nor does it establish the national-park pause or final project scope.

## Filing access gap and points to resolve

The Sept. 29 filing is the essential primary source for the article’s peg. I could not retrieve its text directly. The docket is *The People of La Junta for Preservation et al. v. DHS et al.*, No. 5:26-cv-05245 (W.D. Tex., San Antonio Division). The public docket search result identified Sept. 29 “Status Report” (docket entry 20) and Sept. 28 “Miscellaneous Relief” (entry 19), but the linked public docket page did not expose their document text. The direct CourtListener docket returned 403; the local Big Bend Reporter page timed out in the browser reader. AP and Houston Chronicle independently report the court filing’s central wording, but the exact document title, paragraph/page, full set of commitments, and whether the entry 20 status report is one of AP’s “two filings” remain unverified. Obtain and open entries 19 and 20 before quoting or writing a source note for the Sept. 30 status.

Specific follow-ups for the writer/editor:

1. Compare the Sept. 29 status report and related filing in case 5:26-cv-05245 with any same-day filings in the separate Conserve Big Bend case. Attribute each commitment to its filing and case.
2. Reconcile local “west of the park” with AP “north of the park” using the actual Big Bend 2 line. Do not use a map until the alignment itself is obtained.
3. Treat the March Big Bend Ranch State Park withdrawal as a reported statement, not a formal final park-wide redesign. Check the current CBP project map or a dated agency plan before describing the state park’s current status.
4. Avoid saying the government “paused all construction” across Big Bend. The filings and coverage distinguish the park’s Big Bend 4 work from ground-disturbing activity on the Big Bend 2 private-land project, and at least one other Big Bend project had already begun construction.
5. Keep impact claims in their proper evidentiary category: the plaintiffs’ forecasts are allegations; the waiver is DHS’s asserted rationale; no environmental-impact conclusion is established by the sources listed here.
