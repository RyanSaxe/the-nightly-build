# Voice guide: Big Bend border pause

Write a concise reported explainer for readers comfortable with technical detail but new to this dispute. Sound calm, specific, and sure of what the records establish. The story’s organizing idea is the geography and status of separate project lines, not a generalized argument for or against border barriers.

## Make the boundaries legible

- Name the place every time status changes: Big Bend National Park, Big Bend Ranch State Park, private land, or the broader 61-mile project. Do not let “Big Bend” stand in for all four.
- Explain the project/activity and its review status together. Distinguish ground disturbance, planning, construction, and agency review; “the project” and “work” are too broad where the record separates them.
- Use a compact comparison table only if sourced rows can accurately map area, activity, and status. Do not draw or imply boundaries that the records do not show. Otherwise, explain the distinctions in prose.
- Introduce waivers and the pending case only where they clarify why an activity is allowed, challenged, or awaiting a decision. Keep allegations, agency actions, court orders, and final rulings distinct.

## Report the filing precisely

- Attribute the September 30 filing’s statements to the government. A contemplated narrower approach inside the national park is under consideration; it is not a final redesign or completed change.
- Say separately what the filing says may proceed elsewhere in Presidio County, what it commits the government to do, and what it leaves open. Use verbs that match the document: proposes, represents, intends, agrees, orders, or rules.
- Do not convert advocacy descriptions, project-wide totals, or proposed routes into confirmed construction locations. Tie every distance, acreage, and place name to the source and the scope it actually measures.

## Handle disagreement and uncertainty

- Give advocates’ strongest documented claim in their own terms, then give the government’s stated rationale with the same precision. Attribute each view to a named party or document; avoid “critics,” “officials,” or “experts” without identification.
- Separate sourced fact from inference and judgment. Explain the evidence connecting them. If a record does not settle a point, name the missing measurement, decision, or document; do not use blanket caveats.
- Make no environmental-impact finding beyond the underlying assessment or record. Distinguish predicted effects, observed effects, disputed effects, and effects not assessed.
- When sources conflict, report who says what and whether the difference concerns geography, dates, legal status, or interpretation. Do not average incompatible accounts into a single claim.

## Keep the prose direct

Define specialized terms at first use, then use one consistent name for each place and project line. Prefer short declarative sentences and concrete verbs. Cite disputed or consequential claims to the opened primary record where possible, with precise paragraph, page, or section locations; use independent reporting for context and corroboration. End on the filing’s concrete scope and present status, not a broad lesson or a claim about what the case will ultimately decide.
