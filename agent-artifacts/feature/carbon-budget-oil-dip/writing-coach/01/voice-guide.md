# Voice guide: feature/carbon-budget-oil-dip (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a five-minute data feature for readers who build ML systems and read a confidence interval without being told what one is. The register is a working scientist explaining a figure to a colleague over their shoulder: direct, numerate, first-person-plural at most, no wonder and no alarm. Hausfather's methane piece shows the closest model. He names a line by its color, says what it does over time, and gives the physical reason in the same paragraph. Each visual section here can be written that way: what the axis or bar is, which mark to look at, what it does, and why.

Ritchie's beef arithmetic shows how to handle a small number against a large one. She does the division where the reader can see it and then says in five words what the result means. Where the piece compares a fuel's or a country's change against the total, or a projected change against its own uncertainty range, the same open working may be what the material calls for, so a reader can check the sign question themselves.

Goh's momentum essay shows that a technical reader can be addressed with some personality. He gives the familiar explanation in its own terms, says it is not wrong, and then says what it fails to explain. If the piece meets a common reading of the release (a "first decline" headline, say), it can be stated fairly that way before the figures test it. Goh also lets one sentence carry a feeling ("infuriatingly small") among precise ones; a dry dataset can take one such word where it is earned.

Use the budget's own terms exactly and keep them fixed: fossil CO2, land-use change emissions, projected change, uncertainty range, remaining carbon budget, Gt CO2. Hausfather's "not necessarily physically meaningful" passage shows the level of plainness for a caveat about what a quantity can and cannot tell you; say such a thing once, where it applies, and move on. The brief sets the length: short prose per visual, so a section's text reads as instructions for its chart followed by what follows from it, not as an essay with a chart attached.

## Gabriel Goh, "Why Momentum Really Works"

Source: https://distill.pub/2017/momentum/

> "This standard story isn’t wrong, but it fails to explain many important behaviors of momentum. In fact, momentum can be understood far more precisely if we study it on the right model. One nice model is the convex quadratic. This model is rich enough to reproduce momentum’s local dynamics in real problems, and yet simple enough to be understood in closed form."

Checked: https://distill.pub/2017/momentum/ (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-17
He grants the popular explanation before replacing it, and gives the reason for his choice of model in one sentence with two clauses that pull against each other. The person is visible in "One nice model", a casual phrase set among precise ones.

> "But the exponential decrease, though appealing in theory, can often be infuriatingly small. Things often begin quite well — with an impressive, almost immediate decrease in the loss. But as the iterations progress, things start to slow down. You start to get a nagging feeling you’re not making as much progress as you should be."

Checked: https://distill.pub/2017/momentum/ (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-17
He describes a loss curve as a practitioner experiences it, in the order it happens. "Infuriatingly" and "nagging feeling" show someone who has watched these runs, and the claims stay checkable.

> "For most step-sizes, the eigenvectors with largest eigenvalues converge the fastest. This triggers an explosion of progress in the first few iterations, before things slow down as the smaller eigenvectors’ struggles are revealed."

Checked: https://distill.pub/2017/momentum/ (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-17
Two sentences decompose an aggregate curve into the components that produce it, which is the reading his accompanying figure asks for. He keeps the field's exact vocabulary (step-sizes, eigenvalues) and lets one light word, "struggles", attach to it.

## Zeke Hausfather, "Using a 20-year period for comparing methane to CO2 is a terrible idea"

Source: https://www.theclimatebrink.com/p/using-a-20-year-period-for-comparing

> "If we cut the same amount of “CO2e emissions” in the form of methane using a 100-year period for GWP calculations, we get the red line. We get much more short-term cooling, reflecting the more powerful heat-trapping effects of methane. But the climate effects fade away over time, and after 100 years or so only a fraction of the cooling remains."

Checked: https://www.theclimatebrink.com/p/using-a-20-year-period-for-comparing (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-17
He states the scenario, points at the line by color, describes its shape over time, and gives the physical cause. The scare quotes around "CO2e emissions" show a scientist who distrusts the unit he is using.

> "In the figure below, values above zero reflect periods when we get more cooling from methane reductions than from CO2, while values below zero show periods where CO2 reductions give us a bigger climate benefit. Here the GWP100 metric provides at least some period of climate benefits, while GWP20 only gives a very short term boost."

Checked: https://www.theclimatebrink.com/p/using-a-20-year-period-for-comparing (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-17
The first sentence tells the reader what the sign of the y-axis means before any result; the second gives the result. His view comes through in "at least" and "only", not in adjectives.

> "However, while convenient, they are not necessarily physically meaningful. I cannot tell you how much warmer the world will be in 20, 100, or 500 years based on a certain amount of CO2e."

Checked: https://www.theclimatebrink.com/p/using-a-20-year-period-for-comparing (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-17
A limit of the metric is stated as a concrete thing he, an expert, cannot compute. The first person makes the caveat specific instead of hedged.

## Hannah Ritchie, "You want to reduce the carbon footprint of your food? Focus on what you eat, not whether your food is local"

Source: https://ourworldindata.org/food-choice-vs-eating-local

> "The visualization shows GHG emissions from 29 food products — from beef at the top to nuts at the bottom. For each product, you can see from which stage in the supply chain its emissions originate. This extends from land use changes on the left to transport and packaging on the right."

Checked: https://ourworldindata.org/food-choice-vs-eating-local (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-17
Three plain sentences orient the reader to a stacked bar chart: rows, then segments, then the direction of the axis. Nothing is interpreted yet.

> "Transport is a small contributor to emissions. For most food products, it accounts for less than 10%, and it’s much smaller for the largest GHG emitters. In beef from beef herds, it’s 0.5%."

Checked: https://ourworldindata.org/food-choice-vs-eating-local (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-17
A claim, then the bound, then one exact instance. Each sentence narrows the one before it.

> "This is equivalent to 0.35% of the total footprint of 60 kilograms of CO2 eq per kilogram of beef. If you buy from your local farmer — let’s assume you walk there and have zero transport emissions — your beef footprint is 59.8 kilograms CO2 eq per kilogram [we calculate this as 60kg - 0.2kg]. It makes almost no difference."

Checked: https://ourworldindata.org/food-choice-vs-eating-local (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-17 (source renders "CO 2" with a subscript)
She shows the subtraction in brackets so it can be checked, and the dry aside about walking to the neighbor's farm is where the person shows. The short last sentence gives the verdict after the arithmetic.
