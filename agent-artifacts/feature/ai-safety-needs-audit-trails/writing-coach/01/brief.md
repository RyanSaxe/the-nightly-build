# Writing-coach brief

## Audience

- Write for a widely read audience with strong mathematics and computer-science knowledge.
- Assume readers can follow model versions, deployment contexts, evaluation results, and institutional reporting, but do not assume that they know the U.S.–China proposal, the cited lab policies, or the term recursive self-improvement.
- Supply only the context needed to distinguish incident disclosure, independent verification, and enforcement. Do not add a general primer on AI risk or international relations.
- Keep the register concise, concrete, and analytical. Prefer named actors, dates, fields, thresholds, and observable effects over abstract governance language.

## Central analytical claim

- Build the piece around the claim that cross-organization incident reporting becomes useful for comparison only when records expose comparable facts, evidence, and follow-up, while the current public proposals and reports provide uneven disclosure without a common trigger, severity rule, provenance, or verification procedure.
- Make the original work the derivation of a minimum incident-record schema from the cited material. Treat the schema as this article's analytical proposal, not as an existing standard and not as a recommendation already adopted by any source.
- Keep the claim narrower than a prediction about frontier-model danger. Establish what records can let an outside reader determine, and state what the present evidence cannot establish.

## Section purposes

### Orientation

- Introduce the reporting problem through the U.S. notification proposal, OpenAI's standards position, and Anthropic's public reporting.
- Define incident reporting and recursive self-improvement at first use.
- Establish the distinction between a promise to disclose, a method for verifying a disclosure, and a consequence for failing to disclose.

### Section 1: the proposal's missing protocol

- Report what Bessent's proposal is actually described as saying, without expanding a reported proposal into an operating agreement.
- Test the proposal against four protocol questions: what triggers a report, who receives it, what severity threshold applies, and how anyone verifies it.
- Keep reported facts, the proposal's stated purpose, and the article's deductions visibly separate.

### Section 2: what the labs' publications reveal

- Compare OpenAI's policy commitments and call for mandatory requirements, independent assessment infrastructure, auditor standards, and industry standards with Anthropic's dated, categorized misuse account.
- Explain what a lab's self-report can establish about its own stated categories, actions, and disruptions, and what it cannot establish about completeness, intent, external impact, or comparability with another lab's account.
- Preserve Anthropic's qualification that recursive self-improvement is not currently present and is not inevitable.
- State the strongest case for voluntary coordination before identifying its limits. Do not treat the existence of limits as proof that the reports have no value.

### Section 3: the proposed incident record

- Derive and explain the minimum fields: model and version, deployment context, triggering behavior, observable impact, containment action, independent confirmation, and follow-up.
- Show the analytical job of each field and the comparison failure that occurs when it is absent.
- Use one compact table or equivalent furniture only if it clarifies the field-to-purpose mapping. Label it as a proposal from the article, not an adopted standard.
- Keep every schema choice attributable to the article's reasoning from the public material; do not attribute the full schema to OpenAI, Anthropic, Bessent, AP, or Reuters.

### Section 4: the institutional test

- Move from the record's fields to the institutional requirements for a U.S.–China or other cross-organization mechanism: shared definitions, trigger and severity rules, recipients, evidence and verification, handling of sensitive information, and consequences or follow-up.
- Explain why common measurements and comparable fields matter more than a broad promise of transparency.
- Distinguish disclosure, verification, and enforcement throughout; none substitutes for the others.

## Fair treatment of competing views

- Give voluntary industry coordination its strongest defensible case: the organizations closest to the systems can report quickly, shared practice can develop before formal rules, and a centralized mandatory process may create security, confidentiality, or diplomatic costs. Present these as arguments that require named support or clear analytical labeling, not as anonymous consensus.
- Treat OpenAI's call for mandatory requirements and common standards as a serious response to the limits of self-reporting; do not reduce it to corporate self-interest without evidence.
- Treat Anthropic's report as useful evidence of what a dated, categorized account can disclose while testing its limits. Do not claim that it independently verifies every actor's intent or every reported disruption.
- Treat the diplomatic proposal as an incomplete mechanism because its public description leaves key operating details unspecified, not because international coordination is inherently futile.
- State the strongest version of each view before evaluating it. Identify whether a sentence reports a source, draws an inference, or proposes a field or test of this article's own.

## Quote limits

- Use no more than one short direct quotation in the article, and keep it under 25 words.
- Prefer paraphrase for the policy, report, and diplomatic material. Do not build the argument around a quotable line or use a quotation as a substitute for explaining the mechanism.
- Copy any quotation only after opening the source and finding the exact supporting passage; preserve its wording and punctuation, mark omissions, and cite the precise section, paragraph, or page.
- Do not quote a source in a way that makes a proposal sound adopted, a self-report sound independently verified, or a qualification sound like a prediction.

## Source boundaries

- Use the primary sources first: OpenAI's AI Policy Window for mandatory national requirements, independent safety-assessment infrastructure, auditor standards, and industry standards; Anthropic's recursive-self-improvement page for the definition and its qualification; and Anthropic's September 2026 threat report for the reporting window, seven harm areas, model families, disruption language, and stated limits.
- Use AP for the reported Bessent notification-mechanism proposal and its disclosed purpose. Use Reuters for the independently reported account of OpenAI's call for common measurements and incident-reporting protocols and the international context.
- Use Anthropic's incident-investigation material only if a precise distinction between a published incident review and a generalized safety claim is needed.
- Open every source and find the supporting passage before citing it. Cite the document that owns a claim before an article that reports it, and record the precise section, paragraph, or page so the citation lands on the evidence rather than a homepage.
- Do not treat a proposal as a protocol, a policy commitment as proof of implementation, a lab's report as independent verification, or a source's qualification as evidence of inevitability.
- Do not attribute the proposed record schema or its success criterion to any source. Do not use vague attributions, unsupported social commentary, marketing copy, or secondary summaries where the named primary source is available.
- Define technical and institutional terms on first use, keep source dates and reporting windows exact, and cut any claim that cannot be supported or qualified plainly.

## Ending requirement

- End with the institutional test developed by the article, not with a reading list, a pointer, a generic moral, a broad prediction, or a claim that safety matters.
- The final passage must leave the reader with a specific, checkable success criterion: an outside reader should be able to reconstruct what happened from one record and compare it with another.
- Do not introduce a new source, field, or unresolved prediction in the ending. Make the conclusion follow from the evidence and the proposed record rather than from a rhetorical flourish.

## Slop watchlist

- Generic openings about AI safety, rapid change, trust, or the future; begin with the named reporting problem and its concrete missing information.
- Abstract governance nouns such as transparency, accountability, coordination, or safety when the sentence does not name a field, actor, evidence, threshold, or consequence.
- Vague attribution, including unnamed experts, observers, stakeholders, or “the industry.” Name the source or remove the claim.
- Conflating disclosure, verification, and enforcement, especially by treating a published account as independently confirmed.
- Turning Bessent's reported proposal into an agreement, operating mechanism, or settled diplomatic position.
- Treating recursive self-improvement as imminent, inevitable, or established; preserve Anthropic's explicit qualification.
- Treating company policy language as implementation evidence, or describing a report as complete without evidence about its coverage.
- Empty contrasts, antithesis, tricolons, inflated copulas, decorative metaphors, and rhetorical questions that do not perform the article's own analysis.
- Formulaic section openers, deks, and closers; self-reference to the article or newsroom; and any sentence whose subject could be replaced with a placeholder without changing its meaning.
- Repeating the proposed fields as a cadence instead of explaining why each field changes what can be compared.
- Citation padding, unsupported precision, claims of broad consensus, dramatic predictions, and a final sentence that merely announces significance.
