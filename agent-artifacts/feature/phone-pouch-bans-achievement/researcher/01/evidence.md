# Evidence: feature/phone-pouch-bans-achievement (researcher 01)

The Kane–Staiger report itself could not be opened. Every cepr.harvard.edu address (the report page, the CEPR "cellphones" page, the CEPR press release, the SDP news page) returns an Akamai "Access Denied" 403 to both WebFetch and curl. A retry with browser headers was refused by this session's permission system, and no copy turned up on NBER, SSRN, NWEA, Dartmouth or in any news story's links. The orchestrator decided the article goes ahead on the release plus the primary text that can be reached. So the paper's design, comparison group, pre-trend evidence, standard errors, its weeks-of-learning conversion and any sentence of its own on the "half" comparison are **unestablished**. What the record does establish is narrower but still useful. First, the release's own figures: a 0.01 SD/yr pouch effect against a decline of "0.01 to 0.02 SD per year". The effect is half the decline only at the top of that range, and the whole decline at the bottom. Second, the release's "one grade equivalent since 2013" for 8th graders includes the pandemic. In NAEP grade 8 math, 3 of the roughly 11 points lost between 2013 and 2024 came before 2019, and 8 came in 2019–22. Third, the same authors' May 2026 Education Scorecard says in print that there is "still no direct evidence" that social media caused the post-2013 decline, and that in-class distraction is likely not the only mechanism. Fourth, the strongest US prior study (Allcott, Baron, Dee, Duckworth, Gentzkow and Jacob, NBER w35132, now in the QJE) uses Yondr's own adoption records for about 4,600 schools. It rules out average test-score gains above about 0.007 student-level SD over three years, which is below the new study's 0.01 SD per year. On this evidence the commissioned angle holds and, if anything, gets stronger: the "half" figure is an arithmetic juxtaposition the release presents as an implication. The record is thin on the new study's internals, and the writer must not describe its design beyond what the release, Kane's interview and NPR state.

## Contradictions

- **New study vs Allcott et al. on the size of the pouch effect.** The release gives +0.01 SD per year in achievement growth (student-level MAP Growth, grades 3–10). Allcott et al. give a pooled ATT of -0.006 (SE 0.010) school SD and rule out gains above about 0.007 student SD averaged over three post-adoption years, with later event-study coefficients slightly negative. Both use Yondr adoption data. Tests, grades, units (growth vs level, student vs school SD) and samples differ, so the gap may partly be measurement. Neither text explains it, and the new report's own reconciliation, if any, is unreachable. Dee calls the early results "largely disappointing"; Jacob calls the new one "encouraging".
- **Middle schools.** The release says effects were consistent across elementary, middle and high schools. Allcott et al. find middle schools negative (-0.025, SE 0.012, school SD) and high schools positive.
- **Year-by-year.** NPR has gains continuing "up to three years". Kane in the Gazette says first-year and later-year effects are not statistically different and few schools are in year 3 or 4. Not strictly contradictory, but the "accumulates" claim is a projection, as Dee says.
- **What "half" refers to.** In the release body, the effect is half of the annual decline (rates). In the release headline, in-school phone use "may account for half of the historical loss" (a causal share of the total). K-12 Dive calls it half of the "pre-pandemic annual learning loss". AP says half of the annual decline "since smartphones became mainstream".
- **Arithmetic inside the release.** 0.01 SD is "1–2 weeks" when it is the effect, but the decline of "0.01 to 0.02 SD" is "2–3 weeks". The release's own figures give a ratio of effect to decline between 1/2 (0.01/0.02) and 1/1 (0.01/0.01), and 2/3 at the midpoint. "About half" uses the top of the decline range.
- **Pandemic inside "since 2013".** The release dates the decline 2013–2024 and calls it "gradual". NAEP grade 8 math fell 285 → 282 from 2013 to 2019 (3 points), then 8 points in 2019–22, then was flat to 2024. Grade 8 reading fell 268 → 263 from 2013 to 2019 (5 points), then 3 + 2 points from 2019 to 2024. Most of the math loss since 2013 is the pandemic drop.
- **Same authors, May vs November.** In May 2026 Kane and Staiger wrote that there is "still no direct evidence" that social media caused the post-2013 decline, and that in-class distraction is probably not the whole mechanism. They also summarized Allcott et al. as "no impact on student achievement". In November the release headline says the new research "implies" in-school phone use "may account for half". The May report also states the logic linking the two ("if achievement were to rise sharply following a cell phone ban, it would imply..."). The November effect is small, not sharp: AP's report calls the gains "modest", and Kane calls it "a relatively small effect" (Gazette).
- **Sample counts.** The release gives 4,600 schools in the MAP Growth sample. NPR gives 844 schools using both Yondr and NWEA tests. Allcott et al.'s Yondr file covers 4,608 pouch-buying schools, and AP notes both studies cite 4,600. The writer should not call the new study's 4,600 "pouch schools".
- **Grades.** The release says grades 3–10, AP says 4–10.
- **Cost.** NPR gives about $25 per student. The Gazette interviewer gives about $425 a classroom (Kane agrees, saying it is "about a fifth the cost of a teacher's salary, on a per-student basis"). These are consistent at about 17 students per classroom, but the units differ.
- **England.** Beland & Murphy (school bans, DiD) find +6.41% SD, concentrated among low achievers. Goodyear et al. (cross-sectional, 30 schools) find no attainment association. Kane says low scorers did not gain more in the new study, which differs from Beland & Murphy.

## Limits

- The Kane–Staiger report was not read (Akamai 403 on all cepr.harvard.edu URLs, no mirror found). Unestablished: the design (event study vs DiD, estimator), the comparison group (NPR says "similar schools that did not"; the release describes only a change in growth after adoption), pouch-school pre-trend evidence, standard errors and CIs on 0.01, whether "achievement growth" is fall-to-spring or year-to-year, the SD scale (student-level MAP norms?), how weeks of learning were converted, and any sentence by the authors in the report about extrapolating to "half".
- The source of the 0.01–0.02 SD/yr decline figure is unestablished. The release does not name NAEP, and Kane's "two-hundredths" in the Gazette has no stated basis. Whether it is computed on the same scale as the pouch effect is unknown.
- The writer cannot say the study used an event study, a DiD, matched comparison schools or tested pre-trends for pouch schools. The writer can say the release cautions that the state-ban estimates may be confounded by pre-existing trends.
- Which state is excluded from "49 states and DC", and the AP/release grade discrepancy (4–10 vs 3–10), are unresolved.
- NAEP 2022 and 2024 grade 8 scale scores were not read directly. Only the changes from the NAGB release were read, and the implied about 274 (math) and about 258 (reading) are inferences. Exact NAEP SDs were not obtained.
- Abrahamsson (Norway): only the abstract was read. The full paper on openaccess.nhh.no is blocked in this session's network. No effect sizes are verified.
- Kessel, Hardardottir & Tyrefors 2020 (Sweden) and Beneito & Vicente-Chirivella 2022 (Spain) were not read. They are known only as summarized in Allcott et al. (pp.4–5) and the Scorecard (p.14). No Netherlands evaluation was found.
- Published responses: Dee and Jacob responded in NPR and AP quotes only. No written critique (blog, paper, op-ed) of the November release by a named researcher was found as of 18 Nov 2026.
