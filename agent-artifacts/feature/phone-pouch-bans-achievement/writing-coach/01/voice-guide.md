# Voice guide: feature/phone-pouch-bans-achievement (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a five-minute feature that reads one new working paper closely and says what its headline comparison rests on. The register is that of a methods-literate colleague walking a peer through a paper they have both just opened: plain, direct, specific about the design, and willing to say what the reporting supports. The reader knows regression, fixed effects, event studies and pre-trends. Do not explain those. The reader does not know MAP Growth, RIT scores, or why an education economist converts a standard deviation into "weeks of learning", and each of those wants defining at first use, once, in the paper's own terms.

Julia Rohrer's posts show the main move this piece may need: naming exactly which comparison a claim makes and which assumption would have to hold for it to mean what it is said to mean. She uses the field's vocabulary at full strength (identification strategy, time-invariant confounding, fixed effects) because her reader shares it, and she is frank when the honest answer is that a design cannot settle the question. Where the paper's design does settle something, say that just as plainly; Dynomight's breastfeeding piece is as willing to say "the skeptics have a point" as to say a non-significant result is still a best guess above zero.

Effect sizes of 0.01 SD a year mean nothing to this reader until they are put beside something they already hold. Dynomight's "Is 3.1 points small?" passage turns an abstract figure into a rank in a class of 25 and then gives his own stake in it. Where the material calls for it, this article can do the same with the paper's numbers: translate once, on one scale, and keep using that scale.

Scott Alexander's post shows how to be fair to a framing you doubt: he lays out what the phrase is meant to communicate before showing what it fails to carry, and then asks what the investigation would have to look like. The press release's "half of the historical loss" deserves that treatment: stated in its authors' words, then checked against what the paper measured. If the paper's text supports it more firmly than expected, the piece says so with the same directness.

Humor in all three writers is dry and comes from the subject's own nouns. A feature for this paper has room for little of it. Keep the voice of a person who has read the paper, not a performance of skepticism.

## Julia Rohrer, "Friends don't let friends run moderated cross-country regressions"

Source: https://www.the100.ci/2026/05/20/friends-dont-let-friends-run-moderated-cross-country-regressions/

> "In any case, a failure of imagination is not a very good identification strategy, even if some people like to pretend otherwise. If all else fails, just list all major confounders between X and Y that you can come up with and consider whether it's plausible that some country-level variable affects how strongly those confounders affect your X or Y. It usually is; there's no reason why "Z affects this effect of interest" should generally be more plausible than "Z affects this other effect which confounds the thing I'm interested in." The universe just isn't kind like that."

Checked: https://www.the100.ci/2026/05/20/friends-dont-let-friends-run-moderated-cross-country-regressions/ (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-18
She states a methodological objection as a concrete procedure the reader could carry out. The last sentence is her own exasperation, and it comes after the argument is complete, so it adds a person without carrying any of the reasoning.

> "You don't want your results to be badly confounded, so not controlling is not an option; once you do control, you may end up with the conclusion that you can't conclude anything. To be entirely clear, that would be the correct conclusion. It's just not a fun one."

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-18
The passage reaches a null conclusion and states it as a conclusion, not as a failure of the analysis. The short closing sentences are where she is visible: she knows the reader wanted a result and says so.

> "Alternatively, one may want to look at associations within country, over time. If gender equality impacts gender differences, then one would expect gender differences to (slowly) change as gender equality changes over time. Now this isn't foolproof either, but associations within countries are not confounded by time-invariant country-level confounding (that is, the stable stuff that makes countries different from each other in a consistent manner over time). So hey, that's one less class of confounders to worry about!"

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-18
She gives a design its due precisely: what it removes and what it leaves. The parenthesis translates the technical term into plain words without dropping the term.

## Dynomight, "Nursing doubts: Is breastfeeding good?"

Source: https://dynomight.net/breastfeeding/

> "These results seem more believable. None of the differences are significant, but a non-significant result doesn't mean the true magnitude is zero. If the true difference in full-scale IQ were 3.1 points as above, there would be no chance of significance with a sample of this size. It's uncertain, but given this data, the best guess is 3.1 points, not 0 points."

Checked: https://dynomight.net/breastfeeding/ (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-18
He separates the point estimate from the significance test in four short sentences and commits to a best guess. The plain declaratives show a writer who has done the arithmetic and expects the reader to follow it.

> "Is 3.1 points small? Well, a 100 IQ is higher than that of 50% of the population, while a 103.1 IQ is higher than 58%. Adding 3.1 IQ points to a kid ranked 13th in a 25-person class would push them up to around 11th. And, personally, if you were going to drop my IQ by 3.1 points, I would not be super stoked about it."

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-18
An abstract effect size becomes a percentile and then a rank in a class, two scales the reader already holds. The last sentence is the writer's own stake, stated flatly.

> "But I think the skeptics have a point. The evidence for breastfeeding is much shakier than people realize."

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-18
After arguing against the skeptics' strongest claim for most of the piece, he concedes the part they got right in two unhedged sentences. The person is visible in the "I think" and in changing his mind on the page.

## Scott Alexander, "The Phrase "No Evidence" Is A Red Flag For Bad Science Communication"

Source: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-phrase-no-evidence-is-a-red-flag

> "In an extremely nitpicky sense, these headlines are accurate. Officials were simply describing the then-current state of knowledge. In medicine, anecdotes or hunches aren't considered "real" evidence. So if there hasn't been a study showing something, then there's "no evidence"."

Checked: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-phrase-no-evidence-is-a-red-flag (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-18
Before criticizing a way of reporting science, he states the case for it as its users would. "Extremely nitpicky" is his voice, and it concedes the point without giving up the argument.

> "But I think the most virtuous way to write this is to actually investigate. If it's worth writing a story about why there's no evidence for something, probably it's because some people believe there is evidence. What evidence do they believe in? Why is it wrong? How do you know?"

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-18
The questions are the ones the piece itself goes on to answer, so they work as an outline of an investigation and not as rhetoric. The writer is visible in the moral language ("virtuous") applied to a reporting habit.
