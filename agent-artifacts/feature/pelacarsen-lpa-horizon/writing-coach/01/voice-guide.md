# Voice guide: feature/pelacarsen-lpa-horizon (01)

## How this piece should sound

The register is that of a numerate colleague walking another engineer through a result. The reader can do the arithmetic and wants to see it done. Gabriel Popkin and Niko McCarty both name the quantity and its unit as soon as it matters (a ratio "almost exactly 80%", a volume of "30 µm³", "0.01 seconds" against "six hours"), and Scott Alexander gives each trial's counts and the primary outcome in the sentence that describes it. For this piece that means every figure arrives with its unit and its source population, and mg/dL and nmol/L stay labelled as whatever the source printed. Where two numbers need comparing, a sentence says which is larger and by how much, and the figure carries the rest.

Treat the reader as someone who could check the work. Alexander describes a primary-outcome change, says what a preregistered outcome is for, and then says why this change does not defeat that purpose. He states the objection in terms its holders would accept before answering it. The genetic case for Lp(a) and the case that the trial tested too little have supporters, and each deserves its strongest statement from the documents before the piece weighs it. Where a document does not settle something, Alexander's habit of saying what he could and could not find, in the same flat tone as the rest, is better than a softening phrase.

Introduce a clinical term once, plainly, and then use it. Popkin opens by describing the textbook picture and then replacing it with the measured one, and McCarty defines diffusion in the sentence where it starts to matter. The readers here know statistics and machine learning but not cardiology, so "primary endpoint", "hazard ratio" and "Mendelian randomization" each need one clause the first time and nothing after. People appear when they said something specific: Popkin quotes Luxton's "I don't know how anything ever finds anything" at the point where the result becomes puzzling, and gives each speaker a full title. Use the named investigators, Novartis and outside cardiologists in that way, with their exact titles, and only for what they said.

The piece is short and built around figures, so prose should carry what a figure cannot: how to read the axis, what the comparison shows, what follows. McCarty's brief does this in two or three sentences beside each chart. An analogy is welcome where it makes a number scalable, as in McCarty's sperm scaled to a marble and an oocyte to a refrigerator. Nothing in the exemplars is there for atmosphere, and the piece should not have any either.

## Scott Alexander, "Ivermectin: Much More Than You Wanted To Know"

Source: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/ivermectin-much-more-than-you-wanted

> "Primary outcome was originally percent of patients whose symptoms worsened by two points, as rated on a complicated symptom scale when a researcher asked them over the phone. Halfway through the study, they realized nobody was worsening that much, so they changed the primary outcome to time until symptoms got better, as measured by the scale."

Checked: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/ivermectin-much-more-than-you-wanted, retrieved 2026-11-08
The two sentences give the original endpoint, the reason it failed and the replacement, with the measurement method named. The writer sounds like someone who read the methods section and is reporting what he found in it.

> "Also, it changed its primary outcome halfway through; isn’t that bad? I think maybe not; the reason we want a preregistered primary outcome is so that you don’t change halfway through to whatever outcome shows the results you want. The researchers in this study did a good job explaining why they changed their outcome, the change makes sense, and their original outcome would also have shown ivermectin not working (albeit less accurately and effectively)."

Checked: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/ivermectin-much-more-than-you-wanted, retrieved 2026-11-08
He asks the reader's objection, states what the rule is for, and checks whether this case defeats that purpose. The verdict comes with its reasons and one check against the other outcome.

> "GRIM - make sure means are possible given the number of numbers involved. For example, if a paper reports analyzing 10 patients and finding that 27% of them recovered, something has gone wrong. One possible thing that could have gone wrong is that the data are made up. Another possible thing is that they’re not giving the full story about how many patients dropped out when. But something is wrong."

Checked: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/ivermectin-much-more-than-you-wanted, retrieved 2026-11-08
A consistency check is explained by a worked case small enough to do in the head, and the possible causes are listed and then dropped. The register is plain and a little dry, and the reader is assumed to follow without being coaxed. His jokes and profanity elsewhere in the post do not belong in this genre.

## Niko McCarty, "What Limits a Cell's Size?"

Source: https://press.asimov.com/articles/cell-size

> "A human body is built from 30 trillion cells — excluding microbes — that each arise from a lone, fertilized egg. These cells come in a multiplicity of shapes and sizes, with internal volumes spanning five orders of magnitude. The smallest human cell, a sperm, fills a volume of just 30 µm³, whereas an oocyte boasts a volume of 4,000,000 µm³, making it the largest cell in the human body."

Checked: https://press.asimov.com/articles/cell-size, retrieved 2026-11-08
The opening gives a count, a range and both endpoints with units and names, in three sentences. A reader leaves knowing the scale before any argument starts.

> "A typical protein takes just 0.01 seconds to traverse a bacterium’s diameter (about 1 micrometer), but the same protein would take around four minutes to move one millimeter and more than six hours to move one centimeter. This is, in part, why cells are so tiny."

Checked: https://press.asimov.com/articles/cell-size, retrieved 2026-11-08
One figure is set against two longer distances, so the scaling is visible without a formula. The conclusion is hedged exactly once, with "in part", and the sentence stops.

> "Similarly, when thinking about biological numbers, it’s helpful to scale things up and recontextualize them. Imagine a sperm “blown up” to the size of a glass marble, about two cubic centimeters in volume. A single oocyte, scaled in the same way, would then occupy roughly the size of a refrigerator — more than 100,000-times larger."

Checked: https://press.asimov.com/articles/cell-size, retrieved 2026-11-08
A ratio too large to feel is turned into two objects the reader has handled, with the volume of the marble stated. He says in one clause why he is doing it ("it’s helpful"), and that is the only place the author shows. The move to take is scaling a number to something familiar; the "Imagine" opener is not part of it.

## Gabriel Popkin, "The Biophysical World Inside a Jam-Packed Cell"

Source: https://www.quantamagazine.org/the-biophysical-world-inside-a-jam-packed-cell-20260218/

> "It’s a familiar image, reprinted in countless biology textbooks: an illustration of a typical cell, halved like a grapefruit to reveal its innards. Strands of endoplasmic reticulum encircle a nucleus that floats in the center like a raft. RNA molecules wait patiently at ribosomes to deliver recipes for making proteins. A few vacuoles and Golgi bodies bob about. A mostly deserted cytosol offers a blank backdrop."

Checked: https://www.quantamagazine.org/the-biophysical-world-inside-a-jam-packed-cell-20260218/, retrieved 2026-11-08
He states the picture the reader already holds in concrete parts before saying what the measurements changed. The correction that follows lands because the old picture was drawn in detail first.

> "In the world of cells, the word “crowding” doesn’t have a clear physical definition. That’s why Simone Reber, a biochemist at the Max Planck Institute for Infection Biology, focuses on a simpler, older, and more precisely defined physical quantity: density, the mass of a cell’s contents divided by its volume."

Checked: https://www.quantamagazine.org/the-biophysical-world-inside-a-jam-packed-cell-20260218/, retrieved 2026-11-08
A vague term is replaced by a defined quantity, and the researcher who made the choice is named with her title and institute. The sentence shows that picking the unit was a decision someone made.

> "Once inside the cells, the particles hardly moved. The worm cytoplasm, measurements showed, was around 50 times more crowded with ribosomes than that of Holt’s cultured cells. At first, the graduate student conducting the experiments thought they had made a mistake."

The observation, the number and the people's first reaction are three short sentences in order. The surprise is reported as what the researchers did next, with no remark from the writer on how surprising it was.
