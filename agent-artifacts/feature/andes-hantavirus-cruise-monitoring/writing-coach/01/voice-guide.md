# Voice guide: feature/andes-hantavirus-cruise-monitoring (01)

## How this piece should sound

The register is a numerate colleague walking another numerate person through a result they would want to check. The readers have CS and mathematics degrees, so they do not need a binomial or a distribution explained from scratch, and they will notice if a number is handled loosely. They do not know the public-health vocabulary: contact, monitoring, incubation period, secondary attack rate, case fatality rate. Each of those arrives when the piece first needs it, defined in one plain sentence, and then stays the same word. Tom Chivers defines "case fatality rate" and "infection fatality rate" in two sentences with a worked 10,000-and-100 example, and then uses the terms without restating them. That is the level of definition to aim for.

Work a number through where the reader can follow it. Chivers divides 100 deaths by 10,000 cases and then by 100,000 infections, in the sentences themselves, so the reader sees why the denominator is the issue. For a feature built around visuals, the prose around each figure can do the same: say what the quantity is, give the arithmetic in words and digits, and say what the picture shows that the sentence cannot. Where the paper computes a bound of its own, as the commission asks, the arithmetic can sit in the prose in this way and carry the label of whose arithmetic it is.

A person should be visible, and the person can be a modest one. Adam Kucharski opens by describing his own small mistake and then turns his curiosity on it. Chivers calls his own model "very simple, stupid" and says what it is not for. Simon Raper lets a dry joke carry a statistical point. In a piece with no narrator, this shows up as an author who says what was checked and what was not, states plainly what nobody knows, and is allowed one dry sentence where the material has something funny in it. It does not need first-person confession. The paper is the narrator, so "we" and "I" are used sparingly if at all, and the writer should avoid copying any one of these three personas.

Short, flat sentences can carry the findings. Kucharski's "Somewhat embarrassed, I posted a correction, and interest soon faded." and his three short questions show a plain claim followed by its support. Longer sentences suit the places where a qualification attaches to a figure, as in Chivers's list of what makes the fatality question hard, and the qualification then goes next to the figure.

Use the exact words of the field. "Incubation period", "serial interval", "secondary attack rate", "denominator", "exact binomial" and "rule of three" are how a practitioner says it, and numerate readers will want them. Kucharski writes "outbreak curve" and names its stages; he does not call it a "trend line". Quote the sources' own category names where the piece reports them, such as CDC's risk categories.

Where an argument turns on what an absence shows, a small concrete case does the teaching. Raper reduces "no evidence" to a coin and ten flips. The writer may find a comparably small case for what a monitored group with no infections can and cannot rule out, and may find the material does not need one.

Two directions come from the brief and not from the exemplars. The piece is five minutes and each section is built around a visual, so the prose is short, says how to read the figure, and stops when the figure's point is made. And the headline and dek follow the commission's pattern notes, not these passages.

## Adam Kucharski, "The science of why things spread" (extract from The Rules of Contagion)

Source: https://wellcomecollection.org/articles/Xl0YSxIAACEAQiBn

> "Somewhat embarrassed, I posted a correction, and interest soon faded. But even in that short space of time, almost 50,000 people had seen my tweet. Given that my job involves analysing disease outbreaks, I was curious about what had just happened. Why did my tweet spread so quickly at first? Did that correction really slow it down? What if people had taken longer to spot the mistake?"

Checked: https://wellcomecollection.org/articles/Xl0YSxIAACEAQiBn, retrieved 2026-11-10
An epidemiologist makes himself the first data point, admits the error in one clause, and then turns it into three questions a model could answer. The person is visible in "somewhat embarrassed" and in the plain statement of what his job is. The questions are short and each is specific enough to be tested.

> "When disease researchers hear about a new threat, one of the first things we do is to draw what we call an outbreak curve – a graph showing how many cases have appeared over time. Although the shape can vary a lot, it will typically include four main stages: the spark, growth, peak, and decline."

Checked: https://wellcomecollection.org/articles/Xl0YSxIAACEAQiBn, retrieved 2026-11-10
This introduces a graph by saying what the practitioners do first and defines the term in a dash clause. The vocabulary is the field's own, "outbreak curve", and the four stages are named in one sentence so the reader has labels for what follows.

> "Unfortunately it’s difficult to say, because previous flu pandemics were all slightly different. There were different strains of the virus, and outbreaks hit some places harder than others. In fact, there’s a saying in my field: “if you’ve seen one pandemic, you’ve seen… one pandemic.”"

Checked: https://wellcomecollection.org/articles/Xl0YSxIAACEAQiBn, retrieved 2026-11-10
The limit on what can be known is stated at the start of the paragraph, the reason is two short sentences, and the field's own saying closes it. The writer sounds like a member of a profession quoting its in-joke, which gives the caveat a voice.

## Tom Chivers, "How likely are you to die of coronavirus?"

Source: https://unherd.com/2020/04/how-likely-are-you-to-die-of-coronavirus/

> "It is probably the most basic question you can ask about a disease, and yet it’s bloody hard to answer. It changes between places and over time, and between different groups; it depends on a variety of hard-to-measure factors, and the answer you get itself directly affects our understanding of several other numbers, all of which are vital to any model of the disease."

Checked: https://unherd.com/2020/04/how-likely-are-you-to-die-of-coronavirus/, retrieved 2026-11-10
The difficulty of the question is listed in one sentence with its parts separated by commas and semicolons, and one informal phrase, "bloody hard", marks where the writer is. The terms the next paragraphs define are not yet used, so the reader meets the problem before the vocabulary.

> "So if your country has tested absolutely everyone and found all cases of the disease, then your IFR is the same as your CFR, or 1%. But if it has only found 10% of the people with the disease, then your 10,000 confirmed cases are just the tip of a 100,000-person iceberg. With those 100 deaths, your IFR would be (100/100,000) or 0.1%."

Checked: https://unherd.com/2020/04/how-likely-are-you-to-die-of-coronavirus/, retrieved 2026-11-10
The abstract claim about denominators is carried down to a country with 10,000 cases and 100 deaths, and the arithmetic is printed in the sentence. The one image, "tip of a 100,000-person iceberg", sits beside the number it explains.

> "I want to reiterate: this is a very simple, stupid model, put together by a journalist, not an epidemiologist. The actual models will be far more complex, and will take into account other things — the number of cases in hospital and so on — to try to ground them in objective fact. Don’t mistake this for some plausible estimate of infection numbers."

Checked: https://unherd.com/2020/04/how-likely-are-you-to-die-of-coronavirus/, retrieved 2026-11-10
The writer labels his own calculation and says what it is not evidence for, in plain sentences. The person is visible in "stupid" and in the three-clause statement of his own limits. A paper that computes its own bound would label it the same way, as the paper's arithmetic.

## Simon Raper, "There's no evidence that ..."

Source: https://isi-web.org/article/theres-no-evidence

> "More exposure means more opportunities to mutate. More selection pressure means more variants. This is certainly true for COVID-19 and it might also be true for political rhetoric. The many additional news conferences, press briefings and talk radio slots brought on by the pandemic are perfect conditions for breeding new sound bites and rhetorical tricks. Angry journalists and members of the public provide the selection pressure, weeding out the phrases that fall flat."

Checked: https://isi-web.org/article/theres-no-evidence, retrieved 2026-11-10
A statistician opens a short piece with a joke that borrows the vocabulary of the field, and the joke is about a target the author is not part of. The first sentences are plain and parallel, and the turn to rhetoric comes in the third. The dryness is in the word choices, "perfect conditions for breeding".

> "One way to flush out the ‘no evidence’ trick is to ask about evidence for the opposing view. I might claim, for example, that there is no evidence that a particular coin is biased (withholding from you the fact that this was based on a mere ten flips). If you then ask me whether there is any evidence, based on the same data, that the coin is not biased then I will have to concede that there is not."

Checked: https://isi-web.org/article/theres-no-evidence, retrieved 2026-11-10
The general claim is reduced to a coin and a sample size of ten, and the writer plays the part of the person making the weak claim, in first person and with the sample size confessed in a parenthesis. A reader with a mathematics degree can check the reasoning at a glance. The passage is quoted for the move of using one small instance, and the subject of what a null result shows is the writer's own to work out.
