# Voice guide: feature/medicaid-lawful-immigrant-funding (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a piece about documents, written by someone who has read them and is telling a smart outsider what they say. The register is the one Amy Howe uses at SCOTUSblog when she walks through a statute and the opinions about it: plain declarative sentences, the operative words of the text in quotation marks, and then a sentence in ordinary language saying what those words do. In her Fischer piece the statute is named, quoted, and then put to work in the next clause ("which makes it a crime to..."). That is the model for the statute, the State Health Official letter and any state notice here. Give the citation and the quoted words, then say what they do to a state budget or a person. A defined term can ride inside the sentence that needs it, as an appositive or a "which" clause, so the reader never leaves the sentence to learn what a federal medical assistance percentage or a lawfully present category is. The reader reasons well and has not worked in health financing. Write to them as an equal who lacks one piece of vocabulary.

The central move of this article is separating what the law says from what the coverage says it says. Howe does a version of this in the Total Wine piece: she states what everyone before the court agreed on, then says what the question "really boiled down to." Frakt does a smaller version, answering "Almost!" to a reader's claim and then giving the figure that makes it almost true. Both grant what is right in the common version before moving it. The headline reading of 1 October has something right in it, and saying so, with the figure or date that makes it right, lets the correction read as a finding. Say once what the common account gets wrong and move on; do not rebuild a "not X but Y" frame in every paragraph. `spec/slop.md` counts that as a tic, and Howe never needs it.

Fairness to the law's stated rationale should look like Bagley stating the textualist case and Howe stating the retailers' justifications. Each presents the argument as its holder would, attributes it to a named person or document, and only then tests it against something checkable: a distance, a figure, a provision elsewhere in the text. Take the rationale from the primary source, in its own words, before weighing it, and weigh it with a fact and not with an adjective. Where the evidence supports only part of an argument, say which part, as Frakt does when he follows a claim about plans' extra revenue with the 15 percent cap that limits it.

Authority here comes from specificity: which section, which letter, what date, which state, which group. It does not come from announcing care. Howe's passages carry no sentence about how carefully she is reading. A qualification, when one is needed, names what is unknown, who produced an estimate and over what scope, or what would settle the question. Write "the estimate covers X" and not "the numbers should be read with caution."

Where a person shows up in this writing, it is in the weighing, not in the pronoun. Bagley's "one really good piece of evidence" and "everything else, to my mind, cuts against them" tell you what he thinks each thing is worth, and a reader can disagree with the ranking because he has shown the items. This piece should rank its evidence the same way: say which provision carries the weight and which one only looks like it does, and let the order show the judgment. Bagley's first-person phrasing belongs to his blog. Howe never uses one and is no less present. The piece stays in the third person and states what the documents establish.

Carry the abstraction down to an instance soon after stating it, as Howe does when she sets the residency requirement against a retailer who "could live 500 miles away." When the piece says a state now decides, show one state deciding and what that does for one kind of person in one category. A table can hold the group-by-group and state-by-state grid so that the prose can spend its time on the reasoning the table cannot show.

Punctuation follows the house standard, which is plainer than some of these writers. Frakt and Bagley use dashes freely and Howe uses an en dash as a stand-in for one. Use periods and commas where they do. Sentences can run long, as Howe's do, when they carry a quotation and its consequence, as long as the clauses join one thought.

The citation, the operative words and what they do arrive in one sentence, and the next sentence gives a judge's reading of a neighboring subsection in the same way. ## Amy Howe, "Opinion analysis: A total victory for Total Wine in 21st Amendment dispute"

Source: https://www.scotusblog.com/2019/06/opinion-analysis-a-total-victory-for-total-wine-in-21st-amendment-dispute/

> "At the Supreme Court, everyone seemed to agree that Tennessee would violate the Constitution if it imposed a similar residency requirement on retailers who wanted to sell something else, such as paint or milk, because it would be discriminating against out-of-state residents. The question before the justices really boiled down to whether the 21st Amendment “saves” laws like Tennessee’s: Today the justices concluded that it does not."

Checked: https://www.scotusblog.com/2019/06/opinion-analysis-a-total-victory-for-total-wine-in-21st-amendment-dispute/ (raw HTML fetched with curl), retrieved 2026-10-02
She sets down what was not in dispute before naming the one question that was, and she picks paint and milk, the exact goods that make the premise obvious. The reader leaves this paragraph knowing what the disagreement is. The writer is visible in the choice of that contrast.

> "Alito was equally skeptical of some of the other justifications that the retailers cited. For example, he noted, although the retailers suggested that the residency requirement “would promote responsible alcohol consumption,” because a retailer with community ties might “counsel or cut off sales to patrons who are known to be abusing alcohol,” there is no evidence that it actually has that effect. Moreover, Alito added, the retailer is only required to live in the same state – not the same community where he sells liquor – which in Tennessee means that he could live 500 miles away."

Checked: https://www.scotusblog.com/2019/06/opinion-analysis-a-total-victory-for-total-wine-in-21st-amendment-dispute/ (raw HTML fetched with curl), retrieved 2026-10-02
The retailers' rationale is given in their own words before it is tested, and the test is a fact: no evidence of the effect, and a distance in miles. The "500 miles" does the arguing, and the sentence holds no adjective about the justification.

## Nicholas Bagley, "Does it matter what Congress intended?"

Source: https://theincidentaleconomist.com/wordpress/does-it-matter-what-congress-intended/

> "Why might Congress’s “subjective intent” matter? Because language is an effort to communicate meaning. To understand what a speaker means, a listener must ask, “What did the speaker intend to convey to me?” In conversation and in statutory interpretation, the semantic content of a speaker’s words is almost always the best guide to what the speaker means to communicate. But it’s not the only guide. Other evidence can be persuasive, too."

Checked: https://theincidentaleconomist.com/wordpress/does-it-matter-what-congress-intended/ (raw HTML fetched with curl), retrieved 2026-10-02
A question is asked and answered in the next breath, which is the case the house rhetorical-question rule allows. The argument goes from an ordinary conversation to a statute in four short steps, so a reader without legal training can follow. The writer is a lawyer who is plainly thinking on the page and not displaying what he knows.

> "Why do textualists prefer rule-like approaches? For one thing, making laws ain’t pretty. Statutes are often shaped by awkward compromises. As John Manning explains, “courts risk upsetting a complex bargain among legislative stakeholders if judges rewrite a clear but messy statute to make it more congruent with some asserted background purpose.”"

Checked: https://theincidentaleconomist.com/wordpress/does-it-matter-what-congress-intended/ (raw HTML fetched with curl), retrieved 2026-10-02
Bagley gives the opposing view's reason in a scholar's words, attributed by name, in a paragraph that treats it as a reasonable position. The one colloquial phrase is his own and short. The person is audible there, and the sentence around it stays exact.

> "The challengers have one really good piece of evidence: the “established by the State” language, read in isolation, does support the view that Congress meant to restrict tax credits to state exchanges. Everything else, to my mind, cuts against them. Congress repeatedly used the “exchange established by the State” language as shorthand for “exchange.” Adopting the challengers’ interpretation would make a dog’s breakfast of other provisions of the statute."

Checked: https://theincidentaleconomist.com/wordpress/does-it-matter-what-congress-intended/ (raw HTML fetched with curl), retrieved 2026-10-02
He concedes the strongest point against his view before giving the evidence on the other side, and he counts the evidence on each side where the reader can see it. The judgment is in the weighing ("one really good piece," "everything else").

## Austin Frakt, "Answering questions about Medicare Advantage"

Source: https://theincidentaleconomist.com/wordpress/answering-questions-about-medicare-advantage/

> "Almost! According to government statistics, in 2010, Medicare Advantage plans received payments from the Medicare program equivalent to 113 percent of what it would cost a similar beneficiary to be covered by the traditional program. In 2017, that figure is 100 percent, but grows to 104 percent if you more accurately account for differences in the health of Medicare Advantage enrollees and traditional Medicare beneficiaries."

Checked: https://theincidentaleconomist.com/wordpress/answering-questions-about-medicare-advantage/ (raw HTML fetched with curl), retrieved 2026-10-02
He agrees with part of a reader's premise in one word, then gives the figure, its source, its year, and the adjustment that changes it. The figure is placed beside a baseline the reader can hold, the cost of the same person in the traditional program. His voice is a person answering a question, in a Q&A format this piece will not use.

> "Well, some believe that we should be taking advantage of market efficiency to save the government money, which was part of the original motivation for including private plan alternatives in Medicare. One key point of my piece was to compare what Medicare Advantage plans receive from the government to what it costs the plans to provide care, including marketing, administration, and profit as well."

Checked: https://theincidentaleconomist.com/wordpress/answering-questions-about-medicare-advantage/ (raw HTML fetched with curl), retrieved 2026-10-02
The program's original rationale is stated plainly as a belief some people hold, with its history, before he says what he measured. The comparison he made is described in terms of what goes into each side, so the reader knows what the number counts.

> "But there is some doubt that plans provide additional benefits like these as efficiently as they could. Because the Medicare Advantage market is not as competitive as it might be, studies have shown that plans may pay more for benefits than they should, and enrollees receive less value from them than their costs. However, it’s also the case that the Affordable Care Act limits Medicare Advantage spending on things like marketing, management, and profit to 15 percent of revenue."

Checked: https://theincidentaleconomist.com/wordpress/answering-questions-about-medicare-advantage/ (raw HTML fetched with curl), retrieved 2026-10-02
A claim is followed by the evidence that limits it and then by the provision of law that limits that, all in one paragraph and with no commentary on the balance. The "may" is attached to what studies found, not spread over the writer's own statements. The writer is visible as someone keeping a ledger of what is and is not known.
