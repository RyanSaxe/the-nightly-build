# Evidence: feature/medicaid-lawful-immigrant-funding (researcher 01)

Researched 2 October 2026. Every document below was opened and read, not taken from a search snippet, unless marked otherwise.

**What the evidence supports, and where it is thin.** The statute, the CMS letter and five state records support the commission's core mechanism. Section 71109 of P.L. 119-21 ends federal matching payments (federal financial participation, FFP) for full Medicaid and CHIP coverage of everyone except citizens/nationals, lawful permanent residents (LPRs), Cuban/Haitian entrants and Compact of Free Association (COFA) migrants. It does not itself cancel anyone's coverage, and CMS says in terms that it will not require states to keep covering the dropped groups with state money. The state record then diverges: New Mexico, New York and California keep the dropped adults covered in some form, and Washington, North Carolina and South Carolina do not. Three findings qualify the angle and the writer must absorb them. (1) The loss is narrower than "several categories of lawfully present immigrants" implies for children and pregnant people, because the federal match for lawfully residing children and pregnant people under the CHIPRA 214 option is untouched, and 39 states, DC and 3 territories had elected it when CMS wrote. (2) "Divergence" is not always a free choice and not always a soft landing. New York's continuation is attributed by KFF to a court ruling. California's is funded for nine months, to 1 July 2027, then moves to restricted scope. North Carolina's own statute deleted every state-optional group and made the federal cutoff automatic in state law. (3) CMS treats a state-funded continuation as not being Medicaid or CHIP at all, so "keeps Medicaid" is the wrong phrase for the divergent states. The record is thinnest on headcounts (no primary count of affected people exists; the national numbers are all secondary) and on the stated legislative rationale (the statute has no findings, and I found no committee report text for this section).

Section 71109(c) appropriates $15,000,000 for FY2026 to the CMS Administrator for implementation. Section 71110 adds section 1905(kk): from 1 October 2026 the matching rate for emergency-condition care to the covered aliens "shall not exceed" the state's regular FMAP under section 1905(b), which removes the 90 percent expansion-group rate for that care.

```
Quote:       "in no event shall payment be made to a State under this section for medical assistance furnished to an individual unless such individual is" (1903(v)(5)). The text restricts payment to the state. It contains no sentence that terminates eligibility or requires a state to stop covering anyone.
Notes:       The statute names no excluded groups. It lists the retained ones, so "which lose" is derived by subtraction. The enrolled text carries no findings or purpose clause.
```

### S2. CMS guidance: State Health Official letter SHO #26-001

```
URL:         https://www.medicaid.gov/federal-policy-guidance/downloads/sho26001.pdf
             (plain request returns the 29-page PDF; the same address returned 403 to a request that presented a browser user-agent string, so it is gated by client, not dead)
Kind:        primary. CMS (the implementing agency) authored it and owns every statement of what states must do.
Establishes: firsthand: the effective date, who counts as FFP-eligible, what states must do for applicants and current enrollees, that CMS will not require state-only coverage, that CMS does not treat state-only coverage as Medicaid/CHIP, the exceptions, the verification path, the SPA deadlines, managed-care and claiming rules, and the category-by-category match table (Appendix A).
Paraphrase:  Letter dated 8 April 2026, "RE: Implementation of Section 71109 'Alien Medicaid Eligibility' of the Working Families Tax Cut Legislation (Public Law 119-21)", signed by Dan Brillman, "Deputy Administrator, CMS; Director, Center for Medicaid and CHIP Services". CMS calls P.L. 119-21 the "Working Families Tax Cut" (WFTC) legislation.
  - Scope (p. 3-4): from 1 October 2026 FFP for non-emergency Medicaid and CHIP is limited to citizens/nationals, LPRs, Cuban/Haitian entrants and COFA migrants. CMS names the last three "FFP-eligible noncitizens". FFP is also unavailable for limited-benefit groups such as family planning. Examples of the dropped group: "asylees, refugees, parolees, or victims of trafficking".
  - Three exceptions only (p. 5): emergency Medicaid (1903(v)(2)); the CHIPRA 214 option for lawfully residing children (to 21 in Medicaid, to 19 in CHIP) and pregnant women (1903(v)(4)); CHIP health services initiatives. Section 71109 "did not make any changes" to emergency Medicaid or the 214 option.
  - What the law did not change (p. 6): PRWORA's definition of qualified alien, and the five-year waiting period. States "must continue to apply the five-year waiting period to LPRs" unless an exception applies, and must continue to excuse Cuban/Haitian entrants and COFA migrants from it. So "LPR retained" still means a green-card holder past the bar or exempt from it.
  - No duty to cover with state money (p. 7): "neither the text of section 71109 ... nor its legislative history demonstrates a clear Congressional intent to require that states provide full Medicaid benefits to qualified noncitizens without receiving federal matching funds." CMS "will not require states to provide state-only funded health coverage" to the dropped groups, "including populations listed at section 1902 of the Act". The same reasoning is applied to CHIP.
  - State-only coverage is not Medicaid (p. 7): CMS "would not consider health coverage that a state opts to continue to provide with 100 percent state-only funds to be Medicaid". A dropped person therefore lacks "satisfactory immigration status" for full Medicaid for purposes of section 1137(d).
  - Timing (p. 5, 8): for applicants and for enrollees "on and after October 1, 2026" states must claim FFP correctly. Current enrollees: states "must promptly act" (citing 42 CFR 435.916(d) as of 2023 and 457.343), identify potentially affected enrollees, first re-verify through DHS SAVE without involving the enrollee, and only then ask the enrollee. If status cannot be verified, the enrollee gets a 90-day reasonable opportunity period. Before ending coverage the state must consider every other basis (the 214 option, emergency Medicaid) and give advance notice with hearing rights (at least ten days in Medicaid).
  - Continuous eligibility (p. 10-11): enrollees in a continuous-eligibility or postpartum period in Medicaid lose full-benefit FFP and keep emergency-Medicaid FFP only. Separate-CHIP enrollees get no FFP at all (no emergency category exists in separate CHIP), so those states "would terminate separate CHIP eligibility" after notice, unless they have the 214 option.
  - 214 option count: as of publication, 39 states, DC and three territories had elected the 214 option (footnote 43, p. 10).
  - State-only programs (p. 16, 18): state-only services must sit under "a separate and distinct contract and payment" from Medicaid managed-care contracts (restating SMD letter #25-003). States with state-only programs must control claiming so that only emergency Medicaid is claimed. CMS reports "focused financial oversight of eight states" with state-only programs for noncitizens and "approximately $1.8B in questionable expenditures".
  - Emergency match (p. 19-20): section 71110 caps the emergency-Medicaid match at the regular FMAP from 1 October 2026; CMS expects states "may, in fact, experience an overall total increase" in emergency-only expenditures.
  - State plan amendments (p. 20-21): Medicaid SPAs due by 31 December 2026 for an effective date of 1 October 2026. Separate-CHIP SPAs that restrict eligibility are due by 30 November 2026 (60 days after the effective date, after public notice).
  - Medicare contrast (p. 7-8): section 1899C "applies 18 months after the date of enactment" for current enrollees. Eighteen months after 4 July 2025 is 4 January 2027. The letter promises later guidance.
  - Verification (p. 13-15, Appendix B p. 29): the Hub adds a new "MCEligibleNonCitizenCode" indicator that returns Yes only for LPR, Cuban/Haitian entrant or COFA migrant. CMS's own example: an asylee returns "Yes" for qualified noncitizen and "No" for eligible noncitizen.
  - CMS concedes (footnote 16, p. 4) that 42 CFR 435.406(a)(2) and 457.320(b)(6), which require coverage of qualified noncitizens, "will not be aligned" with section 1903(v)(5) from 1 October 2026 and says it plans rulemaking.
Locators:    Letter pp. 3-8 (statute and interaction with PRWORA); pp. 8-11 (enrollee redetermination); pp. 12-16 (applications, verification, managed care); pp. 16-18 (systems, claiming); p. 19-20 (FMAP); pp. 20-21 (SPAs); Appendix A pp. 23-28 (Table 1); Appendix B p. 29 (Table 2).
Quote:       "CMS will not require states to provide state-only funded health coverage to qualified noncitizens for whom FFP is not available for full Medicaid benefits under 1903(v)(5) of the Act" (p. 7). And: "we would not consider health coverage that a state opts to continue to provide with 100 percent state-only funds to be Medicaid" (p. 7). Both are exact-wording evidence for the angle.
```

## Contradictions

1. **Commission versus the record: "several categories of lawfully present immigrants lose the match."** True for adults who are not pregnant. For children (to 21 in Medicaid, to 19 in CHIP) and pregnant people, the 214 option keeps the federal match, and CMS says 39 states, DC and three territories had elected it (S2, footnote 43). The statute carves it out in the opening words "except as provided in paragraphs (2) and (4)" (S1). KFF and CCF say the same (S16, S18). New Mexico, New York, North Carolina (after S10), California (S7), Washington and South Carolina all say children and pregnant people are unaffected or retained. A reader in a 214 state who is a refugee child sees no change on 1 October.

2. **"Each state decides" versus North Carolina's statute.** NC did not leave the choice open on 1 October. S.L. 2026-1 section 3C.4 wrote "federally required" into G.S. 108A-54.3A(c), effective 1 October 2026 (S8), and the later budget restored only the 214 option (S10). For refugees and asylees, North Carolina's cutoff is automatic by state statute. Legislators and the governor described the deletion of children and pregnant women as unintended (S9; S17 says "some state legislators have indicated that the cut was unintentional"). The fix is a different instrument than the cut and landed two months later. The commission's "state-by-state decision" holds, but it was decided by legislation that pre-committed to the federal floor.

3. **"Keeps coverage" versus CMS's definition.** CMS says it does not consider state-only coverage to be Medicaid or CHIP (S2, p. 7), so the dropped person "does not have satisfactory immigration status for full Medicaid". States describe it differently: New Mexico "creating its own program" (S13), New York a shift from Medicaid to the Essential Plan (S14), California "state-funded" full-scope Medi-Cal in budget language (S4, S5) while its Medicaid plan restricts to FFP-eligible groups (S6). California's SPA says nothing about the state-funded continuation (S6). Headline wording like "keeps Medicaid" is contradicted by the federal position and by the state plan filings.

4. **"Divergence" versus New York's legal duty.** KFF reports that a longstanding court ruling requires New York to provide state-funded coverage to lawfully present immigrants who would be Medicaid-eligible but for status (S17). If that holds, New York's continuation is closer to compliance than to a discretionary choice. I did not read the ruling, and New York's own page (S14) does not mention it.

5. **"Continuing coverage" versus California's end date.** California funds full-scope coverage for the affected groups only until a "July 1, 2027 transition to restricted-scope Medi-Cal" (S4, S5), and moves them to fee-for-service on 1 January 2027 (S4). It is a nine-month deferral, not a settled commitment. The Legislature said it would "revisit" reductions in the 2027-28 budget (S5, overview bullet).

6. **Dollar figure for the same California item.** The enacted budget summary gives $364.9 million General Fund for 2026-27 (S4). The Assembly floor report gives $303.1 million (S5). The enacted summary line is a net cost for the transition to restricted scope; the floor report line is the cost of full-scope coverage through 1 July 2027. They may measure different things, or the final figure may have moved after 27 June 2026. Do not quote either as "the cost" without the source and the label. A search result I did not open also printed $668.1 million for a similar line; I could not find it in S4.

7. **Two different "1.4 million" figures.** CCF's 1.4 million is lawfully present immigrants losing federally funded coverage across three programs (S18). The White House's 1.4 million is "illegal aliens" a House committee projected would be removed from state-funded programs and Medicaid-related coverage (S20). They are unrelated populations and the identical number invites a false match.

8. **Washington's timing versus CMS's instruction.** Washington's toolkit says "most changes will occur at the next coverage renewal after the new requirements take effect" (S12). CMS's letter says states "must promptly act" on the change and apply FFP limits to enrollees on and after 1 October (S2, pp. 8-9). Which of these describes when an individual's coverage actually ends in Washington is not established.

9. **Why "Medicaid ends on October 1" is also imprecise on timing.** CMS requires re-verification first through SAVE, then enrollee contact, a 90-day reasonable opportunity period when status cannot be verified, and at least ten days' advance notice (S2, pp. 8-10, 15). The KFF brief adds that coverage during that period "may extend beyond October 1, 2026" (S16). So the date is when the federal match stops, not when each person is terminated.

## Limits

- **No primary count of affected people.** CMS gives no national number. CBO's own documents were not opened (cbo.gov returned 403 to my requests), so the $6.2 billion and 100,000 figures rest on CRS, KFF and CCF transcriptions. The state counts are the states' own (Washington about 14,000; North Carolina's 27,000 is a governor's estimate for a population that was later restored). I found no verified state count for California, New Mexico or New York in the documents I read. Do not assert a nationwide count of people who lose coverage as fact.
- **Which states let coverage lapse is partly established.** Primary records show North Carolina (adults, by statute), Washington (adults, with other options listed) and South Carolina (state plan amended, "emergency services" for others) following the federal cutoff. South Carolina's and North Carolina's notices do not state that no state money is available; they state who keeps Medicaid. Washington lists substitute programs that already existed. I did not find an explicit "we will not use state funds" statement from any of the three.
- **The state-funded side rests on thinner primary text.** New Mexico's document (S13) names no program, funding amount or start date. New York's page (S14) names no funding source. California's continuation is in the enacted budget summary (S4) and the floor report (S5) but I could not open California DHCS Information Letter 26-21 (31 July 2026, reportedly the outreach letter to affected members) or All-County Welfare Directors Letter 26-13: dhcs.ca.gov blocks command-line and fetch-tool requests with a bot filter, and I have only search-result summaries of them. I did not cite them.
- **New York's court-ruling claim is unverified firsthand.** KFF says a longstanding ruling requires state-funded coverage; I did not open it.
- **California's enacted act is not the document I read.** S4 is the Department of Finance's enacted-budget summary and S5 is a pre-vote committee floor report. I did not open the budget bill, the trailer bill or the governor's signing record, and the two dollar figures differ.
- **Whether CMS has approved any state plan amendment is unknown.** California's two SPAs were "Pending" with approval date "N/A" as filed (S6, S7). CMS's deadline for Medicaid SPAs is 31 December 2026, so approvals may not yet exist.
- **No data on what happened on 1 October 2026.** The record is documents dated up to 22 September 2026 plus state pages whose later updates I could not date. I found no primary report of actual terminations, reverification outcomes or SAVE failures. The CMS press office's own listing for 1 October 2026 is unrelated.
- **The law's stated rationale is thin.** The statute has no findings (S1). The only primary rationale I opened is CMS's (S3) and the White House's 2025 release (S20, about the House bill and a different population). I did not find or open a committee report or floor statement for section 71109, so the "fair to the law's own stated rationale" requirement can be met from the CMS statement and the White House release only. Both are executive-branch statements.
- **The SHO text is CMS's reading, not a ruling.** CMS reasons from Pennhurst, Harris v. McRae and Detgen that Congress did not clearly require states to cover the dropped groups without federal money (S2, p. 7). A state or litigant could contest that reading. I found no case challenging it and did not search the docket.
- **Medicare as a dated contrast is supported only thinly.** S2 (p. 7-8) says section 1899C applies "18 months after the date of enactment" to current enrollees, which I computed as about 4 January 2027. CCF (S18) says current enrollees "will lose their coverage by January 4, 2027" and that new enrollees were affected from enactment. Neither is a Medicare-agency source.
- **Federal share nuance not established.** I did not find the FMAP percentages for the affected groups before 1 October 2026 in a primary document, so the article cannot say what share of cost shifts to states for those groups. SHO p. 19 says regular FMAP applies to full-benefit FFP-eligible groups and says nothing of the dropped groups' previous rate.
