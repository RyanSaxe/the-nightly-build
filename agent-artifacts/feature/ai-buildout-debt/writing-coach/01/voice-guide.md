# Voice guide: feature/ai-buildout-debt (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a five-minute feature for engineers who build machine learning systems
and read widely, about a part of finance most of them have never had to model:
how a data-center buildout gets paid for when much of the obligation is a lease,
a guarantee or a take-or-pay contract and not a bond. The register is a
practitioner explaining financial plumbing to a numerate outsider. Plain
declarative sentences, the instrument's real name, and no adjectives standing in
for a figure. The reader can do the arithmetic and should be handed the inputs
to do it. The piece treats that reader as someone who will check the sum, which
is how McKenzie treats his reader in the margin-loan passage: each step of the
$50k-to-$17k chain is on the page, so the 4X arrives as a result.

Where the material is a chain of parties, write it the way Afonso, Choi,
Cisternas and Riordan write the repo chain: name who puts money in, who sits in
the middle, who ends up holding it, and what each one is paid for. Their
tri-party sentence is the model for a distinction this subject may need, between
a party that handles an arrangement and a party that bears its risk. If the
Fed's box separates a hyperscaler, a special-purpose vehicle, a private-credit
fund, an insurer and a bank by who carries what, say so in those terms and let
the diagram carry the order. Define lease commencement, residual-value guarantee
and take-or-pay once, where each first appears, at the level of precision the
FSR and the 10-Q notes use, and then keep the same term.

McKenzie's symmetric-exposure passage shows a distinction that may matter here:
the size of a commitment is one number and the capacity to pay it is another.
If the reporting supports a gap between the two, state the two numbers
separately and give the source for each. The fair rival view, that the
commitments are serviceable from cash flow, gets stated in its holder's words
before the piece says where the Fed's numbers leave it.

Davies shows how to report a limit on measurement as a fact with its own
evidence, not as a caveat. Where the Fed says what it cannot see, such as
undiscounted commitments or exposures it cannot trace to a final holder, give
that limit its source and its specifics. Davies also shows that a dry subject
can carry a person's curiosity and an occasional dry joke anchored in the
nouns. A line like that is welcome where it falls out of the material. The
piece does not need one, and it should not turn the buildout into a joke or the
Fed into a straight man. It stays an explanatory piece in a news paper: no
forecasts, no first-person asides about the writer's reading, and no aside at
the reader's expense.

## Patrick McKenzie, "Perpetual futures, explained"

Source: https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/perpetual-futures-explained/

> "Where did the money come from? Someone else was symmetrically short exposure
> to Bitcoin via a perp. It is, with some very important caveats incoming, a
> closed system: since no good or service is being produced except the
> speculation, winning money means someone else lost."

Checked: https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/perpetual-futures-explained/,
retrieved 2026-11-19

He asks the question the reader is already asking and answers it at once with a
specific counterparty. "With some very important caveats incoming" is McKenzie
keeping track of what he has not yet explained, out loud, so the reader knows
the simple version is provisional without being buried in it.

> "As a simplified example, if you have $50k of cash, you’d be allowed to buy
> $100k of stock. You now have $50k of equity and a $50k loan: 2x leverage.
> Should the value of that stock decline to about $67k, you still owe the $50k
> loan, and so only have $17k remaining equity. You’re now on the precipice of
> being 4X leveraged, and should expect a margin call very soon, if your broker
> hasn’t “blown you out of the trade” already."

Checked: https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/perpetual-futures-explained/,
retrieved 2026-11-19

Every quantity is given, and each sentence moves the balance sheet one step, so
a reader can verify the arithmetic without a calculator. The trade's own slang,
"blown you out of the trade", comes in quotation marks at the end, after the
mechanism is clear, and it is the one place his voice shows in the passage.

> "Risk in perps has to be symmetric: if (accounting for leverage) there are
> 100,000 units of Somecoin exposure long, then there are 100,000 units of
> Somecoin exposure short. This does not imply that the shorts or longs are
> sufficiently capitalized to actually pay for all the exposure in all
> instances."

Checked: https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/perpetual-futures-explained/,
retrieved 2026-11-19

He states an accounting identity and then, in the next sentence, separates the
size of the obligation from the ability to meet it. The made-up "Somecoin" keeps
the example general without hiding the units, which is a small choice a
practitioner who has explained this many times would make.

## Dan Davies, "bonds are life, equities are casualty"

Source: https://backofmind.substack.com/p/bonds-are-life-equities-are-casualty

> "Except the initial stages of that research seem to suggest that they …
> weren’t? Actuarial science and its history is a big and interesting thing, but
> so far all the books I’ve read have something like eight chapters about life
> assurance and only one short one about every other kind. According to Craig
> Turnbull’s “History of Actuarial Thought in Britain”, there were no articles
> published on the subject of general insurance in the Society of Actuaries’
> journal between 1880 and 1945."

Checked: https://backofmind.substack.com/p/bonds-are-life-equities-are-casualty,
retrieved 2026-11-19

He reports that his own expectation was wrong, then backs the surprise with a
named source and a date range. The chapter count is a joke and evidence at
once. The person is visible in the half-question "they … weren’t?", which
records the moment the evidence went against him.

> "Life insurance is, in its own way, epistemologically bounded. There is only
> one event you need to think about, and the insured amount is agreed in the
> contract. … whether someone is alive or dead is an unambiguous fact which is a
> matter of public record."

Checked: https://backofmind.substack.com/p/bonds-are-life-equities-are-casualty,
retrieved 2026-11-19

He explains why one kind of risk can be measured by listing the conditions that
make it measurable: one event, a fixed amount, a public fact. The large word,
"epistemologically", is used once and then cashed out in short concrete
clauses.

> "The insured events aren’t anything like as comparable, the exposures aren’t
> fixed and there isn’t such a natural unit. Every ship is different (in fact,
> every voyage of every ship is potentially very different). Fires do different
> amounts of damage – it wasn’t even obvious in the early days whether the
> natural unit for fire insurance was a building or a floor."

Checked: https://backofmind.substack.com/p/bonds-are-life-equities-are-casualty,
retrieved 2026-11-19

This is the same three conditions from the earlier passage, each one failing,
with a concrete case for each. The building-or-floor detail is a specific
historical fact that shows what "no natural unit" meant in practice, and
choosing it over a general statement is Davies's taste at work.

## Gara Afonso, Jun-Davinci Choi, Gonzalo Cisternas and Will Riordan, "Follow the Cash! Microstructure of Repo Markets" (Liberty Street Economics)

Source: https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/09/follow-the-cash-microstructure-of-repo-markets/

> "Cash typically enters repo markets through cash-rich investors such as MMFs
> looking for a short-term secured investment. Dealers intermediate the funds,
> directly or through the interdealer market, and lend to cash borrowers, often
> hedge funds seeking to lever their investment strategies."

Checked: https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/09/follow-the-cash-microstructure-of-repo-markets/,
retrieved 2026-11-19

Two sentences carry the whole chain in order, and each party arrives with the
reason it is there. The authors are a team writing in an institutional voice,
so the person shows in the choices: the motives they name and the order they
put the parties in.

> "For instance, as the stylized diagram below shows, a dealer may enter a
> repo—borrowing cash—with a more creditworthy counterparty (for example, a
> government MMF) and enter a reverse repo—lending cash—with a riskier one (for
> example, a levered hedge fund), earning the difference in rates (a 10
> basis-point spread in this example) for intermediating between the ultimate
> borrower and lender."

Checked: https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/09/follow-the-cash-microstructure-of-repo-markets/,
retrieved 2026-11-19

The sentence is written to be read beside a diagram: it names each leg, glosses
the jargon in place ("borrowing cash", "lending cash"), and says what the middle
party earns with one number. It is long, but every clause is a label for
something drawn.

> "In contrast to the centrally cleared interdealer market, tri-party repo does
> not transform the counterparty risk between the buyer and seller, because Bank
> of New York Mellon acts solely as an agent and not as a counterparty (see TMPG
> 2022)."

Checked: https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/09/follow-the-cash-microstructure-of-repo-markets/,
retrieved 2026-11-19

It separates the party that processes a transaction from the party that bears
its risk, names the firm, and cites the document. The contrast is with a market
the post has already described, so the distinction is earned and not set up for
effect.
