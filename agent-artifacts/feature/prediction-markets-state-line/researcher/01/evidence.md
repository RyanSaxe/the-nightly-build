# Evidence record

The record supports the commission's central distinction: Polymarket US is a CFTC-designated contract market, but designation regulates the exchange and does not itself adjudicate every listed contract's status under the Commodity Exchange Act or displace every state-law duty. New York alleges that Polymarket's products satisfy the state's definition of gambling and that the platform operates without a state gaming license; Polymarket says CFTC jurisdiction is exclusive. The strongest current judicial evidence is a circuit split reached on preliminary relief, not a final nationwide rule. The evidence is thinnest on New York's exact pleaded counts because neither the state-court petition nor Polymarket's federal complaint was publicly retrievable from a stable court or government URL during this research. Allegations attributed below to New York therefore come from the state's official announcement and two independent reports, not from an opened complaint.

### 5. Commodity Exchange Act jurisdiction clause

URL:         https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/7/2
Kind:        primary legal text because it reproduces 7 U.S.C. § 2(a)(1)(A)
Establishes: The CFTC has exclusive jurisdiction, subject to enumerated exceptions, over transactions involving swaps or futures traded or executed on a DCM. The same paragraph says that, except for what the exclusive grant already covers, the section does not supersede or limit other federal or state regulatory authorities or state and federal courts.
Paraphrase:  The preemption question depends on the object being regulated. If a product is a swap traded on a DCM, the exclusive-jurisdiction clause is powerful. The savings language leaves room outside that covered field. Designation alone does not answer the antecedent question whether a particular product is a swap.
Locators:    7 U.S.C. § 2(a)(1)(A), especially the first three paragraphs; opened lines 19-26
Quote:       The operative grant covers “transactions involving swaps” traded or executed on a designated contract market.

### 6. Commodity Exchange Act special rule for event contracts

URL:         https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/7/7a-2
Kind:        primary legal text because it reproduces 7 U.S.C. § 7a-2(c)(5)(C)
Establishes: For event contracts based on excluded commodities, the CFTC may determine that contracts involving unlawful activity, terrorism, assassination, war, gaming, or similar activity are contrary to the public interest. A contract so determined may not be listed or cleared. The Commission has a 90-day deadline once it commences that review, unless extended by agreement.
Paraphrase:  Congress expressly anticipated that an event contract can involve gaming. The statute gives the CFTC a product-review function; it does not say that DCM designation converts every event contract into a lawful product. Courts disagree about how this discretionary statutory language interacts with the current regulation.
Locators:    § 7a-2(c)(5)(C)(i)-(iv); opened lines 83-128
Quote:       None needed.

### 7. Current federal event-contract regulation

URL:         https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/chapter-I/part-40/section-40.11
Kind:        primary regulatory text because the eCFR gives the current text of 17 C.F.R. § 40.11; the page notes it was current through September 23, 2026
Establishes: Section 40.11(a) says a registered entity shall not list or clear an excluded-commodity contract that involves, relates to, or references gaming or conduct unlawful under state or federal law. Section 40.11(c) permits a 90-day CFTC review and calls for an approval or disapproval order at its end.
Paraphrase:  This current text is the Ninth Circuit's principal answer to the Third Circuit: an unreviewed self-certification does not erase the regulation's express prohibition. The Third Circuit read CFTC inaction and presumptive self-certification differently. A writer should not resolve this conflict by calling a proposal or enforcement choice the governing rule.
Locators:    § 40.11(a)(1)-(2) and (c)(1)-(2); opened lines 98-109
Quote:       A registered entity “shall not list for trading” a covered contract that relates to gaming.

### 8. New York's statutory gambling definitions

URL:         https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/PEN/225.00
Kind:        primary because the New York Senate publishes the state's current statutory text
Establishes: New York defines gambling as risking something of value on a contest of chance or a future contingent event outside the person's control, with an agreement to receive value upon a specified outcome. “Advance gambling activity” includes creating, soliciting, conducting, financing, or recording a gambling activity. “Bookmaking” means unlawfully accepting public bets as a business on future contingent events. “Unlawful” means not specifically authorized by law.
Paraphrase:  New York's theory does not require showing that Polymarket is the counterparty to each user. The statutory definitions reach business conduct that materially facilitates wagers and financial or recording phases. Whether federal law preempts applying those definitions to contracts on a DCM is the disputed federal question.
Locators:    Penal Law § 225.00(1)-(6), (9), and (12); opened lines 48-100 and 129-149
Quote:       None needed.

### 10. Third Circuit: KalshiEX LLC v. Flaherty

URL:         https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca3/25-1922/25-1922-2026-04-06.html
Kind:        primary because the page reproduces the published April 6, 2026 opinion of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit
Establishes: In a 2-1 decision reviewing a preliminary injunction, the court held that Kalshi had a reasonable chance of proving New Jersey's laws preempted as applied to sports event contracts on its DCM. The majority treated those contracts as swaps because their payouts depend on events associated with economic consequences. It found both field and conflict preemption. It also said ordinary state common-law and general antifraud actions can survive where they do not regulate DCM trading. Judge Jane Roth dissented, arguing the products were functionally sports wagers and that gambling sits within traditional state police power.
Paraphrase:  This is the strongest platform-side authority, but its posture matters. The majority affirmed preliminary relief based on likelihood of success; it did not issue a final judgment binding New York. The opinion is Third Circuit law, while New York is in the Second Circuit. The majority's distinction between direct regulation of DCM trading and preserved fraud/common-law claims supports treating product regulation and consumer protection as separate layers.
Locators:    majority at pages 1-16, especially pp. 4-15; dissent begins at p. 20; web lines 59-122 and 126-137
Quote:       None needed.

### 11. Ninth Circuit: KalshiEX, LLC v. Assad

URL:         https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/25-7516/25-7516-2026-08-28.html
Kind:        primary because the page reproduces the published August 28, 2026 opinion of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
Establishes: A unanimous panel affirmed dissolution of a preliminary injunction as to sports contracts. It held that Kalshi had not shown likely CEA preemption because its sports bets were not swaps and because 17 C.F.R. § 40.11 currently prohibits gaming contracts. The panel rejected express, conflict, and field preemption on the record before it, and remanded the election-contract issue. Judge Kenneth K. Lee concurred, stressing that the statutory special rule is ambiguous but the current regulation controls.
Paraphrase:  This is the strongest state-side appellate authority and directly conflicts with Flaherty on both swap classification and preemption. It also gives the article a product-specific operational test: a court may ask what the contract references and how it functions, not merely whether the venue holds DCM status. The court did not decide all event contracts; it separated sports from election contracts.
Locators:    opinion pp. 10-50, especially pp. 12-14, 34-40, and Judge Lee's concurrence at pp. 49-50; web lines 108-125, 212-235, and 271-276
Quote:       None needed.

## Contradictions

- The Third Circuit and Ninth Circuit disagree on the threshold classification. Flaherty says sports event contracts on a DCM fit the statutory swap definition; Assad says sports bets are not swaps when the CEA is read in context.
- The circuits also disagree about 17 C.F.R. § 40.11. Flaherty treated self-certification and the CFTC's failure to review or prohibit particular sports contracts as supporting federal coverage. Assad held that the regulation's existing “shall not list” text remains operative unless amended and that no 90-day approval order had issued.
- Polymarket emphasizes user-to-user trading, market prices, and fees rather than house odds. New York relies on a functional statutory definition that reaches risking value on an uncontrollable future event and separately reaches those who materially aid the activity. The product mechanics are therefore relevant but not dispositive under the state definition.
- The CFTC designation order confirms exchange-level status and duties. It does not contain the product-specific or preemption determination that Polymarket's public position might lead a reader to infer.
- The commission's proposed three-layer structure needs one qualification: the current New York announcement clearly alleges state gambling violations and consumer harms, but the exact consumer-protection causes of action cannot be identified without the state pleading. Consumer protection is established here as a regulatory interest and remedial layer, not as a confirmed separate pleaded count.

## Limits

- The September 24, 2026 New York state-court petition was not available at a stable public government or court URL during this research. Exact counts, paragraph numbers, exhibits, jurisdictional allegations, and the legal basis for treble-gain relief remain unverified from the pleading.
- Polymarket's September 24 federal complaint was likewise not retrievable. Its preemption theory and requested declaration are confirmed through Reuters, but exact counts, docket number, venue allegations, and citations should not be supplied without the filing.
- No court has decided the newly filed New York dispute. The current procedural posture is competing complaints and requested relief.
- Flaherty and Assad address Kalshi and sports-related contracts. They are highly relevant but do not automatically decide every Polymarket product category or New York cause of action.
- New York lies in the Second Circuit. Neither the Third nor Ninth Circuit controls its federal courts, though the opinions define the live split.
- The state announcement mentions health studies, including a 32 percent suicidal-ideation figure. Those studies were not needed to establish the legal classification and were not opened here, so the article should omit that number unless separately verified from the underlying research.
- The state announcement says Polymarket's value reportedly exceeds $20 billion, but it does not own that estimate. Omit it unless the underlying transaction or valuation source is opened.
- The record supports consumer-protection obligations as a distinct remedial concern, but not a claim that New York pleaded a freestanding deceptive-practices count.
