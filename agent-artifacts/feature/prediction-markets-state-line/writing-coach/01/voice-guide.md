# Voice guide

## How this piece should sound

The central claim is narrow enough to be useful: federal regulation of an exchange does not answer every state-law question about every contract sold on it. The prose should keep matching authority to conduct. CFTC designation, New York gambling law, and consumer-protection duties belong in separate sentences until the article has shown exactly where they overlap. Alison Frankel's two excerpts below show the right combination of direct judgment and visible legal uncertainty; the article can reach conclusions without pretending that a complaint is a holding or that one trial-court ruling has settled preemption.

The orientation should identify the product, New York's allegations, and Polymarket's response before naming the doctrinal dispute. The next movement can explain what federal exchange oversight actually authorizes and supervises. A state-law movement can then state New York's police-power and consumer-protection theories in their strongest form, followed by Polymarket's strongest exclusivity and preemption arguments. Any Kalshi decisions should clarify a particular legal test or procedural posture, not become a parallel history. The final movement should compare the claims provision by provision and end on the operational question in the commission: which product feature or federal approval displaces which state rule, for which contract and which user.

Treat all three positions as arguments with owners. New York gets the benefit of its actual complaint and statutes, not a caricature about hostility to innovation. Polymarket gets its best federal text, regulatory status, and cited precedents, not a generic claim that federal regulation always wins. The federal preemption analysis should distinguish express, field, and conflict theories wherever the sources support those distinctions, and it should say when the present record leaves one unresolved. Patrick McKenzie's short declarations are useful here: after enough mechanics, a plain sentence can identify the consequence without puffing it up.

Primary legal materials set the boundaries. A complaint establishes what New York alleges; it does not establish the alleged conduct. A statute or regulation establishes its own text. A court order establishes its holding and procedural posture. Agency and company statements establish those speakers' positions. Independent reporting can supply chronology and reactions but should not replace any available primary document. Related litigation is comparative evidence only when differences in jurisdiction, contract type, exchange status, or posture are made explicit.

Quote only when the exact wording carries legal force or reveals a party's stated theory. Keep each quotation to the shortest complete unit that preserves its meaning, ordinarily no more than one sentence, and do not stack quotations where a precise paraphrase will do. Attribute contested characterizations in the sentence that contains them.

The register can be assured and lightly conversational, but the personality should come from choices of detail and judgment rather than jokes about the parties. Quinta Jurecic's excerpts show how a short verdict can follow concrete procedural reporting and how a restrained final sentence can return to practical consequences. Avoid a date-led opening, a proposal-schema structure, and a close that claims the dispute “passes a test.” Also watch for “regulatory gray area,” “collision course,” “Wild West,” “at stake,” “only time will tell,” “the tension is real,” “not just ... but,” and ornamental versions of “X is not Y; it is Z.” The last line should state the unresolved allocation question, not offer a moral about innovation and regulation.

The one-sentence paragraph isolates the legal and operational distinction after the surrounding explanation has earned it. McKenzie's voice appears in the willingness to stop and say the decisive fact plainly.

> “This sounds like a joke but it isn't one.”

The sentence acknowledges the reader's likely reaction without turning the explanation into a performance. McKenzie uses personality to keep attention on a counterintuitive mechanism, then returns immediately to how that mechanism works.

The sentence carries Jurecic's judgment while qualifying the relief expressed around it. Its restraint is personal: she refuses both false reassurance and a longer flourish.

> “That final part of his assessment is obviously correct.”

Jurecic marks the precise portion of a concurrence she accepts before supplying concrete administrative evidence. The first-person evaluative presence is clear even though the sentence does not detour into autobiography.
