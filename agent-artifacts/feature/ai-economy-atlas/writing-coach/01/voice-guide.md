# Voice guide: feature/ai-economy-atlas (01)

## How this piece should sound

This feature reads the ATLAS's headline numbers — shares of work-related AI use
by occupation and region, and scientists' self-reports of daily use and hours
saved — for what each one is actually a measurement of. The register is precise,
confident and unshowy. It assumes a reader fluent in mathematics and computer
science, so it does not explain what a percentage or a denominator is; it spends
that room on what a particular percentage is a percentage of.

The three writers below share one habit: before asking whether a number is
right, they ask what quantity it is an estimate of. Lumley separates the figure
a poll produces from the slower-moving thing people actually want to know.
Ritchie compares rates over a denominator rather than totals, and states the
verdict the comparison earns. Chivers asks what a bare number is a count of, and
treats a statistic as a model of the world that is simpler than the world. What
transfers is how they read a number. The subjects belong to them: polls, power
plants, murder rates.

These are things the material may call for, not a checklist the article owes.

- **The target of inference.** A reported share of work-related AI use and a
  figure for hours saved each stand in for some quantity a reader cares about.
  Where the piece can name both the quantity a figure is taken to mean and the
  quantity the instrument actually produces, it can say which one a given number
  is. Lumley's poll passages are the model: the official target stated in its
  owners' words, then the quantity people are really reaching for.
- **What sits between the signal and the number.** A share depends on who was in
  the pool and how their use was detected — product telemetry, a classifier over
  conversations, a survey instrument. Where that is so, the piece can set those
  out one at a time, in their ordinary names, rather than fold them into a single
  hedging clause. Lumley lists the sources of error this way.
- **The denominator.** For a share or a count, the piece can ask which population
  and which pool the figure is over, and compare rates over that denominator
  rather than totals. Where the comparison makes one quantity plainly larger, it
  can say so without softening, the way Ritchie compares deaths per unit of
  electricity and then names the dirtiest source in four words.
- **The baseline.** Where a headline percentage turns on a choice — a start
  year, a reference group, which share is reported as the numerator — the piece
  can show the choice and what it did to the figure, as Chivers does with the
  year a percentage was measured from.
- **What a figure rests on, and uncertainty.** Where a number rests on a
  particular dataset or assumption, the piece can say what it rests on and which
  way that pushes it. Where a difference falls inside the uncertainty, it can
  decline to read the difference and give the reason. Ritchie does both.
- **Self-reports.** A self-reported number — hours a scientist says a tool saved
  them, how often someone says they use it — can be read as a measurement of a
  stated belief about the thing. Where the piece reaches one, it can say what the
  figure is a measure of rather than grant it the authority of the thing itself.
  Chivers's point that a statistic is a model of the world, and Lumley's split
  between the figure and its target, both carry here.

Where the data settles something, the article can say it without hedging; where
it does not reach, it can say that plainly, and state what went unmeasured. It
can give what the ATLAS set out to measure in its authors' terms before testing
any reading built on it.

## Thomas Lumley, "What are election polls trying to estimate? And is Stuff different?"

Source: https://www.statschat.org.nz/2017/07/30/what-are-election-polls-trying-to-estimate-and-is-stuff-different/

> "In thinking about whether this is a good idea, we'd need to first think about
> what the poll is trying to estimate and about the reasons it doesn't get that
> target quantity exactly right. Officially, polls are trying to estimate what
> would happen "if an election were held tomorrow", and there's no interest in
> prediction for dates further forward in time than that. If that were strictly
> true, no-one would care about polls, since the results would refer only to the
> past two weeks when the surveys were done."

Checked: https://www.statschat.org.nz/2017/07/30/what-are-election-polls-trying-to-estimate-and-is-stuff-different/, retrieved 2026-10-03
The move is to ask what the number is an estimate of before asking whether it is
right. Lumley gives the official target in the pollsters' own words, then says
plainly that if that were the real target no-one would care. You can see a
working statistician holding the quantity people quote apart from the quantity
they actually want.

> "A poll taken over a two-week period is potentially relevant because there's an
> underlying truth that, most of the time, changes more slowly than this. [...]
> In my view, that's the thing people are trying to estimate, and they're trying
> to estimate it because it has some medium-term predictive value."

Checked: https://www.statschat.org.nz/2017/07/30/what-are-election-polls-trying-to-estimate-and-is-stuff-different/, retrieved 2026-10-03
He names the thing the poll is really reaching for, a slower-moving underlying
truth, and marks it as his own judgement with "In my view". The figure and the
quantity it stands in for stay two different things on the page. (The bracketed
ellipsis drops one intervening sentence of examples.)

> "In addition to changes in the underlying truth, there is the idealised
> sampling variability that pollsters quote as the 'margin of error'. There's
> also larger sampling variability that comes because polling isn't
> mathematically perfect. And there are 'house effects', where polls from
> different companies have consistent differences in the medium to long term, and
> none of them perfectly match voting intentions as expressed at actual
> elections."

Checked: https://www.statschat.org.nz/2017/07/30/what-are-election-polls-trying-to-estimate-and-is-stuff-different/, retrieved 2026-10-03
He lists the things that sit between the number and the truth one at a time, in
order, and gives each its ordinary name. Nothing is inflated: "polling isn't
mathematically perfect" is as plain as it sounds, and the reader is trusted to
hold three distinct mechanisms at once.

## Hannah Ritchie and Max Roser, "What are the safest and cleanest sources of energy?"

Source: https://ourworldindata.org/safest-sources-of-energy

> "Let's look at this comparison in the chart. Fossil fuels and biomass kill many
> more people than nuclear and modern renewables per unit of electricity. Coal
> is, by far, the dirtiest."

Checked: https://ourworldindata.org/safest-sources-of-energy, retrieved 2026-10-03
Ritchie sets the comparison on the right footing, per unit of electricity rather
than total deaths, and then delivers the verdict in four words. "Coal is, by
far, the dirtiest" commits without a cushion, and the confidence sits on the
comparison she has just built.

> "Even then, these estimates for fossil fuels are likely to be very
> conservative. They are based on power plants in Europe, which have good
> pollution controls and are based on older models of the health impacts of air
> pollution."

Checked: https://ourworldindata.org/safest-sources-of-energy, retrieved 2026-10-03
She says what the estimate rests on, European plants and older air-pollution
models, and which way that bias runs. The qualification states a fact about the
measurement rather than advertising her own care.

> "People often focus on the marginal differences at the bottom of the chart —
> between nuclear, solar, and wind. This comparison is misguided: the
> uncertainties around these values mean they are likely to overlap."

Checked: https://ourworldindata.org/safest-sources-of-energy, retrieved 2026-10-03
She names a real reader habit, reading the small gaps at the bottom of the
chart, and refuses it, with the reason attached. "This comparison is misguided"
is a verdict she can defend from the uncertainty she has just described.

## Tom Chivers, "Statistically, you shouldn't believe the news"

Source: https://unherd.com/2019/09/statistically-you-shouldnt-believe-the-news/

> "There was a bit of a recent kerfuffle about suicides among girls and young
> women going up 83% since 2012; but simply looking at the ONS chart showed that
> the numbers were small, the data was noisy, and the only way you got the 83%
> figure was by choosing the lowest year on record."

Checked: https://unherd.com/2019/09/statistically-you-shouldnt-believe-the-news/, retrieved 2026-10-03
Chivers takes a specific headline figure and shows where it came from, the
lowest year on record, so the number's size becomes a fact about the baseline.
The informal "kerfuffle" keeps the register light while the point stays exact.

> "Another useful hack is to ask "is this a big number?" Lots of statistics are
> presented as bald numbers: "X people have died". But out of how many? If 25
> people have died of cancer out of a population of 50, that's awful; if 25
> people have died of cancer out of a population of 50 million, that's a
> statistical miracle."

Checked: https://unherd.com/2019/09/statistically-you-shouldnt-believe-the-news/, retrieved 2026-10-03
He asks the one question a bare number cannot answer, "out of how many?", and
answers it with the same count over two different denominators. The reader runs
the arithmetic alongside him, and he never stops to explain what a denominator
is.

> "The statistician George Box said: "All models are wrong, but some are useful."
> When you're reducing millions of people and events to a few numbers, you're
> building a model of the world, and that model must be simpler than the world,
> so it will be wrong in some way. You're always making assumptions and drawing
> arbitrary lines."

Checked: https://unherd.com/2019/09/statistically-you-shouldnt-believe-the-news/, retrieved 2026-10-03
He borrows Box's line and then says, in his own plain words, what it means for a
statistic: it is a model of the world, simpler than the world, and so wrong
somewhere. The writer is visible in the step from the quotation to "you're
building a model of the world".
