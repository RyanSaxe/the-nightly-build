# Editorial review: feature/thomson-reuters-ross-appeal (editor/01)

## Correct

Thesis: the Third Circuit's affirmance decides a narrow, fact-bound question and
not the "AI training is not fair use" line coverage drew from it; the loss turns
on necessity, the panel moved factor three without flagging it, and the
licensing-market finding is thin. Claims under it: (1) the software
intermediate-copying cases failed ROSS on necessity, not on any generative/
non-generative line; (2) factors 1, 3 and 4 ran against ROSS and 2 slightly for,
and factor 3 flipped from the district court; (3) factor-4 market harm rests on
three findings, the AI-licensing one supported by two sentences; (4) generative
AI is addressed only in footnote 7, which says the OpenAI concerns "do not apply
here" and does not reserve, decline or leave the question open.

I reopened the Third Circuit opinion (No. 25-2153, filed Sept. 29, 2026, the
canonical www2.ca3 PDF, byte-length matching the record) and read all 28 pages,
and reopened the district court memorandum (D.I. 770, Feb. 11, 2025) for the
comparison that is the article's own work. I tried to break each claim.

- Necessity, not generation. pp. 19-21 distinguish Sega, Sony v. Connectix and
  Google v. Oracle because "copying was necessary to access the unprotected
  functional aspects of computer code" and ROSS "does not need to copy" the
  headnotes, with no mention of generation anywhere in the passage; n.8 adds the
  Oracle declaring-code ground. The article states this and does not repeat the
  "cabined to non-generative AI" error. Held.
- Footnote 7. p. 17 n.7 uses none of the words reserve, decline or leave open; it
  says the concerns "do not apply here" on two stated grounds (no generated
  expression; built to substitute for Westlaw). The article reports exactly that
  and marks the "leaves the question unresolved" reading as the law firms'
  (Ballard Spahr), not the opinion's. Held.
- Factor votes and the factor-3 flip. Third Circuit: 1, 3, 4 against, 2 "slightly"
  for (pp. 21-23, 27-28). District court: "C. Factor three goes to Ross" and "now
  go a step further and decide factor three for Ross ... Ross wins this factor
  anyway" (D. Ct. pp. 20-21), with factors 1 and 4 to Thomson Reuters and the
  balancing "Factor two matters less than the others, and factor four matters
  more" (p. 23). The flip is real and the panel does not flag it. The comparison
  table's every cell and quoted fragment matches both opinions verbatim. Held.
- Thin licensing market. p. 26 gives the AI-training-licensing finding in two
  sentences (market "rapidly developing"; Thomson Reuters's own internal use),
  with no license, price or figure. The article reports the thinness without
  inflating it. Held.
- The 25,000 vs 2,243 figure. p. 22 says ROSS copied "the entire text of the
  25,000 Westlaw-written headnotes," while liability covers 2,243 (p. 7 n.3) and
  0.08% of 28 million is 22,400; 25,000 is also the memo count (p. 5). The article
  reports the inconsistency as unreconciled and asserts no total beyond 2,243.
  Held.

Headline, dek and subheads: court, docket (No. 25-2153), date (filed Sept. 29,
2026), parties, posture (§ 1292(b) interlocutory affirmance of partial summary
judgment), panel (Restrepo, Montgomery-Reeves, Bove; opinion by
Montgomery-Reeves) and every factor outcome check against the opinion. I opened
every citation href as printed; all nine resolve and the full proof with links
returns no link warning. Every data-nb-kind holds: the opinion and the district
memorandum are primary (each court authored its own), the amicus brief is primary
for what the amici argued, and the six news and firm pages are secondary.

Fairness to ROSS: its briefs were gated, and the article says so. ROSS's position
is carried through the opinion's recounting and the CCIA/Chamber of Progress/
NetChoice amicus, stated "on this view" before it is answered, and ROSS is never
quoted directly. The strongest outside criticism (Hansen, Authors Alliance) is
attributed and its forecast marked as his.

One break worth recording, not a correctness defect: the orientation describes
the first certified question as whether the headnotes "are original enough to be
copyrighted," dropping the Key Number System that shared that certified question.
The opinion treats the Key Number System as forfeited and decides nothing about
it (p. 7 n.4), so the simplification describes the live component accurately and
misstates nothing; I left it.

## Reads well

I cut one sentence that said nothing. "That fact pattern sets up the argument ROSS
made, and the reason the court rejected it" was a signpost at a section edge: it
reduces to "that X sets up Y and Z," states no finding, and only announces that an
argument is coming. The section now ends on the concrete ads-and-prices sentence.

I broke a three-fold anaphora in the closing section. Three consecutive sentences
opened "It does not establish / It does not say / It does not say"; the ledger of
what the opinion leaves undecided is the piece's own work, but three identical
openings is cadence under the slop rules. I merged the second and third into one
sentence with "or," which keeps two parallel openings carrying the argument and
loses none of the content.

I did not find borrowed phrasing from the briefing or the voice guide's quoted
passages. The piece takes the voice guide's register (the reader's report that
separates held from said and states each limit as a fact about the opinion) and
runs flat nowhere that needed lifting; the "on this view" framing, the firm scope
statements and the one concrete thread (the free opinions as the operative fact)
are the article's own. Headline and dek do not repeat the recent record's molds:
the dek is a compound predicate, not the banned comma triad or semicolon reversal,
and no heading reuses the "What the X are X of" or "The gap between A and B" shapes
from the recent features.

## The experience

The rendered page reads top to bottom as a single argument. The rs-docket box
carries the standing case facts so the prose argues rather than recites, and the
factor table earns its place: it shows the one real change between the courts
(factor three) faster than a paragraph could, and the prose around it says what
the grid cannot (that the same necessity fact now carries factors one and three,
and that a "factor by factor" description of the affirmance would be wrong). No
chart is warranted; the opinion carries no figures and the one comparison is
categorical.

What the piece gives beyond its sources: it reads the opinion against the district
memorandum and against the coverage, and returns three things none of them states
together: that the loss turns on necessity rather than on any line about
generation, that the panel reversed the district court on factor three without
saying so, and that the opinion's only reach toward generative AI is a footnote
that sets those concerns aside without reserving the question. That matches the
writer's original-work sentence, and it survives the read.
