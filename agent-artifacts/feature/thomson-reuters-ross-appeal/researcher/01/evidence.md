# Evidence record: feature/thomson-reuters-ross-appeal (researcher 01)

The Third Circuit's opinion is in hand and read in full (32 PDF pages, 28 of opinion), so every holding and figure below is checked against it. It holds, unanimously and without separate writing, that 2,243 Westlaw headnotes are original enough to copyright and that ROSS's use of them was not fair use. Factors one, three and four went against ROSS and factor two slightly for it. The opinion does not say what the circulating summary says. It never states a rule about "AI training" in general. Its one reference to generative AI is footnote 7 (p. 17), which says the concerns in a pending Southern District of New York case "do not apply here" because ROSS's system "cannot generate original expression". It does not use the words reserve, decline or leave open. It also does not confine the Sega, Sony and Google v. Oracle line to non-generative systems. It distinguishes those cases on necessity, with no mention of generation. Two commission statements need correcting: the opinion was filed September 29, 2026, not September 30, and the posture is an affirmance of partial summary judgment on a question certified under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b). The record is thin on ROSS's own briefs (not obtained), on the DOJ statement the opinion cites, and on how courts will treat the opinion afterward, which is not yet knowable.

Buena Vista (3d Cir. 2003) directs attention to "the effect of the use upon the ... value of the copyrighted work"; headnotes draw users to a Westlaw subscription and ROSS "diminished their value as a draw" (pp. 24-25). ## Contradictions

1. The commission says the opinion was issued September 30, 2026. The opinion prints "Filed: Sept. 29, 2026" (p. 1). Coverage dates the news September 30 because the opinion was sealed on the 29th and unsealed after. Any date in a headline, dek or docket furniture is Sept 29, 2026.
2. The summary "AI training is not fair use" versus the opinion. The opinion decides a fact-bound question: whether ROSS's use of Westlaw headnotes to train a non-generative legal search tool that competes with Westlaw was fair. It never states a general rule about training. Its own framing is "no more than an ordinary copyright case" (p. 2). Sources that carry the stronger version: the MBW headline ("rejects 'fair use' defense over AI training"), the RIAA/NMPA brief ("can never be fair use", a party's argument written before the opinion), and the Reuters "first-of-its-kind ruling ... over AI training" framing. A headline seen in a search result and not opened: Jenner & Block's 2025 alert on the district court ("Court decides that use of copyrighted works in AI training is not fair use"), which describes the district court. Sources that carry the narrow version: footnote 7, MBW's own last paragraphs, Ballard Spahr, MSK, Plagiarism Today, LawNext, Patently-O.
3. The commission says the opinion "confines its holding and the intermediate-copying line of software cases (Sega, Sony, Google v. Oracle) to" the non-generative situation. The opinion does not. It distinguishes those cases because "copying was necessary to access the unprotected functional aspects of computer code" and ROSS "does not need to copy" (p. 20), and in n.8 because Oracle involved declaring code. The non-generative fact appears at pp. 5-6 and in n.7 (p. 17), and neither is tied to the intermediate-copying discussion at pp. 19-21.
4. The commission asks whether the opinion "expressly reserves" the generative question. It does not use "reserve", "decline" or "leave open" for generative AI. (It does use that kind of language twice elsewhere: p. 10 n.5 on verbatim headnotes, and p. 7 n.4 on the forfeited Key Number System.) Footnote 7 says the DOJ's generative-AI concerns "do not apply here" and gives two grounds. Secondary alerts call this a carve-out or "set aside"; that is their characterization.
5. The "ROSS's licensing market" point. The commission treats the licensing-market analysis as a main spine item. In the opinion it is the third of three market-harm findings in factor 4, supported by two sentences of evidence (a market "rapidly developing"; Thomson Reuters using headnotes for its own AI search products). The district court put weight on the same market but also said it "does not matter whether Thomson Reuters has used the data to train its own legal search tools". The Third Circuit instead cites Thomson Reuters's own training use as evidence the market exists. The district court (not the Third Circuit) mentions that Thomson Reuters refused to license ROSS (D. Ct. p. 3).
6. The factor-3 vote changed on appeal. District court: factor 3 went to ROSS (D. Ct. pp. 20-21). Third Circuit: factor 3 goes against ROSS (pp. 22-23). The opinion says "we will AFFIRM" and does not flag factor 3 as a departure. Coverage that says the panel "affirmed the district court's four-factor analysis" would be wrong; I did not see that claim made, but it is a trap.
7. "First appellate ruling" claims. Reuters, the amicus briefs and several alerts call this the first federal appellate ruling on fair use in AI training. The opinion makes no such claim. I did not find an earlier circuit opinion on AI-training fair use and did not search for one beyond these sources, so the claim is reporting, not established.
8. The opinion says "ROSS copied the entire text of the 25,000 Westlaw-written headnotes into its memos" (p. 22), while the liability holding covers 2,243 headnotes (p. 7 and n.3) and the facts say LegalEase wrote about 25,000 memos from "thousands of Westlaw headnotes" (pp. 5-6). ROSS's 0.08% of 28 million headnotes (p. 23) implies roughly 22,400. The opinion's "25,000 headnotes" is probably the memo count carried into the factor-3 paragraph. I cannot resolve this from the opinion; do not state a headnote total beyond 2,243 without attribution.

## Limits

- ROSS's own opening and reply briefs were not obtained. ROSS's position is stated through the opinion's recounting and one amicus brief (CCIA, Chamber of Progress, NetChoice). The piece cannot quote ROSS in its own words.
- The opinion is not final in the sense that matters for a headline: it is "precedential" per MBW and the filename only. The version read is the one on the court's host. If the court redacted parts before unsealing, I could not tell.
- The opinion does not say whether the licensing market for AI training data would be recognized for generative models. It makes a finding about headnotes only and cites no license, price or contract.
- The opinion does not reserve the generative question in so many words. If the article says "expressly reserves", that is the writer's characterization of footnote 7, and I recommend quoting "do not apply here" instead.
- Nothing in the opinion says whether the intermediate-copying cases would come out differently for a generative system.
- The DOJ statement of interest in In re OpenAI that footnote 7 discusses was not opened. What it argued is known only through the opinion's description.
- The remaining trial issues in Delaware (copyright expiry, other headnotes, damages) come from Patently-O only.
- The § 1292(b) grant date (June 17, 2025) and the transfer to No. 25-2153 (June 24, 2025) come from a tracker and a search snippet; Justia's docket page returned 403 and CourtListener's API was rate limited. The district court's certification order (App. 61) was not opened.
- Whether the Third Circuit decision has been appealed (en banc petition, certiorari) is unknown as of Oct 4, 2026. Nothing I read says.
- The "first appellate ruling" claim is attributed, not verified. I did not survey other circuits.
- Reporter citations for the district opinion (765 F. Supp. 3d 382) come from the amicus brief; page 382 as the first page was not checked.
- Secondary pages were read through a page-extraction tool, so their wording here is paraphrase and nothing from them should be quoted without opening them again.
