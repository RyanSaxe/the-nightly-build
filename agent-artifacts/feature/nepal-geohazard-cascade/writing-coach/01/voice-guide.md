# Voice guide: Langtang Lirung/Rasuwa flood cascade

## Reporting stance

Write as a careful investigator of a physical sequence. The article’s distinctive work is to let the reader trace how one high-mountain collapse propagated into downstream floods, while keeping the evidence for each link and the gaps between links visible. Treat the September 17 World Weather Attribution analysis as a starting claim to examine, not the article’s frame or conclusion.

Keep three causal layers distinct throughout: the initiating event; conditions that may have made slopes, ice, or terrain less stable; and the downstream chain of impacts. Name the evidence that supports each layer. Do not compress them into a single climate attribution. State plainly where the record cannot identify a trigger or quantify climate’s contribution.

## Voice

- Be composed, concrete, and technically literate. Assume strong quantitative readers, but define mountain-hazard terms at first use.
- Prefer physical nouns, named places, dates, measured quantities, and accountable actors over abstractions.
- Give evidence its limits in the same passage as the claim. Avoid both false certainty and ritual hedging.
- Attribute mortality and missing-person figures to a named source and its dated reporting snapshot. Keep categories and dates separate when sources disagree; never add deaths and missing people into a single total without making the categories explicit.
- Describe the WWA counterfactual precisely: distinguish what its analysis estimates from what observations establish, and do not imply it proves the event’s sole cause.
- Link every consequential factual claim to the source that supports it. Favor original scientific, government, hydrology, and response records; use independent reporting to test context and consequences. Do not invent interviews, field observations, quotations, or community testimony.

## Structure and pace

First settle the article’s one-sentence original contribution from the reporting. Then order the piece so the reader can follow the event from collapse through downstream consequences, understand which conditions may have shaped it, and assess what warning and adaptation can realistically cover. Let that evidence determine section breaks and headings; do not force a standard disaster explainer outline.

Open with a documented, specific fact that makes the event legible to someone arriving from a link. Establish why the August 26 event merits attention now without overstating novelty. Move from chronology to causal interpretation only after the event sequence is clear. Put uncertainty beside the disputed link, not in a detached caveat section. End on a concrete limit or capability established by the reporting, not a generic lesson or appeal.

Use short paragraphs for the sequence and its evidence. Slow down only when explaining a mechanism, counterfactual, or warning-system constraint. A compact timeline or causal diagram may help if it makes the chain and its confidence levels easier to inspect; do not add furniture for decoration.

## Avoid

- The single-storm explanation: the commission explicitly rules out attributing the catastrophe to one rainfall event.
- Treating warming as the sole demonstrated cause, or treating a plausible conditioning factor as a proven trigger.
- Repeating a press release’s framing, headline, or conclusions without an independent evidentiary test.
- Turning the story into a general climate-change essay or a generic “lessons learned” piece.
- Mixing confirmed deaths, missing people, affected populations, and estimates; hiding the source date for a changing toll.
- Vague phrases such as “experts say,” unsupported superlatives, abstract agency, and claims about what communities experienced without a source.
- Stock disaster language, dramatic scene-setting unsupported by firsthand reporting, rhetorical questions, decorative metaphors, and a moralizing close.
- Headline/dek templates, generic section labels, repeated heading rhythms, em-dash-heavy prose, and sentences that would fit any hazard story.
