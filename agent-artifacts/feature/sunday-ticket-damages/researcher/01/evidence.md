# Evidence record: feature/sunday-ticket-damages (researcher 01)

**Verdict on the angle: supported, with two corrections.** The Ninth Circuit's
published opinion of 30 October 2026 (No. 24-5493, opinion by Judge Johnstone,
no dissent or concurrence) reverses the exclusion of both plaintiffs' economists
and the judgment as a matter of law for the NFL, affirms the conditional order
for a new trial on damages, and remands to reinstate the liability verdict and
retry damages (opinion pp. 7, 12, 32). It says in its own words that the $191.26
per-subscriber figure "reflected a discount, not an overcharge" (p. 32). So
"discount, not overcharge" is the court's holding and not an inference. The
corrections are these. (1) The discount reading began with the NFL's
post-trial motion and the district court's August 2024 order (Dkt. 1513 pp.
13–16). The appeals court adopted it. The jury never stated its method: the
opinion says the awards are "almost certainly" class size times $191.26 (p.
30). The residential award matches to the cent. The commercial award is 2 cents
off (506,788 × $191.26 = $96,928,272.88; the verdict form says $96,928,272.90).
(2) The plaintiffs offered a rival reading of the same arithmetic at the
district court ($191.26 as the price paid, $0 as the competitive price). The
district court rejected it, and the Ninth Circuit does not mention it. All the
scout's figures check out against the opinion and are listed with pages below.
**Where the evidence is thin:** no statement from plaintiffs' counsel on the
ruling turned up, no rehearing petition had been reported by 5 November, no
expert report could be opened, and the opinion gives the models' effects as
percentages and totals, never as a modelled competitive price per subscription.
Any per-subscription "competitive price" in a figure would be derived, and the
denominators do not line up cleanly (see Contradictions 3 and 4).

Holdings, issue by issue:

- **Rule 702, Rascher (pp. 22–27).** Excluding Dr. Rascher was an abuse of
  discretion. The yardstick method is reliable (p. 25). Whether college football
  is comparable enough "presents a question of fact for the jury" (pp. 25–26,
  quoting Image Tech.). The district court faulted him for an analytical "gap
  . . . of [its own] making" (p. 26). "Dr. Rascher's testimony was admissible
  under Rule 702" (p. 27).
- **Rule 702, Zona direct-to-consumer model (pp. 27–29).** The exclusion was an
  abuse of discretion "by failing to apply the correct legal rule". The district
  court "erroneously weighed Dr. Zona's conclusion" (pp. 27–28). The evidence of
  a feasible competitor was sufficient: Zona's testimony about a "Netflix-like
  service", and an NFL study of putting all out-of-market games on channels
  already in DirecTV packages (p. 28). The "irrational" consumer behaviour the
  district court flagged "was an economic feature, not a bug" of discrete-choice
  modelling (p. 28).
- **Rule 702, Zona "NFL Tax" model (p. 29).** It was an abuse of discretion to
  exclude it "without discussion". The district court's criticisms of the
  direct-to-consumer model "do not apply" to it.
- **Judgment as a matter of law (pp. 29–30).** It rested on the exclusions, so
  it is reversed.
- **New trial on damages (pp. 30–32).** Affirmed. "The district court acted
  within its discretion when it inferred how the jury arrived at its award"
  (p. 31). The jury was instructed to find an overcharge (p. 31, quoting the
  instruction), "But here, the jury awarded damages based on the difference
  between the residential list price and the lower estimated actual price paid
  by a group that included the Subscribers" (p. 32).
- **Disposition (p. 32):** "We reverse the district court's exclusion of Dr.
  Rascher's and Dr. Zona's expert testimony and the resulting grant of judgment
  as a matter of law for the NFL. We affirm the district court's order granting
  a new trial on damages. We remand this case for the district court to
  reinstate the jury's liability verdict and hold a new damages trial."
  "REVERSED IN PART, AFFIRMED IN PART, AND REMANDED."
- **Footnote 1 (p. 18)** points to the memorandum disposition (source 2) on the
  Clayton Act §16 claims and class certification.

The models, as the court records them:

- Rascher, "an economist and professor at the University of San Francisco"
  (p. 22). His yardstick is "the market for telecasts of games between the Top
  25 college football teams" (p. 23), before and after *NCAA v. Board of
  Regents* (1984) (pp. 23–24). In his but-for world, out-of-market games appear
  on over-the-air and basic cable channels, "so customers don't pay anything
  extra above what they were already paying for their TV package" (p. 24).
- Zona, "a practicing economist" (p. 27). Direct-to-consumer model: "if the NFL
  granted telecast rights to one additional competitor, the price of DirecTV
  Sunday Ticket would drop by 49.7%, and would decrease further with each
  additional competitor". Using a single extra competitor, exclusivity "cost
  residential class members $2.81 billion and commercial class members $670
  million" (p. 27).
- Zona "NFL Tax" model: the theory is that the NFL conspired with CBS and Fox to
  price Sunday Ticket at a premium to protect network viewership. "DirecTV's
  price for Sunday Ticket would have been 24.6% lower if the company were
  allowed to set its profit-maximizing price" (p. 29). The opinion gives no
  dollar total for this model.

Background the opinion states: the NFL is 32 teams; 46 of the top 50 TV
programmes in 2021 were NFL games; the class period runs June 2011 to February
2023, and the NFL earned "more than $100 billion" in it (p. 12). Residential
list prices "ranged from $200 to nearly $400 per season", discounted by
promotions. Commercial subscribers paid "from thousands to over one hundred
thousand dollars per season" (p. 15). The jury awarded "$4.6 billion to the
residential class and $96 million to the commercial class" (p. 17, rounded).
The exact figures are on p. 30. Procedural history: suit filed 2015 (p. 15);
dismissal reversed in 2019, 933 F.3d 1136; cert denied, 592 U.S. 1081 (2020)
(p. 16); two classes certified under Rule 23(b)(3) (p. 16).

The court's account of the SBA (pp. 13–14): after the 1961 injunction in
*United States v. NFL*, Congress enacted the SBA, 15 U.S.C. §1291, which "permits
the NFL and other professional sports leagues to jointly sell their rights in
the 'sponsored telecasting' of their games, exempting those agreements from
Section 1". The Ninth Circuit has construed "sponsored telecasting" to mean free
over-the-air broadcasts supported by advertising. The SBA "does not exempt
league contracts with cable or satellite television services, for which
subscribers are charged a fee" (p. 13, quoting the 2019 decision). The CBS/Fox
in-market broadcasts: "The parties agree that these broadcasts were
SBA-protected sponsored telecasts" (p. 14).

## Contradictions

1. **The jury's method is inferred, and there is a rival reading.** The courts
   inferred it from the to-the-cent figures, and nobody on the jury explained it.
   The Ninth Circuit says "almost certainly" (p. 30). The plaintiffs argued in
   the district court that the jury may have taken $191.26 as the price paid and
   $0 as the competitive price, which is Rascher's world (Dkt. 1513 p. 14 n.8).
   The district court rejected that too: it still treats a discount as the price
   paid (p. 16 n.10). The Ninth Circuit opinion does not mention the alternative.
   Both readings use the same two inputs, so the "discount" label is the courts'
   characterisation of inputs that both sides agree were used.
2. **The commercial award is 2 cents off the formula.** 506,788 × $191.26 =
   $96,928,272.88. The verdict form says $96,928,272.90. The district court's own
   table prints .88 against a verdict of .90 (Dkt. 1513 p. 14 vs p. 2). The
   residential award matches exactly ($4,610,331,671.74). Do not write that both
   awards equal the product "exactly".
3. **"Subscribers" or "subscriptions".** The Ninth Circuit says Sunday Ticket
   "served 24,105,049 residential subscribers and 506,788 commercial subscribers"
   (p. 15) and calls them "class members" (p. 30). The district court calls the
   same numbers "Sunday Ticket subscriptions during the class period" (Dkt. 1513
   p. 13; the table header reads "Number of Subscriptions"). Over an 11-season
   class period the count looks like subscription-seasons, not distinct people,
   but no source says so. Safest wording: "24,105,049 residential subscriptions
   over the class period", attributed to the district court, or quote the Ninth
   Circuit's word with its page.
4. **Whose $102.74, and over what group.** The Ninth Circuit calls it "Dr. Zona's
   disputed estimate of the average price paid by Sunday Ticket residential
   subscribers" (pp. 30–31) and "the lower estimated actual price paid by a group
   that included the Subscribers" (p. 32). The district court calls it Zona's
   calculation but cites Dr. Yurukoglu's testimony for it (p. 14), and describes
   it as "the average price paid by all residential DirecTV subscribers who
   received Sunday Ticket" (p. 16). Rascher's totals imply a much higher average
   for the class: about $5.6 billion over 24,105,049 residential subscriptions is
   about $232 each (my division of rounded figures). That suggests the $102.74
   group is wider than paying class purchasers, for example promotional
   recipients, but no document I read says so.
5. **The residential award sits inside the experts' range. The commercial award
   does not.** Residential: $4.61 billion lies between Zona's one-competitor
   $2.81 billion and Rascher's roughly $5.6 billion. Commercial: $96.9 million is
   far below both $670 million and roughly $1.3 billion. The plaintiffs argued
   that an award "within the range" supported by the evidence "cannot be set
   aside" (Dkt. 1513 p. 14). The district court answered that a verdict must also
   "find substantial support in the record" (p. 14), and the Ninth Circuit cites
   the same standard (p. 31). This is the plaintiffs' strongest point and the
   piece should state it in their terms before explaining why both courts
   rejected it.
6. **Remedy.** The district court would have remitted to nominal damages and
   granted a new trial (Dkt. 1513 p. 12). The Ninth Circuit affirmed only "the
   district court's order granting a new trial on damages" (p. 32). It said
   nothing about remittitur. The new jury is not bound to any figure.
7. **Rounded figures in the opinion.** P. 17 gives "$4.6 billion" and "$96
   million". P. 30 gives the exact amounts. Press reports variously say $4.6, $4.7
   and $4.8 billion. The record total is $4,707,259,944.64.
8. **The SBA, two readings.** The Ninth Circuit: the SBA covers free,
   advertiser-supported broadcasts and not pay services (p. 13). The NFL (2020):
   even outside the exemption's letter, Congress's "express sanction of the
   League's essential role" should weigh, and the Ninth Circuit turned "a shield
   . . . as a sword" (cert petition printed p. 25). Justice Kavanaugh's 2020
   statement leans toward the NFL on joint-venture analysis and *Illinois Brick*.
   Neither question is decided in the 2026 opinion, which "concerns only" injury
   and damages (p. 19).
9. **The commission's framing.** Nothing in the opinion undercuts "the jury
   measured a discount, not an overcharge". One point of precision: the court
   does not say $191.26 measures "discounting" in general. It says it is "the
   money the Subscribers may have saved because DirecTV sold Sunday Ticket to
   them below the list price" (p. 32).
