# Voice guide — company-analysis/apple (01)

## Directive

Register: first-principles and unhurried, for a reader who already thinks in
ratios, rates of change, and mix-shift — a math/CS, ML-eng reader. Do not
explain like the reader is new to quantitative reasoning; do explain every
Apple-specific mechanic, once, in the sentence that needs it. Write the piece
the way you'd walk a sharp colleague through a model you built, not the way
you'd pitch them a stock.

Moves that change sentences in this piece:

- **Weave, don't front-load.** Introduce a segment, a margin mechanic, or a
  term (gross margin, mix shift, services attach) at the exact point the
  argument needs it, then keep moving. Before keeping a paragraph, ask
  whether deleting it leaves a gap in the reasoning. If not, cut it or move
  the sentence that does the work into the paragraph that needed it.
- **Give every number a comparison the reader already holds.** Not "gross
  margin was 50.1%" alone — margin against the prior quarter, against the
  segment's own trend, against what the one-time item added. A bare
  percentage is a fact without a frame.
- **Separate one-time from recurring physically, not just in meaning.** The
  tariff refund gets its own clause or sentence, never folded inside the
  headline growth or margin number it inflated. A reader should be able to
  find the sentence that removes it and get the underlying figure.
- **Keep fact, estimate, and synthesis in different grammatical registers.**
  Reported fact takes a plain past tense tied to the filing ("Apple
  reported..."). An estimate is named as one and attributed ("analysts had
  modeled...", "consensus was..."). Synthesis is marked as reasoning, not
  disguised as another data point ("that gap implies...", "the deceleration
  argues..."). The tariff-refund line and the Services miss are the test:
  each fact, each estimate, and the conclusion drawn from comparing them,
  should be visibly different kinds of sentences.
- **Chart carries what prose can't draw; prose carries what the chart can't
  argue.** Put a chart where a trend or a segment comparison would otherwise
  cost three sentences and a table. The caption states what's plotted and
  its source line, nothing interpretive. Never let prose narrate the chart's
  shape ("as the chart shows") — argue from the number, not from the image
  of the number. Never let the chart repeat a comparison the prose already
  made.
- **Commit to a mechanism, not a call.** Answer the market question the
  piece opened with, on the terms the evidence supports, but the sentence
  that closes the argument describes what is true of the business, not what
  a reader should do about it. No instruction, no coy non-answer standing in
  for one either ("time will tell", "investors will be watching").

Recently used, do not reuse: the "the cash came from X, not Y" reveal
structure (Boeing); turning this into an AI-capex story (Alphabet); a
colon-subtitle headline; a heading cadence built from two clauses joined by a
comma and "and"; a dek built as a semicolon reversal or a suspended question.

## Exemplars

## Ben Thompson, "The Services iPhone"
Source: https://stratechery.com/2022/the-services-iphone/
Craft:
- cadence: short declaratives that build, then one turn reframes the whole
  argument in two sentences ("This doesn't make much sense... It does,
  though, make sense for...").
- argument: reads Apple's pricing and product decisions backward from the
  metric they optimize for, then uses that lens to explain moves that look
  irrational under the old frame.
- evidence: itemizes a customer's total hardware spend in dollars before
  pivoting to per-user services revenue, so the segment mix assembles
  bottom-up instead of arriving as a stated pie chart.
- stance: unhurried, first-principles; commits to a reading of strategy
  without softening it into a hedge.
- notice: catches that a pricing decision reads as irrational only if you
  keep grading Apple as a hardware company after it stopped being one.
- diction: plain nouns, no finance jargon beyond what the argument needs;
  each term earns its place at first use.
- reader: a builder who thinks in incentives and systems, not a retail
  investor being coached.
- the important move: explaining the business and making the argument happen
  in the same sentence — the mechanic surfaces exactly where the case needs
  it, never staged ahead of it in a separate section.
Calibration: "they're making upwards of $2000 — $1,000+ for a top-of-the-line
iPhone, $400+ for a Watch, $200+ for AirPods"

## Aswath Damodaran, "Investing Whiplash: Looking for Closure with Apple and Amazon!"
Source: https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2018/12/investing-whiplash-looking-for-closure.html
Craft:
- cadence: conversational but exact; a long sentence carries the
  qualifications, a short one lands the number.
- argument: separates what changed in the underlying story from what changed
  in the price, then asks whether the gap between the two grew or shrank.
- evidence: keeps a visible ledger of his own prior estimate against the
  current one, so the reader can audit the revision, not just the
  conclusion.
- stance: names his own position and its motive without letting the
  admission become the point of the paragraph.
- notice: treats the distance between value and price as the actual object
  under study, not the price on its own.
- diction: everyday words for the mechanics; jargon appears only inside a
  named, defined estimate.
- reader: someone willing to sit with an estimate long enough to watch it
  get revised in public.
- the important move: an estimate is written with a visible history, so a
  revision reads as discipline, not as the writer having been wrong.
Calibration: "Apple's value has declined from $201 to $196, but the stock
price has dropped from $220 to $179, shifting it from being overvalued by
9.54% to undervalued by 9.14%."

## Ben Carlson, "Earnings vs. the Stock Market"
Source: https://awealthofcommonsense.com/2026/01/earnings-vs-the-stock-market/
Craft:
- cadence: short paired sentences — a mechanism stated, then its exception,
  in immediate succession.
- argument: builds a rule from a count of past instances before showing
  where the rule breaks, so the exception reads as earned rather than
  asserted.
- evidence: counts occurrences instead of characterizing a pattern in the
  abstract ("24 instances," not "often").
- stance: comfortable naming when a clean story is available, and separately
  when it usually isn't, without smoothing the two into one hedge.
- notice: catches the lag between when a number is reported and when the
  market has already priced it.
- diction: no adjective stands in for a number; a claim gets a count or it
  doesn't get made.
- reader: someone who wants the mechanism stated once, with no pitch
  attached.
- the important move: a summary sentence restates the finding in plainer
  terms than the data that produced it, so the takeaway never outruns the
  evidence behind it.
Calibration: "Earnings are reported on a lag. The market is forward-looking."
