# Voice guide: company-analysis/spacex (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a chart-forward analysis that values SpaceX by taking the company apart
into its businesses and reasoning about each one. The reader can follow a chart
and a compounding argument but does not carry finance vocabulary by default, so
the register that fits is the calm, plain-sentence baseline the house already
sets: the numbers ride inside ordinary sentences, and the structure of the
argument does the persuading rather than the vocabulary. Warren Buffett writing
to Berkshire's shareholders is the closest model. He hands a reader the three
plain questions an analysis exists to answer without once sounding like a finance
professional, and the writer should be able to move a figure into the argument
the same unforced way.

A company this famous pulls prose toward hype, and the exemplars show the
opposite pull. Howard Marks starts from the flat first principle that a good
asset bought at too high a price is a bad investment, and prices his own car
before he prices a security. The material here may call for treating SpaceX as
one more set of businesses to be priced on their cash flows, so that any verdict
the piece reaches is earned from the segment arithmetic and not from the name on
the rocket. Where the reasoning leans on a business continuing to grow the way it
has, Buffett's warning against judging a company by its lifetime record and
Marks's account of how price swings overshoot the swings in fundamentals both
show a numerate writer refusing the easy extrapolation; the segment work may put
that question directly in front of you.

The piece is numerate, and the exemplars are most useful on how a numerate writer
handles what the numbers cannot pin down. Aswath Damodaran names the belief that
putting a number on a risk makes the risk go away, and Buffett, appraising a
disputed timber claim, values it at "somewhere between zero and a whole lot" and
moves on. A segment decomposition runs on estimates, and some segments rest on
things nobody can yet know; where that is true, the material may call for stating
the range and saying plainly what is unknown, rather than reporting a single
figure the evidence does not support. Damodaran's five steps from a business's
story to a value, and his warning about the business "that can exist only in
spreadsheet nirvana," show a valuation held together by a plausible account of
the company rather than by the mechanics of a model; each segment's economics can
be built up before the number that depends on it, so a reader sees why the figure
is what it is.

Judgment is welcome and can show a person on the page without inflating anything.
Buffett's dry self-deprecation and Marks's plain analogies both carry a clear
point of view in calm words. The strongest of these writers are also willing to
be dull where the subject is dull, spending a paragraph on an accounting change
or a deal's terms because the argument needs it; this analysis can take that
patience where a segment's economics require careful setup, and spend its
sharpest sentences where it has actually found something.

## Warren Buffett, "Chairman's Letter - 1988"

Source: https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1988.html

> "Berkshire's past rates of gain in both book value and business value were achieved under circumstances far different from those that now exist. Anyone ignoring these differences makes the same mistake that a baseball manager would were he to judge the future prospects of a 42-year-old center fielder on the basis of his lifetime batting average."

Buffett warns against extrapolating a company's past record with a single
concrete image an ordinary reader already understands, and the image carries the
statistical point without a statistical word. The person shows in the choice of
the baseball manager and the aging center fielder: this is someone who reaches
for the plainest available comparison rather than the most technical one.

> "What needs to be reported is data - whether GAAP, non-GAAP, or extra-GAAP - that helps financially-literate readers answer three key questions: (1) Approximately how much is this company worth? (2) What is the likelihood that it can meet its future obligations? and (3) How good a job are its managers doing, given the hand they have been dealt?"

He reduces the entire purpose of financial reporting to three questions a reader
can hold in their head, each in short plain words. The confidence to say
"approximately how much is this company worth" instead of dressing valuation up
as a specialist's art is where Buffett is visible, and it shows how a numerate
argument can stay legible to a reader who is not a specialist.

> "Finally, we had to ask ourselves what the redwood claim might be worth. Your Chairman, who can't tell an elm from an oak, had no trouble with that one: He coolly evaluated the claim at somewhere between zero and a whole lot."

Facing a genuinely unknowable figure, Buffett gives its honest range and makes a
joke of his own inability to narrow it, and the honesty and the humor are the
same move. The self-deprecation ("who can't tell an elm from an oak") is the
person on the page, and it shows a numerate writer stating an uncertainty plainly
instead of inventing a precise number to cover it.

## Aswath Damodaran, "Numbers and Narrative: Modeling, Story Telling and Investing"

Source: https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/06/numbers-and-narrative-modeling-story.html

> "For better or worse, we seem to feel better about uncertain outcomes, if we can attach numbers (expected values, risk adjusted discount rates) to them. That, by itself, is healthy but what is unhealthy is the belief that quantifying risk somehow makes it dissipate."

A valuation professor names the trap of his own trade in flat language: a number
on a risk does not remove the risk. Damodaran shows in his even, first-person
sentences ("we seem to feel better") that a deeply numerate writer can be the one
warning against over-trusting the numbers, which is the stance a chart-forward
piece needs to keep its own figures honest.

> "Second, if a valuation is built around line items and individual inputs, there is a strong possibility that you may be creating a business that can exist only in spreadsheet nirvana, where revenues double every year, margins expand without challenge and growth comes without significant reinvestment."

He shows what an ungrounded model looks like by describing the impossible company
it implies, in specifics a reader can check against the real world: revenues that
double, margins that only expand, growth that costs nothing. The phrase
"spreadsheet nirvana" is characteristic Damodaran, a working analyst's dry name
for a familiar failure, and it demonstrates tying every number back to something
that could actually happen to a business.

> "To me, fair value accounting estimates are like microwave frozen dinners, quick and convenient, but you will never mistake them for the real thing."

Damodaran dispatches a technical accounting question with a household comparison
and a clear verdict, so a reader who does not know the accounting still knows
exactly what he thinks of it. The everyday image and the willingness to commit to
a plain opinion are both him, and together they show how to make a numerate
judgment reach a non-specialist reader.

## Howard Marks, "The Most Important Thing" (Oaktree memo, July 1, 2003)

Source: https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2003-07-01-the-most-important-thing.pdf

> "It's only attractive if it's priced right. Hopefully, if I offered to sell you my car, you'd ask the price before saying yes or no. Deciding on an investment without carefully considering the fairness of its price is just as silly."

Marks builds his whole first principle out of an everyday transaction anyone has
done: you ask the price of a car before buying it. The reasoning is airtight and
uses no finance term at all, and the slightly wry "just as silly" is the person,
a writer confident enough to make a serious point sound obvious.

> "Thus price swings usually overstate the swings in fundamentals. When developments are positive and corporate profits are high, investors feel good and often bid assets to prices that more than reflect their intrinsic value. When developments are negative, on the other hand, panicky investors are prone to sell them down to overly cheap levels."

He explains a mechanism step by step, each sentence adding one link, so a reader
watches prices detach from fundamentals rather than being told they do. The
patient, symmetrical construction is Marks's, and it shows how to teach a
quantitative idea by walking through it in order instead of asserting it.

> "One thing each market participant has to decide is whether he (or she) does or does not believe in the ability to see into the future: the "I know" school versus the "I don't know" school. The ramifications of this decision are enormous."

Marks frames a hard question as a plain choice between two named camps, which
lets a reader place themselves before he argues for a side. The homemade labels,
the "I know" school and the "I don't know" school, are his own unfussy way of
making an abstraction concrete, and they show a writer who invents plain names
for things rather than borrowing jargon.
