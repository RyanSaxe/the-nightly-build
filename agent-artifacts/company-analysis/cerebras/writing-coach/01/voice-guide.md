# Voice guide: company-analysis/cerebras (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a business question worked from a filing, for a reader who already
thinks in systems but doesn't yet know the business. Take Hempton's move in
the Hollysys passages below as the default posture toward the record: open
the actual line item, name the actual figure, and let that figure carry the
sentence instead of a characterization of it. Where the piece turns on
concentration, backlog, or margin structure, the reported number belongs in
the sentence that makes the claim, not summarized a paragraph later next to
it.

Rubinstein's passages show the other half of that habit: a statistic stated
once, plainly, and then the mechanism behind it explained in the writer's own
terms rather than left to imply itself. If a customer-concentration number or
a backlog figure is going to carry weight in this piece, it earns a sentence
or two on why the mechanism produces that number, the way Rubinstein explains
why owning a clearinghouse confers power rather than just asserting that it
does. Wafer-scale economics, inference pricing, or contract structure are the
kind of mechanism this reader needs walked through once, in plain terms, the
first time it matters to the argument, not assumed and not re-explained.

Damodaran's Tesla passages are the model for handling a business with more
than one thread running through it at once. He keeps the auto business, the
energy business, and the software business on separate footing, gives each
its own paragraph and its own numbers, and only combines them once each has
been reasoned through on its own terms. If this piece is weighing something
like inference revenue against training revenue, or one customer's contracted
backlog against the rest of the pipeline, that separation is worth holding
onto rather than blending the threads into a single impression. He is also
willing to say plainly which way his own uncertainty runs instead of hedging
it out of the sentence, which is the right register for a piece that commits
to a synthesis without issuing a call the record can't support.

The reader has the mathematics and the engineering already; what they don't
have is the business, so the explaining belongs to the market mechanics and
the company's own structure, not to the technology underneath it. Numbers
that would mean nothing to this reader out of context, the kind Hempton and
Rubinstein both anchor before using, deserve the same treatment here: a
number the reader can weigh against something they already hold, not a
number alone. Where a chart or a table can show a comparison the prose would
otherwise have to carry sentence by sentence, use it the way Rubinstein uses
his own figures, to hold the evidence rather than to decorate the argument.

## John Hempton, "Hollysys - a blog post inspired by the railway crash"

Source: http://brontecapital.blogspot.com/2011/07/hollysys-blog-post-inspired-by-railway.html

> "But as regular readers of this blog know I am a balance sheet kind of guy. When I see such amazing businesses built out of nothing I want to know what assets are employed and how much cumulative R&D there has been."

This states the writer's method as a sentence of intent, not a boast: go to
the balance sheet and check the physical claim against the physical assets.
The person is visible in the plainness of "balance sheet kind of guy" next to
a real question that follows from it.

> "Only $3.1 million of machinery is in use. That is kind of funny because this company is a manufacturer of some pretty high end kit. Indeed it is pretty hard to imagine how you manufacture the control systems (sensors etc and the computers to go with it) with only $3.1 million in kit - but the company seems to do so."

The dollar figure comes first, then the mismatch it creates. No adjective
does the work "kind of funny" and "pretty hard to imagine" together do
casually here; the actual number is doing the arguing, and the writer's voice
just points at it.

> "The rest of the balance sheet is perverse. The company had revenue in 2009 and 2010 of $157.5 million and $174.1 million respectively. Costs estimated in excess of billings are over $60 million. In other words they have done a third of year's work and not been paid. Net income last year was $27 million."

Each sentence adds one fact, in filing order, and only after three sentences
of figures does the writer restate what they mean in plain words. The
restatement earns its place because the numbers came first.

## Marc Rubinstein, "The New Power Brokers"

Source: https://www.netinterest.co/p/the-new-power-brokers

> "Since the financial crisis, the influence wielded by Goldman and banks like it has slowly eroded. In their place are the Big Tech companies, of course [1]. But within financial markets specifically, power has shifted to another group of companies. It's a group whose origins predate the big banks and who lost a power struggle to them only a few years before the crisis. That group is the stock exchanges."

The claim is held back one full sentence past where a weaker draft would name
it, and the delay is doing real work: it sets up a specific, checkable
history (who lost to whom, and when) before the sentence that names the
winner.

> "The power would not be so potent if it were not so concentrated. Six global stock exchange groups control 62% of the revenues in the space. Even though big banks have consolidated from the days when they were small partnerships, it takes over ten of them to get to that degree of concentration."

The comparison is the argument: not "exchanges are concentrated" on its own,
but the same idea measured against how many banks it takes to match it. The
number sits next to another number instead of next to an adjective.

> "Owning a clearing house confers considerable power when it comes to financial markets. Clearing houses decide on the collateral and capital requirements counterparties need to post when they trade. They decide which assets are safe enough to back transactions and which are not."

This explains a mechanism in three short declarative sentences before using
it. Nothing here is decorated; each sentence narrows from the general claim
to the specific lever, which is what lets the next paragraph's example land
without re-explaining the mechanism.

## Aswath Damodaran, "Tesla in November 2023: Story Twists and Turns, with Value Consequences!"

Source: https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2023/11/tesla-story-turns-and-twists.html

> "In the twelve months, ending September 2023, Tesla reported operating income of $10.7 billion on revenues of $95.9 billion; that puts their revenues well ahead of my 2013 projection of $65 billion, albeit with an operating margin of 11.18%, lagging my estimate of 12.5%. That makes Tesla the eleventh largest automobile company in the world, in revenue terms, and the seventh most profitable on the list, making it more and more difficult for naysayers to argue that it is a fad that will pass."

The figures are checked against the writer's own decade-old prediction rather
than against a generic benchmark, and the conclusion ("more and more
difficult for naysayers to argue") is earned by the specific comparison that
just ran, not asserted ahead of it.

> "Tesla's auto business saw revenue growth slow down from the torrid pace that it posted between 2020 and 2022, with third quarter year-on-year revenue growth dropping to single digits, but given the flat sales in the auto sector and a sluggish electric car market, it remains a stand-out. The more disappointing number, at least for those who were expecting pathways to software-company like margins for the company, was the decline in profit margins on automobiles from 2022 levels, though the 17.42% gross margin in the third quarter, while disappointing for Tesla, would have been cause for celebration at almost any of its competitors."

Two readings of the same number sit in one passage without contradiction: a
disappointment against the company's own trajectory and a strong result
against the industry's, held apart and both stated. The specific margin
figure lets both readings stand on the page at once.

> "In short, price cuts can, and often will, change the number of units sold, perhaps offsetting some of the downside to price cut (tactical), make it more difficult for competitors to keep up or enter your business (strategic) and expand the potential for side or supplemental businesses to thrive (synergistic). This figure explains the divide on the Tesla price cuts, with the pessimists arguing that electric car demand is too inelastic for volume increases that will compensate for the lower margins, and the optimists arguing that the value losses from lower margins will be more than offset by a long-term increase in Tesla's market share, and increase the value from their software and robotaxi businesses."

The three named channels turn a single move (a price cut) into three
distinct, checkable claims instead of one vague one, and the paragraph then
routes the actual market disagreement through those three channels rather
than restating that people disagree.
