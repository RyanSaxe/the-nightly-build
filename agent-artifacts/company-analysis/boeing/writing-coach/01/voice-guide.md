# Voice guide — company-analysis/boeing (invocation 01)

## Directive

Write as a numerate analyst explaining, from first principles, how a manufacturer
turns a backlog into cash. The reader holds mathematics and engineering, follows
an argument built in plain sentences, and does not follow aerospace. Assume none
of the business. Earn every step: name the three segments the first time they do
work, define a term of art in the sentence where it first appears (advance,
progress billing, reach-forward loss, deferred production cost), and only then
lean on it.

The subject is a cash-flow statement read against an order book, so the craft
that matters here is quantitative grounding. Never let a magnitude float. Every
claim about "recovery," "improvement," or "burn" lands on a specific line from the
filing with its unit, its period, and a comparison the reader already carries
(the year-ago half, the capital-expenditure line, the size of the order book).
When two numbers point in opposite directions — operating cash flow up, free cash
flow still negative — put both in the same paragraph and make the reconciliation
the sentence's work, not a hedge.

Keep three registers distinct and visibly so: what the filing reports, what you
estimate by arithmetic from it (say "by subtraction" or "on these figures" when a
number is derived), and what you synthesize. The synthesis is welcome and should
commit — but it carries the same citation load as the reporting, and it is held
to a higher bar exactly where it agrees with the market's read. The popular read
(a positive-cash quarter proves the turnaround) is the one to test hardest.

Cadence: short, single-purpose sentences carrying the mechanics; a longer,
controlled sentence when a reconciliation genuinely spans two facts. Let the
verbs and the numbers persuade; delete any sentence that only announces
importance. One earned contrast for the whole piece at most, and only where the
misconception it corrects is real and named (here: that the cash improvement
reflects delivered-jet margin). No sentence that grades the article or its
method. No "the real story is / the catch is / here is what the market missed"
frames — show the number that makes the point and let it carry.

A single quarter's free cash flow is a noisy number; the strongest writers on
this refuse to over-read one period and instead show the structure underneath it
(seasonality, the working-capital line that moved). Borrow that discipline: the
argument is about the mechanism, not one green print.

Recently used, do not reuse: the AI-capex trio's shapes and openers
(alphabet-free-cash-flow, vertiv, coreweave) — no "capex vs. FCF" cold open, no
"backlog vs. guidance / vs. depreciation" framing used as a template scaffold.
No colon-subtitle headline, no headline or dek that hedges with a question mark,
no semicolon-reversal or comma-triad dek. Do not open on a scene-setting quarter
recap or a stock ticker. Vary section-heading shape; do not repeat a
two-clauses-joined-by-and cadence across headings.

## Michael J. Mauboussin, "The Neglected Value Driver" (Counterpoint Global Insights)
Source: https://www.morganstanley.com/im/publication/insights/articles/article_theneglectedvaluedriver_ltr.pdf
Craft:
- cadence: layered scaffolding — a principle stated plainly, then the framework
  built, then applied; never application before the conceptual floor is poured.
- argument: establishes *why* a driver matters before showing *how* to measure
  it, so the reader is never asked to trust a metric they have not been taught.
- evidence: densely quantitative but humanized — specific metrics and time series,
  exhibits that turn theory into visual logic, historical cases as principle-in-action.
- stance: teacherly, first-principles, measured; assumes intelligence, not prior
  knowledge; a guide unpacking complexity, not a prophet issuing certainty.
- notice: points at the value driver others skip past and makes its materiality
  the thing to prove, not assert.
- diction: precise without jargon; unfamiliar terms are defined on arrival.
- reader: treated as smart and patient, walked up the ramp one step at a time.
- the move the axes missed: he proves materiality before spending the reader's
  attention — "consider what this looks like once the adjustment is made" — so the
  reader agrees a thing matters before the analysis leans on it.
Calibration: "The market's job is to price securities; your job is to assess
value. When the two diverge, an opportunity exists." (texture only)

## Aswath Damodaran, "Earnings and Cash Flows: A Primer on Free Cash Flow" (Musings on Markets)
Source: https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2022/10/earnings-and-cash-flows-primer-on-free.html
Craft:
- cadence: opens with a punchy provocation ("Free cash flow is one of the most
  dangerous terms in finance"), then definition, then measurement mechanics — a
  descent from claim to plumbing.
- argument: respectfully skeptical of received wisdom; grants "cash is king" its
  intuition, then rejects the oversimplification and rebuilds the term precisely.
- evidence: one company carried as a worked case (Microsoft's fiscal year), exact
  figures throughout, adjustments shown rather than asserted.
- stance: constructive precision, never superiority; the critique aims at getting
  the term right, not at scoring the reader.
- notice: flags that a single year's free cash flow is *noisier* and less
  informative than a single year's earnings — a caution against over-reading one
  period.
- diction: plain, exact, scaffolds FCFE vs. FCFF explicitly instead of assuming it.
- reader: never assumed to already hold the definition; the floor is poured first.
- the move the axes missed: he pre-empts "does this even matter?" by computing the
  difference and letting its size answer — skepticism, then materiality by data.
Calibration: "If you are wondering whether it makes enough of a difference to
bother, consider what Microsoft's FCFE look like with all five adjustments made to
them below." (texture only)

## Marc Rubinstein, "Banks in Disguise" (Net Interest)
Source: https://www.netinterest.co/p/banks-in-disguise
Craft:
- cadence: a concrete origin story or scene first, then extraction of the
  financial mechanism the story reveals; each case follows the same clean arc.
- argument: shows that an ordinary-looking business (a cruise line, a coffee card)
  is quietly running a financing operation — the general principle earned from the
  particular, not stated up front.
- evidence: scales translated into benchmarks a reader already holds ("$1.9bn of
  stored value" set against "85% of US banks have less than $1bn in assets";
  customer deposits that finance "a sixth of the fleet").
- stance: conversational and patient; explains escheatment or deferred revenue
  without pedantry; a dry wit that never crowds out the mechanics.
- notice: sees the balance-sheet liability (customer money held before delivery)
  as the real engine and names it — directly relevant here, where customer
  advances are the liability doing the work.
- diction: clean, unshowy, parenthetical asides used sparingly for wit.
- reader: brought along; a technical concept is unpacked the first time it appears.
- the move the axes missed: he reads a liability as a business model — where the
  customers' prepaid money sits, and what it is really funding.
Calibration: "Cruises on the Sunward sold as fast as Ted's people could take the
orders; passengers rolled into the Port of Miami by the hundreds in their
tail-finned Buicks and Chevrolets." (texture only)
