# Voice guide: company-analysis/super-micro

## How this piece should sound

This piece is answering one mechanical question: does Super Micro's margin
jump survive contact with its balance sheet, and does the revenue surge come
back as cash or sit in inventory and receivables. The register that question
calls for is the one Rubinstein, Hobart, and Damodaran all share below: state
the figure, state what it implies, and let the implication do the work a
verdict would otherwise have to do. None of the three writers below ever tells
the reader what to do with the stock. They tell the reader what the numbers
say, and stop there, which is the discipline this piece needs given the
commission's own bar against a buy, sell, or allocation call.

Rubinstein's move on De La Rue and on NCR is the direct model for the margin
and cash-conversion sections: put the profit or cash-flow figure in the same
sentence as the revenue it came from, so the gap is visible without a
sentence announcing that a gap exists. "17.5% gross margin" and "9.9% the
prior quarter" want to sit next to what they cost or produced in cash, the
way Rubinstein sits De La Rue's £14 million of operating profit next to its
£255 million of revenue. When the piece reaches inventory, receivables, and
operating cash flow against reported net income, the NCR passage is the
closer model still: name the free-cash-flow figure, name the percent of
revenue it represents, name the prior year's percent, in one breath, and let
the reader do the subtraction.

Hobart's opening move, naming the general failure mode before naming the
company, is worth borrowing for wherever this piece has to teach the assembler
model itself: what an integrator's margin actually is, why inventory and
receivables move the way they do for a company that buys parts and configures
racks. State the mechanism in general terms first, in plain declarative
sentences, then bring Super Micro's own numbers to it, the way Hobart brings
GE and Amazon to a general point about planning before either company is
named as an example rather than a subject. That also protects against writing
a mechanism explanation that only makes sense once the reader already knows
what a gross margin step looks like.

Damodaran's discipline is the one to hold hardest to. He spends an entire
piece being opinionated about how free cash flow gets miscomputed and abused,
and then, at the exact point where that opinion could convert into a verdict
on a stock, he declines the conversion in his own voice rather than going
quiet about it: he says plainly that he sees little benefit in the very
metric he has spent the piece defending, once the use case shifts to pricing.
This piece's own equivalent moment is the guidance section: FY2027 numbers
and the backlog are claims, not findings, and the honest move is to say so in
that same direct register rather than either endorsing them or hedging around
them.

Keep the accounting history in the same plain, figure-first register as
everything else. The Hindenburg report, the auditor resignation, and the
near-delisting are facts with dates and documents behind them, and they earn
the same treatment as a margin percentage: stated, sourced, and left to carry
its own weight, the way Rubinstein lets Diebold Nixdorf's bankruptcy sit as a
fact rather than dressing it up. Nothing about the assembler's cash story
needs a promotional lift or a skeptic's wink to land.

## Marc Rubinstein, "The Money Business"

Source: https://www.netinterest.co/p/the-money-business

> "But it doesn't exactly print money, the money-printing business. Last
> year, De La Rue earned an operating profit of just £14 million on £255
> million of revenue. That's down from £42 million on £399 million of
> revenue in 2019."

The joke in the first sentence only works because the two numbers right
behind it prove it. Rubinstein doesn't say margins are thin and then cite a
figure to back it up; the figure is the sentence. The prior-year comparison
in the next sentence does the same job again without restating what "thin"
means.

> "However, while it has been ramping up this strategy, free cash flow has
> been weak. Last year, the company generated just $204 million of free cash
> flow, equivalent to 5% of revenue, down from 13% in 2020. With $2.7 billion
> in debt, NCR isn't as leveraged as Diebold Nixdorf but by betting that ATMs
> can survive beyond cash, it is hoping not to run out of road."

This is a revenue-to-cash-flow gap made concrete in two sentences: the dollar
figure, then the same figure converted to a percent of revenue, then the
prior year's percent right beside it, so the decline is visible without the
word "decline" doing any of the work. The debt figure that follows is placed
where it changes the stakes of the number just given, not dropped in as a
separate fact.

> "All this means that while cash may be dying out, managing it still
> sustains a large industry. Companies exist to manufacture cash, to
> transport it and to distribute it."

This is the sentence that turns a pile of individually interesting facts
about ATMs and banknotes into a structure the reader can follow: three verbs,
three sections to come. It is a signpost, but it is built from the material
itself rather than from a generic transition, which is why it can be this
short.

## Byrne Hobart, "FP&A as the Telemetry and Counterespionage"

Source: https://www.thediff.co/archive/fp-a-as-the-telemetry-and-counterespionage/

> "The two most common grades a company gets for managing its finances are an
> F and a gentleman's C. A company gets a failing grade if it had a
> reasonable business that could have turned a profit, but managed to finance
> it the wrong way so it ran out of cash."

The piece's whole argument is set up before a single company is named. Hobart
states the general failure mode in plain terms a reader can hold onto, and
only after that does the essay start attaching it to Amazon, GE, and Disney.
The grading metaphor is concrete enough to double as a structure the rest of
the piece returns to.

> "GE's model looked amazing for a while: sell durable equipment, turn that
> into a predictable stream of maintenance revenue, turn the sale itself into
> recurring revenue by helping to finance it, etc. But once they had a big
> enough finance division, there was no end to the number of ideas that
> looked good on paper, and the returns from esoteric lending are more stable
> quarter to quarter than the returns from selling consumer durables or
> capital goods. But not every quarter is any given quarter, and GE ended up
> being an over-levered specialty credit hedge fund with a few manufacturing
> businesses bolted on."

Hobart narrates the business model as a sequence, each clause a step, before
naming what went wrong with it. The last sentence is blunt about the outcome
without needing an adjective to tell the reader how bad it was; "specialty
credit hedge fund with a few manufacturing businesses bolted on" is the
assessment, stated as a description rather than announced as a verdict.

> "So beyond the pass/fail binary, FP&A is particularly effective at helping
> stealthily great businesses maximize their lead before anyone catches on.
> It's a weird mix: a lot of finance, a fair amount of strategy, and, in the
> end, a company that gets an A+ for effective counterintelligence."

The closing line returns to the grading frame from the opening without
restating it, which is why it reads as an ending rather than a summary. It
extends the metaphor one step further instead of repeating it.

## Aswath Damodaran, "Earnings and Cash Flows: A Primer on Free Cash Flow"

Source: https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2022/10/earnings-and-cash-flows-primer-on-free.html

> "Free cash flow is one of the most dangerous terms in finance, and I am
> astonished by how it can be bent to mean whatever investors or managers
> want it to, and used to advance their sales pitches. I have seen analysts
> and managers argue that adding back depreciation to earnings gives you free
> cash flow, an intermediate stop, at best, if you truly are intent on
> computing free cash flow."

Damodaran commits to a strong claim about a term the reader may think is
settled, and the sentence that follows immediately shows the specific error
rather than leaving the claim to float. The commitment is in the verb choice
("bent," "astonished") married to a concrete example of the bending, not in
either one alone.

> "As a believer in cash flows, I have always gravitated to the statement of
> cash flows as the accounting disclosure that is least contaminated by
> accounting overreach and the one that best reflects the true operations of
> a business."

A stated point of view sits next to the reason for it in the same sentence.
Naming himself as a believer in cash flows before making the claim is what
lets the claim read as a position rather than a fact smuggled past the
reader.

> "As a cash flow advocate, it pains me to say this, but if your game is
> pricing stocks, I see little benefit from replacing traditional multiples
> (like PE and EV to EBITDA) with free cash flow scaled pricing measures.
> That is because a single year's free cash flow (to equity or the firm)
> actually has more noise in it, and is less informative about a company's
> operating health, than a single year's earnings (net income or EBITDA)."

Here the writer's own stated allegiance works against the point he is about
to make, and he says so before making it. That is what keeps a strongly
argued piece from tipping into a recommendation: the conclusion follows the
evidence into a place the writer's own priors did not want it to go, and he
names the tension rather than smoothing over it.
