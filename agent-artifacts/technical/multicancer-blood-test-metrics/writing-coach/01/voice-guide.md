# Voice guide

## How this piece should sound

Write as a technically exact guide for a reader who can follow conditional probability but may not know clinical-test vocabulary. Begin with the population being screened, then define prevalence, sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value in the order needed for one worked cohort. Keep one notation throughout: use \(D^+\) and \(D^-\) for cancer present and absent, \(T^+\) and \(T^-\) for positive and negative test results, and define every conditional probability in words when it first appears. Give counts for a fixed population alongside the equation so that the denominator remains visible. Gawande's short verdicts show how a plain sentence can stop the calculation and state what it means.

Treat the assay as a sequence of distinct questions. A cancer-signal result, a tissue-of-origin prediction, a completed diagnostic workup, and a confirmed diagnosis each use a different denominator and support a different claim. The prose may admire an assay's technical ingenuity while remaining exact about who was studied, which test version was used, and whether the endpoint was detection, diagnostic resolution, stage shift, or mortality. Spiegelhalter's age-stratified comparison is the model for letting benefits and harms change with the population instead of assigning the technology one universal verdict.

Write about benefits and harms in the same concrete units. A true positive can lead to an earlier diagnosis; a false positive or unresolved signal can lead to imaging, procedures, time, expense, and anxiety. Do not imply that every detected cancer would have become clinically important, or that every workup is harmful. State the strongest case for multicancer screening in terms its advocates and investigators use, then state what the cited design can and cannot establish. Greenhalgh's willingness to name both the power and the limits of evidence-based medicine is useful here: qualification should sharpen a conclusion, not erase it.

This is an explanation of test metrics and study claims, not medical advice. Do not tell a reader whether to buy, request, decline, repeat, or act on a test. Do not treat an advisory-panel vote as approval, an association as a patient benefit, a tissue-of-origin prediction as a diagnosis, or a negative result as proof that cancer is absent. Prefer FDA meeting materials and the study report that owns a number; use independent clinical commentary for interpretation and disagreement. Attach every performance figure to its population, protocol, endpoint, and test version, and distinguish prespecified results from exploratory analyses.

End with a compact reading checklist that preserves the article's stages and denominators. The final sentence should settle what a reported metric permits the reader to infer, without a slogan, forecast, medical recommendation, or generic appeal for more research. Avoid an antithetical opener, repeated display-math blocks, “turns X into Y,” “the key is,” “the catch is,” “the promise and peril,” “only time will tell,” and a heading or closing command that begins with “Test.” Also cut performed caution, decorative metaphors, and any sentence whose subject-specific nouns could be swapped for those of another diagnostic technology.

## Atul Gawande, “Overkill”

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2015/05/11/overkill-atul-gawande

> “There’s nothing less memorable.”

Checked: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2015/05/11/overkill-atul-gawande, retrieved 2026-09-25

The four-word sentence follows Gawande's account of remembering a missed diagnosis but forgetting unnecessary interventions. Its force comes from the clinician admitting how his own memory is biased, not from inflating the claim.

> “It isn’t enough to eliminate unnecessary care. It has to be replaced with necessary care.”

Checked: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2015/05/11/overkill-atul-gawande, retrieved 2026-09-25

The paired sentences narrow a long reported case to a practical distinction. Gawande sounds decisive because the case has earned the judgment, and humane because he keeps the patient's unmet need in view.

## David Spiegelhalter, “Regulation in an age of personalised medicine”

Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/news/sir-david-spiegelhalter-regulation-in-an-age-of-personalised-medicine

> “For older people the benefits clearly outweigh the potential harms, but for younger groups these become finely balanced.”

Checked: https://www.gov.uk/government/news/sir-david-spiegelhalter-regulation-in-an-age-of-personalised-medicine, retrieved 2026-09-25

The sentence keeps the comparison tied to strata rather than announcing one verdict for everyone. “Finely balanced” is restrained, but it follows an explicit age comparison and therefore names a result rather than performing caution.

> “I admit to a conflict-of-interest.”

Checked: https://www.gov.uk/government/news/sir-david-spiegelhalter-regulation-in-an-age-of-personalised-medicine, retrieved 2026-09-25

Spiegelhalter uses this dry disclosure before explaining his own prostate cancer and treatment preferences. The first person makes his stake visible without asking the personal story to substitute for population evidence.

## Trisha Greenhalgh, “Will Evidence-Based Medicine Survive COVID-19?”

Source: https://www.bostonreview.net/articles/trisha-greenhalgh-covid-19-and-evidence-based-medicine/

> “In this country at least, we are failing.”

Checked: https://www.bostonreview.net/articles/trisha-greenhalgh-covid-19-and-evidence-based-medicine/, retrieved 2026-09-25

The qualification limits the claim geographically while the verb still commits to a judgment. Greenhalgh's presence is clear in “we”: she writes as a participant in the medical system she is assessing.

> “On the contrary, medicine often can and does act more promptly when the need arises.”

Checked: https://www.bostonreview.net/articles/trisha-greenhalgh-covid-19-and-evidence-based-medicine/, retrieved 2026-09-25

This sentence answers a quoted opposing view directly and then moves into examples. The cadence is firm without pretending that prompt action is always correct; “often” leaves the scope visible.
