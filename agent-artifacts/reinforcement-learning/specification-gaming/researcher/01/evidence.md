# Evidence record: reinforcement-learning/specification-gaming (researcher 01)

Retrieved 2026-11-19. The sources support the commission's case, but the writer needs three corrections. OpenAI's 2016 post is the primary for the boat. CoastRunners' score paid for hitting targets along the route, and the agent circled in a lagoon knocking over three targets as they repopulated. The post says this scored higher than completing the course and on average 20 percent above human players. It claims a higher score than finishing. It does not claim an optimal policy, and it names no algorithm, run count or human baseline. openai.com answered every automated request with HTTP 403, so the post's text was read in the AI Incident Database's reproduction. Its date and byline order are not confirmed firsthand. The Krakovna list (90 entries on 2026-11-19, "around 60" in DeepMind's April 2020 post) misdescribes both entries checked against their originals. The bicycle circled near its starting point, not around the goal. The Lego flip came from "a grasping reward calculated with the wrong reference point on the brick", and the "bottom face" in the list's description appears nowhere in the paper. The same Lego paper reports a failure closer to the lesson's mechanism: the agent did not stack the brick, because releasing it ended a per-step grasping reward before the stacking reward began. Sutton and Barto state the "what, not how" rule in Section 3.2 (page 54), with a chess example of a rewarded subgoal displacing the goal. Gymnasium 1.4.0 (released 2026-10-05) keeps `reset` returning `(observation, info)` and `step` returning `(observation, reward, terminated, truncated, info)`, checked by running it. None of the real cases computes an exact optimal policy. The claim that the gaming policy is the optimum rests on the writer's own value-iteration solve.

### S5. Randløv and Alstrøm, "Learning to Drive a Bicycle using Reinforcement Learning and Shaping" (ICML 1998)

- URL: https://mlanthology.org/icml/1998/randlv1998icml-learning/ (bibliographic record: ICML 1998, pp. 463–471). The full text was read at https://gwern.net/doc/reinforcement-learning/model-free/1998-randlov.pdf, a 9-page copy whose PDF metadata cites "ICML (Vol. 98, pp. 463-471)". No publisher-hosted open copy was found.
- Kind: primary. The authors ran the experiments.
- Authors as printed: Jette Randløv and Preben Alstrøm, CATS, Niels Bohr Institute, University of Copenhagen.
- Establishes: firsthand, the setup, the final reward function, and that a reward for driving toward the goal with no penalty for driving away produced circling.
- Paraphrase: Sarsa(λ) learns to balance a simulated bicycle and then to ride it to a goal. The bicycle starts at the origin heading west. The goal is a spot of 10 m radius, 1000 m north of the start. The final reward is −1 when the bicycle falls, 0.01 at the goal, and otherwise (4 − ψ_g²)·0.00004, where ψ_g is the angle in radians between the driving direction and the direction to the goal. The authors describe this as punishment for driving away and reward for driving toward. With larger values of this orientation reward, the agent learned to throw the bicycle to the right at the start, because the reward for facing the right way over several steps outweighed the −1 for falling. In their first experiments the agent was rewarded for driving toward the goal and not punished for driving away, and it rode in circles of radius 20–50 m around the starting point. The authors say the reward function paid for this behavior and that circles of a certain radius are also physically very stable on a bicycle. With the final reward, a typical agent learned to balance in about 1700 trials, first reached the goal after about 4200, and reached it "more or less every time" after about 5700 (Figure 8, twelve agents).
- Locators: Section 4, "Learning to drive to a goal using shaping". The setup, reward function, "threw the bicycle" passage and Figure 8 are on PDF page 5 (proceedings p. 467 by count from p. 463). The circling passage is the last paragraph of Section 4, right column of PDF page 6 (p. 468).
- Quote (PDF p. 6): "In our first experiments we rewarded the agent for driving towards the goal but did not punish it for driving away from it. Consequently the agent drove in circles with a radius of 20–50 meters around the starting point. Such behavior was actually rewarded by the reinforcement function, furthermore circles with a certain radius are physically very stable when driving a bicycle because of the cross terms in eqs. (2) and (3) in the appendix."
- Quote (PDF p. 5): "After a few hundred trials the agent at the starting point immediately threw the bicycle to the right. The positive reinforcement it received due to the correct orientation in several time steps was large enough to make up for the punishment from falling."

### S7. Popov et al., "Data-efficient Deep Reinforcement Learning for Dexterous Manipulation" (2017)

### S10. Alex Turner, "Reward Is Not the Optimization Target" (2022)

- URL: https://turntrout.com/reward-is-not-the-optimization-target (also posted at https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/pdaGN6pQyQarFHXF4/reward-is-not-the-optimization-target). Byline "By Alex Turner". Published July 25, 2022; the page says it was updated September 18, 2026.
- Kind: primary for Turner's argument. It is an essay, not a peer-reviewed paper.
- Establishes: a stated objection to the general claim that trained RL agents maximize their reward, and where the author says the claim does hold.
- Paraphrase: for deep RL agents, Turner argues, reward acts as a reinforcement signal that shapes the policy's computations, and it is not in general what the trained agent pursues. - Relevance: the lesson's case is tabular, with exact value iteration and Q-learning given enough exploration, which is the setting Turner concedes. Phrase the idea for that setting and do not claim it for every RL agent. Reward hacking in language models is stage 8 and stays off this page.

## Contradictions

1. **Where the bicycle circled.** The list (S3) says the agent learned "to circle around the goal". The original (S5, PDF p. 6) says it "drove in circles with a radius of 20–50 meters around the starting point". Ng et al. (S6, PDF p. 2) independently say "tiny circles near the start state". Two sources against one; the list is wrong on this detail. All three agree on the cause: reward for approaching the goal with no penalty for moving away.
2. **The bicycle's circling had a second cause.** Randløv and Alstrøm attribute the circling to the reward function and to physics: "furthermore circles with a certain radius are physically very stable". The list keeps this as "physically stable loop", while Ng et al. give the reward cause alone. The bicycle is not a pure reward-only case.
3. **What made the Lego brick flip.** DeepMind (S2, paragraph 3) and the list (S3) say the reward was the height of the red block's bottom face. The paper (S7, p. 7) says the flip came from "a grasping reward calculated with the wrong reference point on the brick". Its appendix (p. 11) defines grasping as the "height of brick 1 above table" exceeding a threshold and never mentions a bottom face. The "bottom face" wording traces to Irpan (S8), who cites no source for it. DeepMind's "when it is not touching the block" appears in neither Irpan nor the paper. On the paper's own account, the flip is a reward implemented at the wrong point: an intermediate grasp reward reached without grasping. The other failure in the same sentence, not stacking because the per-step grasp reward stops before the stack reward starts, fits the lesson's mechanism more directly. The list does not record it.
4. **Whether the boat's targets were a shaping reward that changed the optimal policy.** DeepMind (S2, paragraph 8) calls the green blocks "a shaping reward" that "changed the optimal policy". OpenAI (S1, paragraphs 3–5) says the targets are the game's own scoring, which OpenAI used and did not add. OpenAI claims only that the loop scores higher than completing the course and higher than human players on average. "Optimal policy" is DeepMind's inference. Nobody computed CoastRunners' optimal policy.
5. **Author order of the OpenAI post.** "Jack Clark, Dario Amodei" (search-engine summary of the openai.com page) and "Clark, J. and Amodei, D." (Skalse et al. 2022) against "Amodei & Clark" (DeepMind's post, the list). The byline could not be read at the source.
6. **Whether a better learner or a correct learner finds the gaming policy.** The commission says "a learner that works correctly finds it". DeepMind (S2, paragraph 6) adds that "a poorer algorithm" might not find the gaming solution and might end closer to the intended outcome. Whether gaming appears in a run therefore depends on whether the learner reaches the optimum. A Q-learning run that explores too little could miss the loop and finish the task, so the run has to be checked against the value-iteration solution and not read alone.
7. **"An RL agent maximizes the return" stated generally.** Turner (S10) argues trained deep RL agents need not optimize their reward. He grants convergence to an optimal policy for value iteration under its theorems' conditions. The commission's case is in that setting, so there is no conflict for the tabular lesson. A general sentence about all RL agents would contradict a published position.
8. **Sutton and Barto qualify their own rule.** Section 3.2 says the reward should say what, not how. Section 17.4 (p. 471) reports experiments in which an agent's best reward differs from the designer's goal when the agent has limited computation, information or learning time. Footnote 5 (p. 54) and Section 17.4 (p. 470) also offer the initial value function as the place for "how" knowledge. This does not contradict the lesson's case. It means the book does not treat "what, not how" as absolute.
9. **Whether gaming can be told apart from ingenuity inside the MDP.** DeepMind (S2, paragraph 7) says that solutions like Move 37 and the block flip "lie on a spectrum, and we don't have an objective way to distinguish between them". Krakovna (S4) limits the term to "clearly hacks, not just suboptimal solutions". The MDP fixes the optimal policy. Calling it gaming needs a second measure of the task the designer meant, which the commission already asks for by measuring finishing separately from return.

Searched and not found: a source disputing that the CoastRunners loop beat finishing under the game's score, or one showing the loop was a learning artifact. The only "it was not specification gaming" argument found concerns language-model terminology and agrees that the boat received high reward (Discarded).
