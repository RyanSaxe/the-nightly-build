# Evidence record: reinforcement-learning/learning-values-from-samples (researcher 01)

The record supports every definition the lesson needs, each at a page or section locator I opened: first-visit and every-visit Monte Carlo (MC) prediction, the running-mean update, the TD(0) update and TD error, bootstrapping, the convergence statements for each method, and the MC-versus-TD comparison. It also supports one real-world case for the opener, Tesauro's TD-Gammon, read in full. It is thin in three places. First, no source proves that TD(0) has lower variance than MC or that it converges faster; Sutton & Barto call that an open question in Section 6.2 and Sutton's 1988 paper says the same, so the lesson's bias-variance contrast has to be earned from the writer's runs. Second, every-visit MC is biased at finite sample sizes (Singh & Sutton 1996), which the commission's "MC is unbiased" glosses over. Third, TD-Gammon used TD(lambda) with a neural network and chose moves by its own predictions, so it is a real case of TD learning and not of tabular TD(0) prediction. The running numbers (RMS error, v(13) estimates, episode counts) are not in any source and come from the writer's run.

Paraphrase:  (all locators are printed pages; quotes verified in the PDF text layer, and the p. 93 passage also checked on the rendered page)
  1. Setting. MC methods need only experience, "sample sequences of states, actions, and rewards", and are defined only for episodic tasks. Only when an episode completes are estimates changed, so MC is incremental episode by episode and not step by step (online). Chapter 5 opening, p. 91. The prediction problem is the computation of v_pi for a fixed arbitrary policy pi (p. 92, first paragraph after the chapter opening, and again at the start of Chapter 6, p. 119).
  2. First-visit and every-visit MC. A "visit" is an occurrence of a state in an episode, and the first visit is the first time it occurs in that episode. First-visit MC averages the returns following first visits to s. Every-visit MC averages returns following all visits. Section 5.1, p. 92. The first-visit procedural box is on p. 92 (loops backward through the episode, G <- G + R_{t+1}, and appends G to Returns(S_t) only "Unless S_t appears in S_0, ..., S_{t-1}"). The text states that every-visit MC "would be the same except without the check for S_t having occurred earlier in the episode." The authors focus on first-visit and note every-visit "extends more naturally to function approximation and eligibility traces".
  3. Convergence and unbiasedness of MC. Both converge to v_pi(s) as the number of visits (first visits) to s goes to infinity. For first-visit MC, each return is an i.i.d. estimate of v_pi(s) with finite variance, so the law of large numbers applies, "each average is itself an unbiased estimate", and the standard deviation of its error falls as 1/sqrt(n) for n returns averaged. For every-visit the book says only that it is "less straightforward" and that estimates "also converge quadratically" to v_pi(s), citing Singh & Sutton (1996). Section 5.1, p. 93. The book does not say here whether every-visit is unbiased. Singh & Sutton (below) say it is not.
  4. MC target and backup. For MC estimation of v_pi the root is a state node and below it is the entire sampled trajectory to the terminal state, unlike DP's one-step all-successor diagram. p. 94-95. MC estimates for each state are independent, and MC does "not bootstrap". p. 95.
  5. Running-mean (incremental) update. Section 2.4, p. 31, Eq. (2.3): Q_{n+1} = Q_n + (1/n)[R_n - Q_n], derived from the sample average with constant memory (n and Q_n only). Eq. (2.4) gives the general form NewEstimate <- OldEstimate + StepSize[Target - OldEstimate]. The text notes that step size 1/n changes each step. Applying it to MC returns (replace R_n with the return G and n with the visit count N(s)) is the writer's step, not stated in Chapter 5. Chapter 5's own "Incremental Implementation" (Section 5.6) is the off-policy weighted-importance-sampling version and is not the on-policy update the lesson needs.
  6. Constant-alpha MC. Section 6.1, p. 119, Eq. (6.1): V(S_t) <- V(S_t) + alpha[G_t - V(S_t)], introduced as "a simple every-visit Monte Carlo method suitable for nonstationary environments", named constant-alpha MC. p. 119-120.
  7. TD(0). Section 6.1, p. 120, Eq. (6.2): V(S_t) <- V(S_t) + alpha[R_{t+1} + gamma V(S_{t+1}) - V(S_t)], made "immediately on transition to S_{t+1} and receiving R_{t+1}". "The target for the Monte Carlo update is G_t, whereas the target for the TD update is R_{t+1} + gamma V(S_{t+1})." Called TD(0) or one-step TD. The tabular TD(0) box (step size alpha in (0, 1], V(terminal) = 0, update after each step, loop "until S is terminal") is on p. 120.
  8. Bootstrapping. "Because TD(0) bases its update in part on an existing estimate, we say that it is a bootstrapping method, like DP." The MC target samples the expectation in Eq. (6.3), v_pi(s) = E[G_t | S_t = s]. The DP target uses Eq. (6.4), E[R_{t+1} + gamma v_pi(S_{t+1}) | S_t = s], with the model supplying the expectation. The TD target is an estimate for both reasons: it samples the expectation and it uses V in place of v_pi. p. 120-121. TD backup diagram: one sample transition from the state to its successor, p. 121.
  9. TD error. Section 6.1, p. 121, Eq. (6.5): delta_t = R_{t+1} + gamma V(S_{t+1}) - V(S_t). It is "the error in V(S_t), available at time t + 1". If V does not change during the episode, the MC error equals the discounted sum of TD errors, G_t - V(S_t) = sum_{k=t}^{T-1} gamma^{k-t} delta_k (Eq. 6.6), and the identity "is not exact if V is updated during the episode (as it is in TD(0))". p. 121.
  10. MC waits and TD does not. "Whereas Monte Carlo methods must wait until the end of the episode to determine the increment to V(S_t) (only then is G_t known), TD methods need to wait only until the next time step." p. 120. Section 6.2, p. 124: TD is "naturally implemented in an online, fully incremental fashion", which matters for long episodes and continuing tasks.
  11. TD(0) convergence. Section 6.2, p. 124: "For any fixed policy pi, TD(0) has been proved to converge to v_pi, in the mean for a constant step-size parameter if it is sufficiently small, and with probability 1 if the step-size parameter decreases according to the usual stochastic approximation conditions (2.7)." Most proofs apply to the tabular case. Attribution in the Chapter 6 remarks, p. 139: in the mean by Sutton (1988), with probability 1 by Dayan (1992).
  12. Which is faster is open. Section 6.2, p. 124: "At the current time this is an open question in the sense that no one has been able to prove mathematically that one method converges faster than the other. In fact, it is not even clear what is the most appropriate formal way to phrase this question! In practice, however, TD methods have usually been found to converge faster than constant-alpha MC methods on stochastic tasks, as illustrated in Example 6.2."
  13. Random-walk comparison. Example 6.2, p. 125: five-state undiscounted Markov reward process A to E, start at C, equal chance left or right, +1 only on terminating at the right end, so true values are 1/6, 2/6, 3/6, 4/6, 5/6. Measure is RMS error against v_pi averaged over the five states, then over 100 runs, with V initialized to 0.5. "The TD method was consistently better than the MC method on this task." With constant alpha (0.1 in the single-run left graph) "the values fluctuate indefinitely in response to the outcomes of the most recent episodes." Exercises 6.4 and 6.5 (p. 126) ask whether the ranking depends on alpha and why TD's RMS error rises again at high alpha, so the book itself flags the result as alpha-dependent.
  14. Batch updating. Section 6.3, pp. 126-128: under batch updating batch TD(0) converges to the certainty-equivalence estimate (exactly correct for the maximum-likelihood model of the observed episodes), while constant-alpha MC converges to the sample averages of observed returns, which minimize mean square error on the training set. Example 6.4 ("You are the Predictor", pp. 127-128): eight episodes, V(B) = 3/4 for both, V(A) = 3/4 for batch TD and 0 for batch MC. The book says nonbatch methods "move roughly in these directions" and that "nothing more definite can be said about the relative efficiency of online TD and Monte Carlo methods." p. 128.
  15. Where bias and variance are stated. Not in Chapters 5 or 6 for on-policy prediction. Chapter 5 uses "unbiased" for first-visit MC (p. 93) and discusses variance only for off-policy importance sampling (Section 5.5, pp. 104-108). The MC-versus-TD statements are elsewhere: Section 9.3, p. 202, "the Monte Carlo target U_t = G_t is by definition an unbiased estimate of v_pi(S_t)" while bootstrapping targets "will be biased" because they depend on the current weights. Section 13.5, p. 331, "the one-step return is often superior to the actual return in terms of its variance and computational congeniality, even though it introduces bias." Section 13.8, p. 337, bootstrapping TD methods are "often superior to Monte Carlo methods (substantially reduced variance)". These are general statements made in a policy-gradient chapter, not a derivation for tabular prediction.
  16. Where MC does better. Section 5.10, p. 115: MC "may be less harmed by violations of the Markov property" because it does not bootstrap. Section 12.13, p. 317 repeats it for non-Markov tasks. Section 11.3, p. 265: bootstrapping can impair learning "where the state representation is poor and causes poor generalization", and a poor representation "can also result in bias".

## Contradictions

1. Commission: MC "is unbiased". Singh & Sutton 1996 (article p. 5 and Table 1, p. 16): only first-visit MC is unbiased. Every-visit MC is biased at finite sample sizes, with the bias shrinking as visits accumulate, and has lower MSE early and higher later. S&B p. 93 says first-visit averages are unbiased and gives every-visit only "converge". The lesson teaches both estimators, so any "MC is unbiased" sentence must name first-visit, or say every-visit is consistent but biased.
2. Commission: TD "has lower variance than MC", to be "shown with real runs". No source proves it. S&B p. 124 and Sutton 1988 p. 29 both say faster convergence of TD has not been proved in general. Sutton 1988 p. 18 supports the direction with an informal noise argument and says it "does not prove TD methods will be better on balance". S&B states the variance advantage only as a general remark in Chapter 13 (pp. 331, 337). The claim is empirical and task-dependent, and the book's own Exercise 6.4 asks whether the ranking holds across alpha.
3. Commission: both methods "converge to the same v_pi here (tabular, decaying or small alpha)" and the learning curves should fall "toward 0". S&B p. 124: TD(0) converges with probability 1 only if alpha decreases under the stochastic-approximation conditions, and with a small constant alpha it converges only in the mean. S&B p. 125: with constant alpha the estimates "fluctuate indefinitely". A constant-alpha RMS curve therefore flattens at a nonzero floor that depends on alpha, which is the opposite of "falling toward 0". Decaying alpha, or 1/N for MC, reaches the limit. The lesson has to say which step-size rule each trace uses.
4. Commission: TD-Gammon is "the canonical real success of TD prediction". Tesauro's article trained TD(lambda) with a multilayer neural network and selected moves by maximizing the network's predicted outcome, which Tesauro calls a "combined prediction/control task". S&B (pp. 421-425) says the same. It is neither tabular nor TD(0), and the policy being evaluated changed throughout training. The opener can honestly claim that a value function was learned by temporal-difference updates from self-play outcomes, and must not claim tabular TD(0) prediction of a fixed policy.
5. TD-Gammon strength depends on the version. The zero-knowledge, raw-input network reached only "approximately equal to Neurogammon", a strong intermediate player (Tesauro, Results of Training). The near-grandmaster results belong to versions 1.0 to 2.1, which add handcrafted features and, from 2.0, a 2-ply search. S&B p. 421 opens with "near the level of the world's strongest grandmasters", which compresses that.
6. TD-Gammon training-game counts disagree. Tesauro's article: best raw-encoding network, 40 hidden units, 200,000 games. S&B Table 16.1 (p. 425) and p. 424: TD-Gammon 0.0, 40 hidden units, 300,000 games. S&B cites Tesauro's 1992, 1994, 1995 and 2002 papers together and may describe a different run. I did not resolve this. If the opener gives a game count, use Tesauro's own article and name the network.
7. Bootstrapping is not uniformly better. S&B p. 115 and p. 317: MC may be less harmed by non-Markov structure. S&B p. 265: bootstrapping can hurt with a poor state representation. Sutton 1988 p. 18 gives a constructed case where TD does worse. These do not contradict the commission's grid result, where the state is a cell number and the Markov property holds, but they bound how far the lesson can generalize from the grid.
8. Sutton 1988, printed Figure 3 on p. 20 is wrong. Use the corrected figure on p. 377 for any claim about error against lambda.

## Limits

1. No source establishes TD(0) has lower variance than MC, or converges faster, in general. S&B p. 124 and Sutton 1988 p. 29 say it is unproved. The lesson's bias-variance contrast rests entirely on the writer's runs on this grid, and it should be stated as what the runs show with the step-size rule used, not as a theorem. The commission's "MC unbiased, high variance; TD biased, lower variance" is a standard framing but its only textual homes are Section 9.3 p. 202 (MC target unbiased, bootstrap target biased) and Chapter 13 pp. 331, 337 (TD reduced variance). Chapters 5 and 6 never state it for tabular prediction.
2. All running numbers come from code. RMS error against the exact v_pi, the v(13) estimates, episode counts and the seed-to-seed spread are the writer's to produce by running the committed script. I ran no code and give no value for them. Lesson 1's published -2.01, -1.94 and 0.05 are inherited, not re-verified.
3. The step-size rule changes what the curves look like. With constant alpha, TD(0) and constant-alpha MC fluctuate indefinitely (S&B p. 125) and the RMS curve has a nonzero floor. With 1/N(s) for MC and a decaying alpha for TD the error approaches 0. I found no source with a decaying-alpha curve for this exact comparison; Example 6.2 uses constant alpha.
4. TD-Gammon is TD(lambda) with a neural network and move selection from predictions, not tabular TD(0) on a fixed policy. The article text never states the lambda value used. The update formula is in the transcription only as an image (tdl-eq1.gif), which I did not view, so I know it only from its alt text: w[t+1] - w[t] = alpha (Y[t+1] - Y[t]) times the sum over k = 1 to t of lambda^(t-k) grad_w Y[k], with no discount factor. At lambda = 0 this reduces to the TD(0) form alpha (Y[t+1] - Y[t]) grad_w Y[t] (that reduction is my algebra, not a sentence in the article). S&B Eq. 16.1 (p. 424) gives the same TD(lambda) rule in its own notation and says gamma = 1 and the reward is zero except upon winning, so the TD error there is V(S_{t+1}) - V(S_t). A claim about the formula should cite Tesauro for the form and S&B p. 424 for the gamma = 1 statement.
5. I could not open the ACM page. Tesauro was read in full from the bkgm.com transcription, which has no page numbers. The citeable address is the ACM DOI https://doi.org/10.1145/203330.203343, which resolves to a gated page, and the transcription is a copy made with ACM's permission. The writer should cite the DOI as the document and cite the heading, not a page. I could not check the transcription against the printed journal page by page.
6. Springer (Sutton 1988, Singh & Sutton 1996) and Science (Schultz et al.) landing pages were gated. I read the authors' own PDFs. The Singh & Sutton PDF is an image scan with no text layer, and the Sutton 1988 PDF has a garbled text layer, so quotes from both were read from the page images or checked by eye and should be re-read before printing in the article.
7. I did not open Dayan (1992), the proof that tabular TD(0) converges with probability 1, nor Jaakkola, Jordan and Singh (1994) or Tsitsiklis (1994). The convergence claim is cited through S&B p. 124 and p. 139, and Sutton 1988 Theorem 3 (p. 31) for convergence in the mean of linear TD(0). That is a repetition of the proofs, not a reading of them.
8. S&B states that every-visit MC "converges quadratically" (p. 93) without defining the term, and I found no gloss on it in Chapter 5. I would not repeat the phrase. The authoritative statements on every-visit MC are Singh & Sutton's (biased, consistent, lower early MSE).
9. Page numbers refer to the 2018/2020 PDF. I did not compare them with the MIT Press print edition. The printed page equals the PDF page minus 22.
10. The Schultz, Dayan and Montague result is recorded as a second candidate and I read it, but the opener should use only one real case. Their model is a hypothesis that fits the monkey recordings, and the authors list unresolved issues (p. 1598), so a sentence in the opener should say "the dopamine neuron data match" only with that scope.
11. The commission says lesson 1 did a one-cell MC average with a known model. I confirmed that from lesson 1's published text but did not read its script.
