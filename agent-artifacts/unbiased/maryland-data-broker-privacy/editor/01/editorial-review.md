## Neutrality (the load-bearing check)
Both positions are steelmanned from the record at equal strength, and the piece
reaches no house verdict. Position A runs one route: the statutory march from the
flat sale ban through the immigration-buyer rule to the warrant-not-subpoena
answer. Position B is not a strawman. It runs three independent doctrines the
record actually supports — the publicly-available carve-out, the DPPA and a
preemption argument, and Sorrell's speaker-based-scrutiny problem — and closes on
Penlink's and Thomson Reuters' on-record denials. The two sides are shaped
differently rather than mirrored, so the no-verdict close reads as the form, not
a hedge. Neither camp carries an adjective the other is denied. Each position is
voiced by a named holder: Laura Moy for A, Penlink and Thomson Reuters for B.

## Edits made directly
- Accuracy: the opening called the statute "three months on the books"; in force
  since July 1 against an August 21 dateline, that is seven weeks. Corrected, and
  now consistent with the dek.
- Neutrality/accuracy: the orientation claimed "two of its features... neither
  has a twin in another state's privacy law." A flat sensitive-data sale ban is
  not unique to Maryland, so the blanket claim overstated the statute's reach in
  Position A's favor. Reworked to attach the no-analog claim to the immigration
  provision alone, where NPR (s2) supports it, and cited it.
- Slop/heading: replaced the orientation heading "One statute, seven firms, and a
  definition doing the work" (a comma-and triad that only sounds comprehensive,
  plus the stock idiom "doing the work") with "The definition that decides how
  far the statute reaches," which names the section's real pivot.
- Slop/close: tightened the orientation's handoff, cutting the engineered
  "part ways on it before they part ways on anything else" doubling.
