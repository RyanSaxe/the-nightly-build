## The anchoring event (settled facts)

[s3, s4] On 2026-08-20 a coalition of twelve privacy and civil-rights
organizations, led by We Are CASA and represented by Georgetown University Law
Center's Technology Law Clinic (Prof. Laura Moy, director), filed a 29-page
consumer complaint asking Maryland Attorney General Anthony G. Brown to
investigate and enforce against seven companies. Coalition members include the
Center for Democracy & Technology and the Electronic Privacy Information Center.

[s3] Companies and allegations:
- Penlink — sells cell-phone location data through its Webloc program.
- Flock Safety, Motorola Solutions, Insight LPR — sell vehicle location data
  captured by automated license-plate readers (ALPRs).
- Thomson Reuters — CLEAR database; ALPR/vehicle-location and records tools; ICE
  contracts. [s10, s11]
- LexisNexis — Accurint database; records tools used by ICE. [s10]
- ThunderCat Technology — contract with Homeland Security Investigations (part of
  ICE) to supply Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN) data. [s3]

[s3] Penlink's statement to NPR: "The allegations against Penlink in the
complaint are false, as Penlink does not process or sell precise location data of
Marylanders in accordance with Maryland privacy law."
[s3] Thomson Reuters' statement to NPR: "We are confident that we are in
compliance with all applicable laws and regulations governing our business and
operations."

[s2] HB 711 passed the House 94-35 and the Senate 28-8; became law without the
governor's signature; effective 2026-07-01. Sponsors: Delegates Charkoudian,
Solomon, Wolek.

[s11] ICE's planned five-year, $125M sole-source CLEAR contract with Thomson
Reuters Special Services included nationwide license-plate/vehicle-location and
alerting tools; ICE later withdrew the sole-source award to compete it. (Use for
the factual backbone that these tools carry vehicle-location data to immigration
enforcement; note the withdrawal so the article does not overstate a live deal.)

## The statute, quoted (primary, s1)

Article — Commercial Law (as amended by HB 711):

- § 14-4707(a): "A controller may not: ... (2) Sell sensitive data".
- § 14-4707(a)(5): may not "Sell the personal data of a consumer if the
  controller knew or should have known that ... (II) THE PURCHASER SEEKS TO USE
  THE PERSONAL DATA FOR THE PURPOSE OF IMMIGRATION ENFORCEMENT".
- § 14-4707(a)(6): may not "KNOWINGLY SELL THE PERSONAL DATA OF A CONSUMER TO A
  FEDERAL, STATE, OR LOCAL GOVERNMENTAL UNIT THAT, WITHIN THE 6 MONTHS THAT
  IMMEDIATELY PRECEDE THE SALE OF THE PERSONAL DATA, HAS ENGAGED IN OR SUPPORTED
  CIVIL IMMIGRATION ENFORCEMENT THROUGH THE PROVISION OF PERSONNEL OR MATERIAL
  RESOURCES."
- § 14-4701(gg)-(hh): "sensitive data" includes, among enumerated attributes,
  "National origin", "Citizenship or immigration status", and "Precise
  geolocation data"; "sensitive data" means personal data that includes a
  sensitive attribute.
- § 14-4701(x): "'Precise geolocation data' means information derived from
  technology that can precisely and accurately identify, WITHIN A RADIUS OF 1,750
  FEET, the specific location of a consumer, A MOBILE DEVICE, OR A VEHICLE." (The
  capitalized words are additions HB 711 made; the prior text covered only "a
  consumer." The amendment expressly extends the term to a mobile device and a
  vehicle.)
- § 14-4701(w): "'Personal data' means any information that is linked or can be
  reasonably linked to an identified or identifiable consumer. ... 'Personal
  data' does not include: (i) De-identified data; or (ii) Publicly available
  information."
- § 14-4701(cc): "'Publicly available information' means information that a
  person: (i) Lawfully obtains from a record of a governmental entity [subject to
  the government's use restrictions]; (ii) Reasonably believes a consumer or
  widely distributed media have lawfully made available to the general public; or
  (iii) [obtains from a person to whom the consumer disclosed it, absent
  audience restriction]."
- § 14-4703(b)(8): exempts "Personal data collected, processed, sold, or
  disclosed TO THE EXTENT NECESSARY TO COMPLY AS REQUIRED BY the federal Driver's
  Privacy Protection Act of 1994".
- § 14-4712(a)(1): nothing in the subtitle restricts a controller's ability to
  "(I) Comply with federal, State, or local laws"; or "(II) Comply ... with ... a
  civil, criminal, or regulatory inquiry, investigation, subpoena, or summons ...
  EXCEPT TO THE EXTENT THAT AN INQUIRY, AN INVESTIGATION, A SUBPOENA, OR A SUMMONS
  PERTAINS SOLELY TO IMMIGRATION ENFORCEMENT"; or "(III) Cooperate ... with law
  enforcement".
- § 14-4712(a)(2)(I): the compliance safe harbor "DOES NOT APPLY IF A CONTROLLER
  OR PROCESSOR IS PRESENTED WITH A VALID WARRANT ISSUED BY A FEDERAL OR STATE
  COURT THAT PARTICULARLY DESCRIBES THE PERSONAL DATA TO BE ACCESSED." And
  (a)(2)(II): the subpoena/summons safe harbor "DOES NOT APPLY" to a subpoena
  from a governmental unit that supported civil immigration enforcement in the
  prior six months. (This is the "warrant, not subpoena" rule: for
  immigration-enforcement transfers, only a particularized judicial warrant lifts
  the bar.)

Article — General Provisions (as amended):
- § 4-320(g)(2) / § 4-320.1: a recipient of motor-vehicle personal information
  may not disclose it to a person or government agency "for the purpose of ...
  immigration enforcement unless the person is presented with a valid warrant
  issued by a federal court or a court of this State"; custodians must "deny
  inspection" to anyone seeking a record for immigration enforcement absent a
  particularized warrant.

## Position A — the sales violate the statute

Thesis: geolocation and ALPR-captured vehicle location are the sensitive
personal data MODPA bars selling outright; the immigration-buyer bar and the
warrant-not-subpoena rule independently reach these transfers; the AG should
enforce.

Claims and support:
1. Selling this data is banned on its face. Precise geolocation is a sensitive
   attribute [s1 §14-4701(gg)]; selling sensitive data is flatly prohibited [s1
   §14-4707(a)(2)]; MODPA "strictly prohibits the sale ... of sensitive data"
   [s9]. HB 711 extended "precise geolocation" to a mobile device and a vehicle
   [s1 §14-4701(x)], so cell-phone pings (Webloc) and ALPR vehicle location fall
   inside. No warrant or consent cures a *sale* of sensitive data; the ban is
   unconditional.
2. The immigration-buyer bar reaches sales to ICE and its supporters. A
   controller may not knowingly sell personal data to any governmental unit that
   in the prior six months supported civil immigration enforcement through
   personnel or material resources [s1 §14-4707(a)(6)], nor sell where the buyer
   seeks the data for immigration enforcement [s1 §14-4707(a)(5)(II)]. This is
   the provision that distinguishes Maryland from every other state law [s3, s8].
3. "We respond to lawful process" is not a defense here. The safe harbor for
   complying with a subpoena or summons expressly does not apply where the
   process pertains solely to immigration enforcement, or issues from a unit that
   supported such enforcement in the prior six months; only a particularized
   judicial warrant lifts the bar [s1 §14-4712(a)(1)(II), (a)(2)]. For DMV data
   the same warrant-not-subpoena rule applies [s1 §4-320.1]. State of
   Surveillance: the law "explicitly reject[s] detainers or administrative
   subpoenas," requiring "a warrant signed by a state or federal judge" [s8].
4. The factual predicate is documented. Thomson Reuters' CLEAR and LexisNexis's
   Accurint feed ICE [s10]; the planned CLEAR contract included nationwide
   vehicle-location tools [s11]; ThunderCat supplies ITIN data to HSI [s3].

Champion: Laura Moy, Professor of Law and Director of the Technology Law Clinic
at Georgetown Law, who authored the filing. Quoted in the coalition release:
companies "must not be allowed to profit off of giving agencies a 'data broker
loophole.'" [s4] Standing: she directs the clinic that built the legal theory and
filed the complaint.

## Position B — the sales are lawful, or the complaint overreads

Thesis: much of this data is "publicly available information" the statute
excludes from "personal data"; federal law authorizes the motor-vehicle
disclosures; brokers respond to lawful process; and a ban keyed to the buyer's
identity collides with the First Amendment.

Claims and support:
1. Publicly available information is outside the Act. "Personal data" does not
   include "publicly available information" [s1 §14-4701(w)(2)(ii)], defined to
   cover information lawfully made available to the general public [s1
   §14-4701(cc)]. A license plate displayed on a public road, read by a fixed
   camera, is arguably publicly available; if so, its sale is untouched by
   §14-4707. Penlink says its Webloc product "does not process or sell precise
   location data of Marylanders in accordance with Maryland privacy law" [s3] —
   i.e., its data is not the covered category.
2. Federal law authorizes the DMV disclosures. The DPPA bars a state DMV from
   disclosing motor-vehicle personal information but permits it "For use by any
   government agency, including any court or law enforcement agency, in carrying
   out its functions" [s6]. MODPA itself exempts data disclosed "to the extent
   necessary to comply as required by" the DPPA [s1 §14-4703(b)(8)]. Where the
   disclosure is federally authorized, the state ban is at least in tension and
   raises a preemption question.
3. The First Amendment protects selling truthful information. Sorrell held "the
   creation and dissemination of information are speech for First Amendment
   purposes" and struck a Vermont law that "imposes content- and speaker-based
   burdens on protected expression" under "heightened judicial scrutiny" [s5]. A
   Maryland ban that forbids sale to buyers identified by who they are
   (governmental units that support immigration enforcement) is a speaker-based
   restriction of the same kind, and faces the same scrutiny.
4. Brokers respond to lawful process and ALPRs serve legitimate policing. The
   statute's own text lets a particularized warrant lift the bar [s1
   §14-4712(a)(2)(I)]; the companies say they comply [s3]; Thomson Reuters "is
   confident" it complies [s3]. ICE reconsidered the sole-source CLEAR deal after
   industry feedback and moved to compete it, a sign the market and the agency
   are responsive to legal constraint rather than defiant of it [s11].

Champion: Penlink, a named respondent and the only firm selling cell-derived
(not just plate-derived) location. Its on-record denial to NPR disputes that its
product is the "precise location data" the statute covers [s3]. Standing: it is
one of the accused brokers and the company whose product most squarely tests the
"precise geolocation" definition.

## Enforcement path (for the close, not a verdict)

[s7] MODPA violations are enforced by the Attorney General as unfair trade
practices under Maryland's Consumer Protection Act, with a 60-day cure period.
The AG's first move is a classification call — is the captured data "sensitive"/
"personal" or "publicly available" — and that call decides most of the rest; the
First Amendment question waits behind it. No court has ruled; the AG has not
announced an investigation as of filing.
