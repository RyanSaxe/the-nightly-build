# Voice guide: unbiased/iran-war-powers

Register: house default (Hashimoto calm-precise, Olah/Weng patience), specialized
for constitutional-law argument. Reader relationship: an informed adult being
walked through a genuine doctrinal fork by someone who has read the primary law
closely enough to show exactly where it runs out, not someone refereeing a
partisan fight. The piece never tells the reader what to conclude, but it also
never goes soft to avoid concluding — every sentence commits to what the cited
law actually says, right up to the point where the law itself stops
committing.

Three moves change sentences here, beyond default steelmanning:

1. **Name the fact the legal conclusion turns on, don't resolve it.** Every
   contested doctrine in this record (is this "war," are these "hostilities,"
   was there an "attack on the United States") reduces to one open factual or
   definitional question. State that question as a question the sourced
   record leaves open, in the sentence where the doctrine needs it, not as a
   rhetorical shrug at the section's end.
2. **Fold the primary text into your own sentence grammar.** A clause of the
   Constitution, the WPR, or a holding does the arguing inside your sentence,
   not beside it in a block quote you then explain. This is how legal writers
   who don't tip make the record carry the weight instead of their own voice.
3. **Test every move for its structural twin.** Any limiting principle,
   exception, or piece of skepticism you apply to one position's strongest
   evidence gets the equivalent question asked of the other position nearby,
   in the same section. Symmetry is enforced sentence by sentence, not
   declared once in an editor's note.

## Licenses

```text
form: named pivot-fact
move: Goldsmith and Bellinger each isolate the single factual or definitional
      question a legal conclusion actually depends on, and say plainly that
      the record does not settle it, rather than letting the surrounding
      exposition imply an answer.
bar:  the named question must be one the cited primary sources genuinely
      leave open — not a hedge invented to sound balanced. State it once,
      in the paragraph doing that doctrine's work, and move on without
      resolving it. Reads as a finding, not a shrug.
```

```text
form: primary text as sentence spine
move: Ramsey (via Somin) and Bellinger quote a clause of the Constitution,
      the WPR, or a case holding as the grammatical subject or object of
      their own sentence, so the quoted words do the interpretive work
      instead of decorating a claim already made.
bar:  the quoted fragment must change what the sentence would otherwise
      have had to argue on its own. If the sentence's claim survives with
      the quotation deleted, the quotation was decoration and the license
      wasn't earned.
```

```text
form: matched skepticism
move: Egan and Bridgeman answer each side's strongest claim with the same
      depth and the same follow-up question, so a limiting principle raised
      against one position's reading gets its analogue raised against the
      other position's reading in the same stretch of prose.
bar:  any sentence that tests one side's exhibit against its weakest point
      needs its counterpart within the same section for the other side. A
      section that spends its skepticism on only one position has tipped,
      whatever the topic sentence claims.
```

```text
form: crisp admission of doctrinal indeterminacy
move: Goldsmith builds a doctrine up with real evidence, then punctures it
      with one short declarative sentence naming exactly what the law does
      not resolve, and moves on rather than adjudicating it for the reader.
bar:  earn it first — the buildup must show genuine work on both readings.
      The admission names the specific unresolved question in plain words.
      No more than once or twice in the piece, or it reads as a verbal tic
      rather than a finding.
```

Recently used, do not reuse:
- The "Everyone agrees X; the fight is Y" headline/framing mold (daylight-
  saving, college-athletes).
- The semicolon-reversal dek ("X did A; Y refuses B"), the suspended-question
  dek, and the comma-triad dek.
- Scaffolding section headings ("Background," "Implications," "Key
  Takeaways") or the template's own component vocabulary (position name,
  concise statement, argument area, holder) published as headings or
  explanatory prose.
- Whatever structural shape ai-training-fair-use used to tell its
  legal-doctrine split. Do not consult that article. The instruction from
  the brief is structural distinctness, not a specific shape to avoid —
  build this piece's shape from the Article I/II and WPR record itself.
- Any house catchphrase or reused closer/opener.

## Jack Goldsmith, "Was the Iran Strike Constitutional?"
Source: https://www.execfunctions.org/p/was-the-iran-strike-constitutional
Craft:
- cadence: alternates long, clause-stacked sentences that lay out a doctrine
  ("The Constitution gives Congress many war-related authorities...") with
  short, flat ones that puncture it ("But it doesn't."). The short sentence
  always follows real buildup, never substitutes for it.
- argument: diagnostic before pragmatic. Goldsmith establishes that the text
  and precedent are genuinely indeterminate before explaining why executive
  branch theory nonetheless reaches the strikes, then ends on the political
  constraint rather than a legal verdict.
- evidence: anchors to the one governing case (*Prize Cases*), the actual OLC
  "national interest" / "nature, scope, and duration" test quoted directly,
  and a catalogue of practice (Korea, Kosovo, Libya, Soleimani) offered
  without a ranking among them.
- stance: openly declines to answer the question he was best positioned to
  answer, and treats that refusal as the finding rather than a hedge.
- notice: the "qualifications and fudges" in OLC's own test are the tell he
  singles out — not that the executive branch's theory is wrong, but that it
  is too elastic to constrain anything.
- diction: technical terms (commander-in-chief, appropriations, gloss) used
  once and reused exactly, never varied for elegance.
- reader: assumes the reader can hold two live legal theories at once without
  being told which to prefer.
- the move the axes miss: he treats his own expertise as evidence of the
  question's genuine difficulty, not as license to rule. That is what keeps
  the piece from tipping despite forty years of him having a view.

## Brian Egan and Tess Bridgeman, "Top Experts' Backgrounder: Military Action Against Iran and US Domestic Law"
Source: https://www.justsecurity.org/64645/war-powers-trump-iran-strikes/
Craft:
- cadence: short, declarative, evenly weighted sentences. No sentence is
  built to be the memorable one.
- argument: organized by the questions a reader actually has (what can the
  president do alone, what has Congress already authorized, what tools does
  Congress hold), each answered by laying the competing readings side by
  side rather than by narrative.
- evidence: quotes the WPR's operative clause verbatim ("only pursuant to
  (1) a declaration of war, (2) specific statutory authorization, or (3) a
  national emergency...") and sets the executive branch's OLC practice
  directly beside it without ranking them.
- stance: descriptive throughout — "considerable debate," "the Executive
  Branch has not adopted a consistent view" — language that reports
  disagreement as a fact about the doctrine, not as an opinion about who is
  right.
- notice: states the constitutional design argument as a neutral fact about
  the framers' intent ("this is a feature, not a bug") rather than as
  praise or complaint.
- diction: plain, low-latinate, no legal jargon left unglossed.
- reader: a working journalist or staffer who needs to get the doctrine
  right on deadline, not a specialist being impressed.
- the move the axes miss: every question gets exactly one answer-length
  treatment regardless of how settled or contested it is. The piece never
  signals importance by giving one side more space, which is the real
  mechanism of its neutrality — not the hedged language, the equal word
  count.

## Ilya Somin (quoting Michael Ramsey), "Trump's Iran Air Strikes and the Constitution"
Source: https://reason.com/volokh/2025/06/22/trumps-iran-air-strikes-and-the-constitution/
Craft:
- cadence: long explanatory clauses resolving into a crisp conclusion
  sentence; technical vocabulary carried without apology.
- argument: originalist and text-first — establishes what "declare war" and
  "war" meant to the founding generation before applying that meaning to the
  present strikes, so the historical case is doing the work, not the
  present-day stakes.
- evidence: *Bas v. Tingy* and the Quasi-War practice, plus Washington,
  Hamilton, Madison, and James Wilson's own words on the war power, cited to
  show that even executive-power maximalists of the founding era accepted
  congressional primacy over initiating hostilities.
- stance: makes Position B's strongest textual case without softening it,
  but the confidence is earned by the density of sourcing, not asserted.
- notice: engages the small-scale-engagement exception seriously — the
  strongest available counterargument — and answers it on the specific
  facts (stakes, retaliatory capacity) rather than dismissing it.
- diction: precise, technical ("material initiation," "state of war")
  reused exactly rather than varied.
- reader: assumes familiarity with founding-era sources but glosses each
  case and figure enough that the unfamiliar reader isn't lost.
- the move the axes miss: he treats the counterargument as the load-bearing
  test of his own claim — the piece is only as strong as its answer to the
  best objection, and he puts that answer in the body, not a footnote.

## John Bellinger III, "Does Trump Have the Authority to Strike Iran?"
Source: https://www.cfr.org/articles/does-trump-have-authority-strike-iran
Craft:
- cadence: measured, medium-length sentences; no rhetorical peaks.
- argument: builds Position A's case from the actual constitutional grant
  (Article II, defense of persons and interests) and from bipartisan
  precedent, then names — in the same breath, not a caveat paragraph later —
  where prior presidents of both parties chose to seek authorization anyway.
- evidence: quotes his own prior OLC-adjacent formulations of the "national
  interest" standard folded directly into sentences, and cites the Gulf War
  and Iraq War as counter-precedent against his own side's strongest reading.
- stance: calls the question "highly debatable" and means it structurally —
  the piece's own architecture gives real space to the doctrine that cuts
  against the case it just built.
- notice: distinguishes the domestic-law question from the international-law
  question explicitly, so a reader can't mistake a win on one for a win on
  both.
- diction: institutional and exact (Article II, Take Care Clause, OLC)
  without lapsing into brief-speak.
- reader: a policy reader who wants the doctrine straight, not persuaded.
- the move the axes miss: even-handedness here is structural, not tonal — he
  places the historical counter-precedent inside the same paragraph as the
  claim it complicates, so it can't be skimmed past.

## Jack Goldsmith and Matt Gluck, "The War Powers Resolution and the Counter-Houthi Mission"
Source: https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/the-war-powers-resolution-and-the-counter-houthi-mission
Craft:
- cadence: expository sentences establishing doctrine, then a short
  applying sentence that measures the facts against it — repeated as a
  unit, doctrine-then-application, through the piece.
- argument: works the WPR's actual mechanics (the 60-day clock, the
  "hostilities" trigger, the reporting requirement) against one real set of
  strikes, showing what the statute settles and where it runs out, which is
  this article's own required contribution in miniature.
- evidence: quotes WPR Section 5(b) directly and measures the facts against
  its text clause by clause; uses the 2014 ISIL/AUMF episode as the nearest
  precedent and marks exactly where the current facts depart from it.
- stance: "it is not obvious to us," "this is a very plausible reading" —
  qualifies conclusions at the level of the specific claim, never as a
  general disclaimer.
- notice: flags that the WPR itself is "poorly drafted, loophole-filled" as
  a finding about the statute, not a complaint about the administration
  exploiting it.
- diction: statutory language quoted verbatim and then paraphrased once in
  plain words, never re-paraphrased a second way.
- reader: assumes the reader will follow a clause-by-clause statutory
  argument if each step is shown.
- the move the axes miss: uncertainty is dated and specific ("Congress on
  board by April 11 at the latest") rather than open-ended, so the piece's
  hedges read as legal precision instead of caution.

## Self-test
A writer following only the house default would produce a competent,
symmetrical two-position piece that never says anything false and never
commits to anything memorable — the default's steelmanning requirement
guards against unfairness, not against blandness. What this guide adds: the
named-pivot-fact move gives the writer a way to sound decisive about the law
without deciding the case, which the default's "cut the unearned verdict"
rule does not by itself teach how to do. The matched-skepticism bar
operationalizes "same standards to both" at the sentence level, catching a
piece that is symmetrical in space (equal word counts) but not in scrutiny
(one side's weak points are on the page and the other's are not) — a failure
mode the default names but does not test for. And the primary-text-as-spine
license is the specific antidote to this template's biggest risk: a
two-position piece that quotes the Constitution and the WPR as inert
decoration around the writer's own paraphrase tips toward whichever
paraphrase reads more persuasively. Grounding sentences in the statute's own
words is what keeps Position A and Position B equally load-bearing.
