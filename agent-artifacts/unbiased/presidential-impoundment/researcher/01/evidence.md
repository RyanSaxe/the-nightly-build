# Evidence record: unbiased/presidential-impoundment (01)

The record supports a strict two-position piece on the impoundment question and
confirms the commissioned anchor with one correction to the framing. The anchor
is real: the en banc D.C. Circuit, in a per curiam judgment **filed August 4,
2026** (not Aug 6, a summarizer error), held that EPA likely violated the law
by terminating and clawing back roughly $20 billion in already-obligated
clean-energy grants "based solely on a policy disagreement," because that
"likely contravened the IRA's mandatory appropriation." The correction the
writer must carry: the ruling's stated ground is the **Inflation Reduction Act's
mandatory appropriation**, *not* the Impoundment Control Act, and the court
fractured over a 2025 statute (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act) that repealed the
underlying program. So the case is the strongest *current* anchor for the
power-of-the-purse question, but it is not a clean impoundment holding, and its
fractured, repeal-entangled posture must be stated honestly rather than sold as
a square anti-impoundment win.

On the underlying legal merits the documented record leans clearly toward the
**power-of-the-purse / anti-impoundment** side: the Supreme Court (Train), the
statute's own text (ICA rescission/deferral procedures that forbid
policy-based deferral), repeated 2025 GAO decisions, and — most tellingly — a
1969 OLC memo by the future Chief Justice Rehnquist all reject a *general*
constitutional power to decline to spend. The executive-discretion side's
strongest, best-documented material is not court precedent but historical
practice (Jefferson, Grant, FDR, Kennedy) and a constitutional theory
("appropriations are a ceiling, not a floor") advanced chiefly by the current
administration's own officials. That asymmetry is the honest finding; the
steelman below gives the executive side its strongest primary form anyway, and
the record contains the one point where even the anti-impoundment authorities
concede ground (an appropriation ordinarily permits but does not by itself
compel spending absent a mandate).

Where the record is thin: I could not retrieve a stable primary of Louis
Fisher's own text (his site now redirects to an unrelated domain), so the
leading anti-impoundment scholar is anchored through his publisher, not his
words; and the original GGRF appropriation text is codified at a section now
repealed, so its dollar language is carried by the enacting court's citation
plus CRS/EPA figures rather than a live U.S. Code page.

## Contradictions

- **Decision date, Aug 4 vs Aug 6.** The opinion's own first page reads "FILED
  ON: AUGUST 4, 2026," Bloomberg reports "Tuesday" (Aug. 4), and the CourtListener
  storage path is dated 2026/08/04. The JURIST write-up's "August 6" is an
  outlier; the primary controls. Use **August 4, 2026**.

- **What the anchor actually holds.** The commission frames the ruling as the
  impoundment anchor, but the majority's stated ground is the IRA's *mandatory
  appropriation* and the APA, and it does not rest on the ICA. The ICA is the
  broader statutory frame (and the GAO decisions), not this opinion's ratio. The
  writer must not put an impoundment holding in the court's mouth.

- **The repeal cuts against a clean anti-impoundment reading.** Four judges would
  vacate paragraph two because the 2025 OBBBA repealed IRA Sec. 60103; even Chief
  Judge Srinivasan, who would otherwise affirm, treats the repeal as decisive
  going forward. So the case reinstates the block on a *policy-based clawback of
  already-disbursed funds* while leaving EPA's future authority post-repeal
  unresolved (an equally divided court). The "power of the purse" won the past;
  the future is undecided on this record.

- **Ceiling vs floor — where the sides genuinely meet.** The executive-discretion
  primary (Paoletta/CRA) says an appropriation authorizes but does not compel
  spending. The Rehnquist OLC memo — an anti-impoundment primary — CONCEDES that
  same point ("permits but does not require") before holding it is merely a rule
  of construction that cannot justify defying a statutory spending command. Train
  and the D.C. Circuit both turn on statutes they read as commands. The real
  dispute is therefore narrower than the slogans: not whether every appropriation
  is a floor, but whether *these* appropriations were mandates and whether any
  residual constitutional impoundment power survives a clear one.

- **History, contested.** Paoletta/CRA read Jefferson, Grant, FDR, Truman, and
  Kennedy as exercises of constitutional discretion; Price (Stanford) reads the
  same episodes as statutory or interpretive, with Nixon the lone
  constitutional claimant who lost. Two readings of one factual record; the
  writer should present the episodes as facts and the constitutional
  characterization as the contested question.
