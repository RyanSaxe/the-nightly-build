# Evidence record: unbiased/mail-in-voting-order (01)

The evidence firmly establishes the anchor and the legal posture, and it complicates
the commission's framing in one load-bearing way. The primary record is complete and
was read in full: the Supreme Court's August 24, 2026 per curiam order in *Trump v.
California*, No. 26A124, with both dissents (Sotomayor joined by Kagan; Jackson);
Executive Order 14399; the Solicitor General's stay application (D. John Sauer); and
Judge Talwani's June 25, 2026 summary-judgment opinion and permanent injunction,
which is reproduced in the application's appendix. The central factual finding to hold
onto: **the August 24 order is a stay on justiciability grounds only — standing and
ripeness — and is not a merits ruling on presidential authority.** The Court said so
itself, the Solicitor General never argued the merits, and no court that has reached the
merits has upheld the order. The two positions are genuinely contested, but the
disagreement the order actually sharpens is *when* states may challenge such a federal
directive (and, underneath it, the still-unadjudicated question of whether the President
has any authority here) — not a decided contest that the President won on the merits.
Where the evidence is thin: there is no credible named holder defending the *merits* of
presidential authority over mail-ballot mechanics, because none of the order's defenders
(the SG, the intervenor states, the majority) defended the merits — they defended
prematurity. The federal-authority side's strongest evidence-backed case is the
justiciability case, plus the President's own Article II assertion in the EO. This is
flagged again in Contradictions and in the report.

## Contradictions

1. **The order's ground vs. the commission's framing (most important).** The commission
   frames the live question as "Can the President, by executive order, impose nationwide
   restrictions on mail-in voting for a federal election?" and casts Position A as: the
   order "rests on that authority" and "the Court properly stayed an injunction that was
   overbroad or premature." The record supports the *premature* half but not the *rests
   on that authority* half. The August 24 order decided **only** justiciability
   (standing/ripeness); the majority wrote that its disposition "does not mean that any
   measure taken by the Government to implement the Order will necessarily be lawful."
   The Solicitor General's application argued prematurity, **not** the President's
   authority. Both dissents and the district court stress that no court has upheld the
   order's legality. Net: the Aug 24 order is not a merits win for presidential
   authority, and the strongest evidence-backed federal-side case is the
   justiciability/prematurity case (plus the President's own Article II assertion in the
   EO), not a defended merits theory of election power. The writer must not present the
   order as deciding the constitutional question.

2. **Is the EO a harmless "internal directive" or a mandatory overhaul?** This is a
   genuine factual/textual dispute, not just values. The majority and the SG read the EO
   as internal guidance that "imposes no obligations on the States" and leaves agencies
   discretion; §2(a) lists are optional, §2(b) merely sets prosecutorial priorities, §3
   is only a directive to *propose* a rule. The dissents (Sotomayor at 1–3; Jackson at
   3–5) and the district court read §2 as a unified whole in which states that refuse the
   citizenship lists face a "credible threat" of prosecution — quoting the Government's
   own Reply that a state's "refusal to consider" a list "could... be relevant evidence
   of an intent to violate... the federal laws... under Section 2(b)." Sotomayor: the
   Government "cannot have it both ways" — calling implementation too speculative to
   confer standing while asserting it must implement immediately to show irreparable harm.

3. **Standing now vs. later, and the tension with precedent.** The majority rests on
   *Trump v. New York* and *Clapper* (injury too speculative before a final rule).
   Jackson argues those are inapposite because the EO sets firm deadlines and USPS
   actually issued a proposed rule (June 2, 2026), and that the ruling is in "obvious
   tension" with last Term's *Bost v. Illinois Bd. of Elections*, which found candidates
   have standing to challenge election-rule changes on fair-election interest alone. This
   is a live doctrinal disagreement the writer can present as the real merits of the Aug
   24 order.

4. **Scope: "nationwide" is imprecise.** The commission says the order took effect
   "nationwide." The injunction the Court actually stayed (Talwani's June 25 permanent
   injunction) was limited to the 23 Plaintiff States and D.C. (App. 61a–64a). A
   *separate* nationwide injunction from the League of Women Voters suit (Talwani, Aug
   11) remained in place after the stay (Votebeat), and as of Aug 28 a new TRO again
   blocked the USPS final rule pending a Sept 3 hearing. So the stay did not, by itself,
   let the order operate nationwide for November. The writer should say precisely which
   injunction was lifted.

5. **Reporting/label conflicts.** Hasen's Election Law Blog headline (403-gated; not
   citable here) and one Votebeat line call the stayed injunction a "preliminary
   injunction"; the primary order and the SG appendix make clear it was the June 25
   *permanent* injunction / final judgment (App. 61a–64a). Use the primary. NPR's
   auto-extract attributed an unverified "painful setback" quote to NY AG James — not
   confirmed; do not use it.
