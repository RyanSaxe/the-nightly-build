# Voice guide: unbiased/mail-in-voting-order

## How this piece should sound

This is a two-position brief on one contested question: whether the President
can, by executive order, impose nationwide restrictions on mail-in voting for a
federal election. The paper reaches no verdict, so the writing carries one
discipline above the rest. A reader should finish unable to tell which side the
writer holds, and every calibration here serves that.

Voice each position the way Amy Howe voices the two advocates in Chatrie. State
the case for the executive order in the terms its serious holders use, drawing on
the administration's stated rationale and the Solicitor General's filing, and
state the case against it in the terms its holders use, from the Elections Clause
commitment of the "times, places and manner" of federal elections to the states
and Congress. Prefer the holder's own quoted words to your paraphrase, and keep
the flat attribution Howe keeps: "argued," "countered," "contended." A position
given at full strength in a named person's own language is harder to slant than
one you summarize.

Build the two cases to the same scale, as Howe's paired paragraphs and Elwood's
petitioner and Colorado paragraphs are built. If one side gets three sentences of
its strongest argument and the other gets one, the imbalance reads as a judgment
though no word of it is stated. Match the specificity as well as the length: if
you quote the district court's opinion and Justice Jackson's dissent for the
challengers, quote the order and the government's filing for the defenders at the
same depth.

Apply scrutiny to both sides, and let it fall evenly. Howe reports the justices
pressing the government hard on the church-and-political-organization worry
directly after she has given the government its strongest line. Pressure that
reaches the position which seems, for a moment, to be winning is what keeps the
piece level. When you surface the weakest point in one side's case, look for the
weakest point in the other's.

State the procedural posture exactly, the way Taraleigh Davis states it. The
August 24 order granted a stay. It let the executive order take effect for the
November election while litigation continues, and it allocated the risk in the
interim rather than deciding the constitutional question. Davis's line on what a
stay pending certiorari does and does not signal is the model for that care. Hold
the stay, the district court's injunction, the underlying merits, and the
ultimate constitutional question apart as four separate things, so that no
sentence implies the Court settled the merits when it granted the stay.

Keep the political story at arm's length, as Davis does when he shows a
cross-ideological coalition and declines to sort the justices into teams. The
disagreement here is real and is held on both sides for reasons of constitutional
structure. Name the two positions the way their serious holders would, and let
that naming resist the default political labels. Loaded framing usually enters
through the verbs and the modifiers: one side that "claims" while the other
"explains," an order called "sweeping," a challenge called "activist." Read your
attribution verbs and your adjectives for any lean before you settle them.

Let each side's labels and internal reasoning come from the record. Elwood's two
paragraphs answer each other point for point because the briefs do, and the
constitutional text, the order, and the filings should decide how each argument
runs here. The reader should come away able to state each side's strongest case
in its holders' own terms, no closer to guessing your own view at the last
sentence than at the first.

## Amy Howe, "Justices appear mixed on whether geofence warrant violated the Fourth Amendment"

Source: https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/04/justices-appear-mixed-on-whether-geofence-warrant-violated-the-fourth-amendment-/

> Representing Chatrie, lawyer Adam Unikowsky argued on Monday morning that
> "[t]he government conducted a search" of Chatrie's location history, and that
> the geofence warrant "that purported to authorize that search violated the
> Fourth Amendment." Chatrie, Unikowsky contended, had a reasonable expectation
> that his location data would be kept private, "given both its sensitive and
> revealing nature and the fact that it was stored in his password-protected
> account."

Howe states the petitioner's case in the advocate's own words, quoting the exact
phrases he reached for and adding only neutral attribution, "argued" and
"contended." The reader gets the strongest form of Chatrie's position and no sign
of what Howe makes of it. She is visible in the restraint: she reports the claim
in full and stops.

> Deputy U.S. Solicitor General Eric Feigin, representing the federal government,
> countered that Chatrie was "asking for an unprecedented transformation of the
> Fourth Amendment into an impregnable fortress around records of his public
> movements that he affirmatively consented to allow Google to create, maintain,
> and use."

The government's answer comes in the very next paragraph, at the same length and
again in its lawyer's own image, the "impregnable fortress." The two paragraphs
are built the same way, so neither reads as the favored one: the same class of
verb ("countered" beside "argued"), the same reliance on direct quotation, the
same absence of comment. The evenness is a choice Howe makes sentence by
sentence.

> Roberts worried aloud that, if the government were to prevail, it could use
> geofence warrants "to find out the identities of everybody at a particular
> church, a particular political organization."

Here Howe reports the bench testing the government's side, quoting Roberts'
concrete worry rather than characterizing it. She presses the position that had
had the better of the earlier exchanges, and she does it with the same flat verb,
"worried aloud," that she uses everywhere else. The scrutiny does not follow a
preferred outcome.

## Taraleigh Davis, "What really happens on the emergency docket"

Source: https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/04/what-actually-happens-on-the-emergency-docket/

> Justice David Souter was more expansive: "Without going beyond John's [John
> Paul Stevens'] memo, it strikes me that the conclusion reached by the Supreme
> Court of Wisconsin is open to serious challenge, but I doubt that I will vote
> to grant if the State files a petition for cert." In other words, Souter
> thought the Wisconsin court may have gotten the law wrong, but doubted the case
> was significant enough for the Supreme Court to take up.

Davis restates a justice's position precisely, separating two things a reader
could easily blur: whether the lower court erred on the law, and whether the case
merits the Court's time. The gloss stays inside what Souter's memo said and adds
no verdict of its own. Davis is visible in the care taken to keep those two
questions apart.

> A stay granted pending disposition of a petition for certiorari carries an
> implicit signal: that the court found the underlying legal question
> sufficiently worthy of Supreme Court review to preserve the status quo (there,
> keep Moeck in custody) while it considers whether to take up the case. That is
> the natural reading of the public order in Moeck.

This is the procedural posture stated exactly. Davis names what a stay pending
certiorari does, hold the status quo and mark the question as possibly
review-worthy, and by naming only that he keeps it distinct from a ruling that
anyone was right on the merits. Precision is all the sentence is doing, and he
reaches it without commenting on the case.

> This is perhaps especially interesting because the vote itself defies any
> simple ideological story. The five justices who voted to grant Wisconsin's
> emergency application, keeping the defendant in custody, were Rehnquist,
> Kennedy, O'Connor, Ginsburg, and Stevens. The four who voted to deny, which
> would have freed Moeck, were Thomas, Scalia, Breyer, and Souter.

Davis lays out who voted which way and what each vote would have done in concrete
terms, "keeping the defendant in custody" and "would have freed Moeck," then lets
the roster show the coalition crossing the usual lines. He declines the
ready-made narrative and adds no interpretation the names do not already carry.
The writer is present in the refusal to sort the justices into teams.

## John Elwood, "'Universal' pre-K causes court to re-re-reconsider major religious precedent"

Source: https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/04/universal-pre-k-causes-court-to-re-re-reconsider-major-religious-precedent/

> Petitioners press three questions. First, they contend that the 10th Circuit's
> decision deepens a 7-4 circuit split over what kinds of secular exemptions
> render a law "not generally applicable" under Smith – specifically, whether
> courts must evaluate all secular exemptions and discretionary carve-outs, or
> whether only "unfettered" discretion and exemptions for "identical" secular
> conduct count. Second, petitioners argue the 10th Circuit has joined a growing
> number of courts in misreading the 2022 case of Carson v. Makin as prohibiting
> only explicitly religious exclusions, even though Carson holds that
> conditioning a public benefit on willingness to abandon religious exercise
> triggers the highest form of scrutiny regardless of whether the exclusion is
> expressed in religious terms. Third – and most ambitiously – petitioners urge
> the court to overrule Smith entirely.

Elwood states the petitioners' case at its full reach, in their own doctrinal
vocabulary, the circuit split, the reading of Carson v. Makin, the request to
overrule Smith. He keeps the technical terms intact because they are the
argument, and he flags the third question as "most ambitiously" without hinting
whether it should win. Elwood is visible in how completely he is willing to state
a case he does not adopt.

> Colorado's brief in opposition insists there is no circuit split to resolve
> here because the 10th Circuit held as a threshold matter of state statutory
> interpretation that Colorado's equal-opportunity requirements allow no
> exceptions whatsoever: the provisions petitioners point to as "secular
> exemptions" (for children with disabilities and children from low-income
> families) represent instead targeted protection for those groups, not
> carve-outs from the nondiscrimination mandate. As for Carson, Colorado
> maintains that the program does not exclude religious providers at all; it
> welcomes them, and conditions participation only on compliance with the same
> neutral, generally applicable requirement imposed on every provider. And
> Colorado asserts that "nothing has changed" that would warrant overruling
> Smith.

The opposing brief gets a paragraph built to the same scale and the same
specificity, tracking each of the petitioners' three points and answering it on
its own terms. Elwood gives Colorado its threshold statutory argument, its
reading of Carson, and its position on Smith, with the same neutral verbs,
"insists," "maintains," "asserts," he could have turned on either side. Read
beside the paragraph before it, neither one carries a tell.

> The solicitor general's office has a reputation for splitting the difference in
> its amicus briefs, and there is some evidence of that here. The government ends
> its brief by saying that "resolving the question presented as to general
> applicability could obviate any need to resolve at this juncture whether Smith
> itself retains vitality, as petitioners urge in their third question
> presented."

Elwood characterizes the government's posture precisely, a brief that offers a
narrower ground and steps back from the broadest question, and quotes the
sentence that shows it. He names the move, "splitting the difference," as a known
habit of the office rather than as praise or complaint. The reader learns exactly
where the government stood and is left to judge it.
