# Voice guide: unbiased/ai-training-copyright (01)

## Amy Howe, "Supreme Court likely to discard Chevron"

Source: https://www.scotusblog.com/2024/01/supreme-court-likely-to-discard-chevron/

> "It has been nearly 40 years since the Supreme Court indicated in Chevron v. Natural Resources Defense Council that courts should defer to an agency's reasonable interpretation of an ambiguous statute. After more than three-and-a-half hours of oral argument on Wednesday, it seemed unlikely that the rule outlined in that case, known as the Chevron doctrine, will survive in its current form. A majority of the justices seemed ready to jettison the doctrine or at the very least significantly limit it."

This is the opening of a report on a case still undecided, and every load-bearing verb is hedged to match: "seemed unlikely," "seemed ready ... or at the very least." Howe is visible in the discipline of that hedging — she commits to what the argument showed (a majority leaning one way) without committing to what the Court will do. The scene-setting sentence earns its place by dating the doctrine and naming the case, not by throat-clearing.

> "Justice Neil Gorsuch told Prelogar that he was less concerned about businesses subject to changing regulations, observing that the companies 'can take care of themselves' and seek relief through the political process. Instead, Gorsuch pointed to less powerful individuals who may be affected by the actions of federal agencies, such as immigrants, veterans seeking benefits, and Social Security claimants. In those cases, Gorsuch stressed, Chevron virtually always works for the agencies and against the 'little guy.'"

Every claim here is tied to the person who made it, in the sentence that makes it — "Gorsuch told," "Gorsuch pointed," "Gorsuch stressed" — so the reader never has to guess whether this is Howe's inference or the justice's own argument. The short quoted phrases ("can take care of themselves," "little guy") are doing the characterizing work instead of an adjective Howe would have had to supply herself.

> "Martinez rejected any suggestion that the Chevron doctrine should be limited but not overruled. The justices, he said, should 'recognize that the fundamental problem is Chevron itself.' By the time the session drew to a close, it seemed — but was not completely clear — that a majority of the justices agreed with him."

The piece ends on a claim about where the argument landed, not a claim about who was right, and the parenthetical hedge — "but was not completely clear" — is doing real work: it tells the reader exactly how much confidence the reporting supports, rather than rounding a strong impression up to a certainty.

## Jack Goldsmith, "The Tariff Case and the Major Questions Doctrine"

Source: https://www.execfunctions.org/p/the-tariff-case-and-the-major-questions

> "Courts sometimes tie the clear congressional authorization requirement, as here, simply to whether the asserted authority has vast economic consequences. But that factor alone does not a MQD case make. As Curt Bradley and I explained, the Court 'in practice looks to a variety of factors — including the breadth of the claimed authority, the history and novelty of the agency action, persistent congressional inaction, and other contextual clues about congressional intent — to determine whether agency action is "major" and thus demands clear congressional authorization.'"

Goldsmith states the legal test in its full, multi-factor form before he uses any of it, including the version that would complicate his own argument later ("that factor alone does not ... make" a case). Naming the factors precisely, rather than gesturing at "the doctrine," is what lets a reader who knows nothing about major-questions law follow exactly what is and is not being argued.

> "The language of IEEPA is undeniably broad on its face. It lists a host of powers — some (such as prohibition and prevention) even more restrictive than tariffing. There is no suggestion that the IEEPA-specified authority must be exercised only for specified types of products or only for a narrow set of countries. The facially evident intent is to provide flexibility in the tools available to the President to address the unusual and extraordinary threats specified in a declared national emergency."

This is Goldsmith quoting the dissent at length rather than paraphrasing it into a clause of his own. The choice to run several sentences of someone else's reasoning verbatim, instead of compressing it to "the dissent argued the statute was broad," lets the reader judge the argument in the words its author actually chose.

> "But I make no predictions. The consequences of this case are too big in too many directions — a win or a loss for Trump has massive economic and political consequences, not to mention important legal implications for future presidencies. I do not know how the Court will process these factors; but it will surely not ignore them."

Goldsmith spends the whole piece arguing which reading of the statute is better, then closes by refusing to convert that into a forecast of the outcome. The two are kept visibly separate — an assessment of the legal merits on one hand, an acknowledgment of what he cannot know about the institution deciding the case on the other.

## Bob Bauer, "A Disagreement about the Tariff Case and the Major Questions Doctrine"

Source: https://www.execfunctions.org/p/a-disagreement-about-the-tariff-case

> "Jack Goldsmith has critiqued here the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit's decision on the Administration's comprehensive program of tariffs imposed pursuant to the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA). He focuses in particular on the court's application of the 'major questions doctrine,' or MQD, which requires clear congressional authorization for 'highly consequential' exercises of executive authority 'beyond what Congress could reasonably be understood to have granted.' He has found the court's reasoning on this question to be 'inadequate' and the dissent to provide a more reliable 'guidepost' to the Supreme Court majority's approach. At the same time, Goldsmith declines to predict how the Supreme Court will rule."

Bauer opens a rebuttal by restating the piece he's rebutting so accurately that Goldsmith's own words ("inadequate," "guidepost") carry the summary. A reader who had never seen the first piece would still know exactly what claim is about to be contested, in terms its author would recognize as his own.

> "In this short reply, I don't take issue with either Goldsmith's sense of the Court majority's thinking or any of his uncertainty about the outcome of the case. I do, however, have reservations about his evaluation of the merits of the Federal Circuit's treatment of the MQD issue. I have trouble taking the majority's position to be as inadequate on the law as he does."

Before disagreeing, Bauer marks off what he is not disputing. This is the opposite of a general "on the other hand": it names two specific claims he accepts and isolates the one he doesn't, so the disagreement that follows can be read as narrow and specific rather than a wholesale rejection.

> "However, Dames & Moore could also be cited for the proposition that courts have approached these issues with marked uneasiness about throwing the doors wide open to presidential claims of emergency authority. Dames & Moore upheld the power of two presidents (Carter and Reagan) to proceed under IEEPA to dispose of claims against Iran in connection with the agreement for the release of American hostages. Nevertheless, as Jack notes, the Court drew lines, rejecting the government's position that IEEPA authorized presidents to direct suspension of legal claims against Iran."

Bauer is reading the same precedent Goldsmith cited and finding it cuts partly the other way, but he credits Goldsmith's own point inside the sentence ("as Jack notes") rather than only inside his own argument. The disagreement is built on top of the shared fact, not instead of it.

## How this piece should sound

This is a two-position explainer for a reader who already knows how to evaluate an argument and wants the actual structure of this one — the compensation-and-consent case and the transformative-use-and-access case, each at its strongest, with nothing settled between them. The unbiased template already forbids a house verdict; the craft question these three exemplars answer is how a piece manages to sound authoritative and precise without ever supplying one.

Each side's claim should arrive attached to the person or institution making it, the way Howe writes "Gorsuch told Prelogar" or "Martinez rejected" rather than describing what "critics of the ruling" think. Where the piece states a legal test or an economic mechanism a side depends on — a fair-use factor, a statutory-damages calculation, a licensing-market argument — Goldsmith's move of quoting the framework in full before doing anything with it is worth following: state the test the way its own proponents would state it, in their vocabulary, before the piece turns to what the other side does with the same facts. When one side's number or precedent looks like it complicates the other side's claim, Bauer's habit of marking exactly what is and isn't in dispute — crediting the specific point that stands before adding to it — keeps a contested figure from reading as a rebuttal of everything around it.

The hardest sentences to write in this piece will be the ones at the seam between the two positions, where an unbiased piece is most tempted to lean. Howe's "seemed — but was not completely clear" and Goldsmith's decision to separate his read of the legal merits from any prediction of the outcome are both models for that seam: report what the record shows about where an argument stands or what a court filing concedes, without rounding that up into who has the better case. The declared reader here has the background to follow a fair-use test or a per-work damages figure stated in its own terms; the piece can use that vocabulary exactly, the way Goldsmith uses "major questions doctrine" and Howe uses "Chevron doctrine," rather than softening the terms of art into something vaguer for safety. Named sources should do the arguing throughout — a scholar's own sentence, a filing's own language, a developer's own brief — so that by the end the reader has watched two real positions contest a real question, not a summary of a fight the piece declined to show.
