## What the evidence supports and where it is thin (updated for pass 02)

The record firmly establishes: the exact settlement terms and figures (owned by
the court order itself); the fair-use legal architecture as one federal judge
has actually applied it, factor by factor, to training copies versus pirated
library copies (owned by Alsup's order); the Copyright Office's own extensive,
factor-by-factor institutional analysis and its explicit refusal to recommend
legislation (owned by the Office's Part 3 report); and named, quoted positions
from plaintiffs, the Authors Guild, the AAP, Anthropic, and multiple legal
scholars and economists on both sides. It is thinner on: the *outcome* of
Concord II and other still-pending suits (a motion to dismiss was filed August
3, 2026 and is unresolved; no ruling or hearing has occurred as of this
writing); and on independent verification of the private Malackowski and
Peterson expert declarations, which are known to the record only through Judge
Alsup's order and one piece of legal-affairs journalism, not the underlying
sealed filings.

The previously-thin point on the Copyright Office's institutional standing is
now closed: the removal of Register Shira Perlmutter is independently
documented (two non-party, reputable secondary sources, cross-checked against
a Congressional office's contemporaneous statement) as occurring **the day
after** the Part 3 report's pre-publication release — the report was released
May 9, 2025, and Perlmutter was terminated by White House email on May 10,
2025. The article's "the next day" framing is accurate; "within days" would
understate the record and should not be used as an alternative. No source
establishes a causal link as fact (only that critics, including the House
Judiciary Committee's ranking member, asserted one, and the Copyright Office
report itself is silent on it) — the record supports the sequence and its
timing, not a proven motive, and the piece should not claim more than that.

Separately, the Concord II motion-to-dismiss date is now corrected against the
court's own docket: Anthropic (and, in a separate filing, Dario Amodei
individually) filed partial motions to dismiss on **August 3, 2026** (a
Monday), not "August 5-6." The trade-press source cited in pass 01 reported on
the filing on August 6, and pass 01's paraphrase conflated the coverage date
with the filing date; that conflation is what produced "August 5-6" in the
draft. The docket also shows something no prior source in this record
established: a motion hearing is calendared for **November 4, 2026**, before
Judge Eumi K. Lee in San Jose — so as of today (August 14, 2026) the motion is
filed but not yet argued. Any clause describing the motion as "argued" in
early August should be corrected to "filed."

## Pass 01 (preserved)

### Step zero: timeliness and two-sidedness

The commission's proposed anchor and framing hold, and current reporting makes both sharper.
The freshest live hook is **Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín's July 20, 2026 order granting final
approval** of the $1.5 billion settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, No. 4:24-cv-05417-AMO
(N.D. Cal.) — the largest copyright class-action settlement on record. As of today (August 14,
2026) that order is inside its Ninth Circuit appeal window (the district court's own order notes
distribution begins only after the appeal period runs), so the case is not merely history; it is
still capable of being disturbed. Independently, the same defendant is fighting a second,
unresolved copyright suit over the same underlying legal question: *Concord Music Group v.
Anthropic PBC* ("Concord II"), a $3 billion suit by music publishers over song lyrics, in which
Anthropic filed a motion to dismiss reported on August 5–6, 2026 — inside the reporting window
this piece is written in. The fight is not resolved; it is ongoing in a second forum right now.

The fight is genuinely two-sided, and the record refines the commission's framing rather than
undercuts it. The commission asked whether the live question is "should unlicensed use ... be
priced and deterred through copyright's damages machinery, or does that outcome misprice a use
closer to learning." Judge Alsup's June 23, 2025 fair-use order already forces exactly that
sharper question by *splitting* the underlying conduct: training Claude's models on lawfully
acquired books was ruled "exceedingly transformative" fair use, while acquiring and permanently
retaining a library of pirated books was ruled *not* fair use on every factor, with trial ordered
on damages. The settlement resolves only the piracy-damages half of that split, and by its own
terms does **not** release claims about AI outputs or future conduct — so the transformative-use
question Alsup already answered for Anthropic remains open industry-wide (Concord II, and the
still-pending suits by other authors, publishers, and news organizations against other AI firms,
carry it forward). This is the live fight: not "is training fair use," which one judge has now
answered for lawfully sourced material, but whether the piracy-driven per-work damages machinery
that just produced a $1.5 billion number is the right lever to govern the training-data economy
going forward, or whether it prices a learning-like activity in a way that will not scale and
will freeze out everyone but the largest incumbents. Adopt the commission's framing as stated,
sharpened by these facts.

### What the evidence supports and where it is thin (pass 01, superseded by the updated paragraph above — kept verbatim for traceability)

The record firmly establishes: the exact settlement terms and figures (owned by the court order
itself); the fair-use legal architecture as one federal judge has actually applied it, factor by
factor, to training copies versus pirated library copies (owned by Alsup's order); the Copyright
Office's own extensive, factor-by-factor institutional analysis and its explicit refusal to
recommend legislation (owned by the Office's Part 3 report); and named, quoted positions from
plaintiffs, the Authors Guild, the AAP, Anthropic, and multiple legal scholars and economists on
both sides. It is thinner on: the *outcome* of Concord II and other still-pending suits (motions

Malackowski and Peterson expert declarations, which are known to the record only through Judge

on the Copyright Office's own institutional standing, since the Register who issued the report
was removed the day after its release, a fact every side of this fight cites selectively and
that a careful writer should flag rather than let either side use unqualified.

### Contradictions

```text
Where sources disagree with one another or the commission:

1. What the settlement actually proves. Anthropic (Sridhar, NPR July 2026) frames the $1.5B
   payment as compatible with — even alongside — the claim that "training AI on books is fair
   use under copyright law." The Authors Guild (Rasenberger) and AAP (Pallante) frame the same
   payment as vindication of the broader claim that "AI companies cannot simply steal authors'
   creative work" and must "pay for the books they use." Both readings are literally consistent
   with Judge Alsup's order: he ruled training on *lawfully acquired* books was fair use and
   piracy was not, and the settlement resolves only the piracy claims. Neither side's public
   framing states that limitation; a careful piece should hold both to it rather than let either
   side's quote imply the settlement resolved the training question generally.

2. Whether a licensing market for training data actually exists at the needed scale. Anthropic's
   economist Steven Peterson: the "hypothetical competitive market" for training-scale licenses
   "would be impracticable" (TechPolicy.Press, quoting the sealed declaration). The plaintiffs'
   economist James Malackowski, cited repeatedly in Judge Alsup's order as "Opp. Expert
   Malackowski," took the contrary position that a market for licensing books for LLM training was
   already emerging; Judge Alsup wrote on summary judgment that "[o]ur record could support either
   account — so this order must assume Authors are correct" (Alsup order at 28), i.e., he did not
   resolve the factual dispute, he construed it against the moving party at the summary-judgment
   stage. The Copyright Office's own Part 3 report (pp. 85-94) documents real licensing deals in
   music, stock photography, and news, but explicitly declines to conclude the market can scale to
   "all AI training needs" (p. 85), leaving the underlying empirical dispute open rather than
   settled by either side's economist.

3. Good faith and its relevance. The order Anthropic now cites for "training is fair use" is the
   same order that quotes CEO Dario Amodei preferring to "steal" books to avoid "legal/practice/
   business slog," and that describes Anthropic's piracy as knowing and, potentially, willful
   (triggering up to $150,000-per-work statutory exposure under 17 U.S.C. § 504(c)(2) had the case
   gone to trial rather than settled). Judge Alsup held bad faith was not dispositive to the fair-use
   analysis under Warhol — but it was material to why the piracy half of the case was not excused,
   and to the settlement's own reasoning that continued litigation carried "the possibility of an
   adverse ruling" on willfulness (settlement order at 11-12). A piece that quotes only Anthropic's
   present-tense "fair use... remains the law today" without this context understates how contested
   and fact-specific that claim already was inside the very case Anthropic won on training.

4. The Copyright Office's own institutional standing is itself contested. The Part 3 report was
   released as a "pre-publication version" on May 9, 2025; Register of Copyrights Shira Perlmutter
   was removed from her post the next day, a sequence widely reported (see, e.g., contemporaneous
   coverage cited in secondary sources during research) and read by some commenters as evidence the
   report's fair-use-skeptical framing displeased the administration, and by others (e.g., EFF, in
   a piece titled "The U.S. Copyright Office's Draft Report on AI Training Errs on Fair Use")
   as itself too favorable to copyright owners. Both camps in this dispute cite the report
   selectively as neutral government authority; a careful piece should note the report is the
   Office's own analysis, not binding law, and was issued amid unusual controversy over the
   Office's independence.
```
