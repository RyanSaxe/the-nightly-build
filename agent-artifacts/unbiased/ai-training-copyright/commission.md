# Commission: unbiased/ai-training-copyright

## The contested question and its timely anchor

The subject is the reckoning over training generative-AI models on copyrighted
work without a license. The disagreement is genuinely two-sided, consequential,
and central to this paper's beat, and it has a concrete US anchor: the wave of
authors-and-publishers litigation against AI developers, and in particular the
settlement and statutory-damages exposure that has now put a price on unlicensed
training copies (the Anthropic/Bartz class action is the largest such US case on
the public record, reportedly valuing the copied works in the low thousands of
dollars each across hundreds of thousands of works).

The researcher confirms the freshest timely hook from current original US
reporting before the piece commits. The most likely live question, given the
record, is not "is training ever fair use" in the abstract but the sharper one
the settlement forces: **should unlicensed use of copyrighted works to train
commercial AI be priced and deterred through copyright's damages machinery, or
does that outcome misprice a use closer to learning and choke off beneficial AI
and open research?** If August-2026 reporting supports a sharper or different
live framing of the same fight, adopt it and keep the two-position structure. If
the researcher finds the fight is not genuinely two-sided or lacks a timely US
anchor, stop and report the strongest alternative timely US disagreement found,
so this article can be re-commissioned rather than forced.

## The two positions (to be filled by the evidence, not prejudged)

Each side must be a real camp with named champions, its own thesis, and its
strongest sourced arguments, presented so its own adherents would accept it.
Steelman both before any weighing; the template carries no house verdict.

- The compensation-and-consent side: unlicensed copying of expressive works to
  build commercial systems is infringement; creators are owed consent and
  payment; a real price deters the largest unlicensed copying. Champions to
  source from the actual record: author organizations (e.g., the Authors Guild),
  named plaintiff authors, and legal scholars who defend the infringement
  reading.
- The transformative-use-and-access side: training is closer to reading and
  learning than to copying a work's expression; a damages regime priced per work
  overprices at scale, is infeasible to license, transfers little to most
  creators, and entrenches incumbents while chilling open models and research.
  Champions to source: named AI developers' legal positions, fair-use scholars
  (the field has prominent voices on this side), and economists or technologists
  who have argued the access case.

## Boundaries and required contribution

- Strict template: orientation section plus exactly two flex position-sections,
  each built from the `nb-side-*` components, per-section citation. No verdict.
- Source floor is hard: at least 10 sources, at least 4 primary and at least 3
  secondary. Primaries are the court filings, opinions, the settlement record,
  the Copyright Act provisions at issue, the US Copyright Office's AI report, and
  named scholars' or parties' own writing. Secondary reporting supplies context.
- Keep the event US-based; position-holders and evidence may come from anywhere.
- The article's contribution is a fair, sourced map of a fight most coverage
  flattens to "authors vs. robots," giving a technical reader the actual legal
  and economic structure of each side.
