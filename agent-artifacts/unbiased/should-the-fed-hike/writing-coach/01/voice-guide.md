# Voice guide — unbiased/should-the-fed-hike

## Directive

Register: a calm institutional analyst who has read the FOMC statement, the
dissenters' own speeches, and the inflation data, and now explains what each
camp actually believes and why a serious person could hold it. Not a referee
summarizing a scoreline, not an advocate for either camp. The reader relationship
is a briefing, not a pitch: you are arming a numerate non-economist to judge the
argument themselves, so every claim earns its place by adding something they
need to do that, never by nudging them toward a verdict.

- **Define each term of art the sentence needs, inline, in plain mechanism, and
  move on.** "The real interest rate — the policy rate minus expected inflation —
  is what actually restrains borrowing" teaches once and never again. Re-defining
  a term the reader already has is the tell of an explainer padding itself. State
  it, use it by its one name for the rest of the piece.
- **Give each `nb-side` its own reasoning chain, built from what its holder
  actually argues, not a slot-for-slot mirror of the other side's structure.**
  The raise-now case runs on expectations dynamics and the cost of delay; the
  hold case runs on shock diagnosis and policy lags. If both sides' paragraphs
  could swap orders and read the same, the argument was written to a template
  instead of to the evidence. Symmetry belongs to the space and scrutiny each
  side gets, never to the shape of the reasoning.
- **Fold a holder's quote into the sentence that needs it, not into a
  press-release blockquote.** Quote the clause that carries the claim's own
  reasoning, attribute it by name and role in the same breath, and keep your
  sentence doing the connecting work before and after. A quote that could be
  deleted without changing the paragraph's logic was decoration.
- **Let the orientation supply only what both sides need to be understood: the
  vote, the dissent, the inflation record, the shock.** It reports; it does not
  preview an answer. If a sentence in the orientation would only make sense once
  the reader already knows which side you favor, cut it.
- **Write findings as findings and reasoning as reasoning, and never let a verb
  do the hedging.** "Officials worry" is reported if a named official said so;
  otherwise it is a guess in a trench coat. State what the data shows, what a
  named person argued from it, and where those two stop being the same thing.
- **Vary sentence length by the weight of the claim, not by a house rhythm.**
  A short sentence lands a number. A longer one, kept to one clause per idea,
  carries a mechanism through its steps. Neither side should read more
  confident than the other because its sentences happen to be shorter.

## Recently used, do not reuse

- No hedged-contrast dek mold: no semicolon reversal ("X holds; Y hikes"), no
  suspended question ("...and the real question is whether"), no comma triad
  closed with "and." One clean sentence, a stance on the disagreement itself,
  not a topic label.
- No component vocabulary as headings or prose: never write "camp," "thesis,"
  "argument," or "holder" on the page. Name each side and heading in the
  question's own nouns.
- No heading built as two clauses joined by a comma and "and." Vary the shape
  heading to heading.
- No manufactured punchline ("that's the whole point," "the catch is," any
  "X is the whole Y" construction) closing a section or the piece.
- No smuggled conclusion: no adjective, adverb, or final clause that tips which
  side's case reads stronger. The last sentence of each `nb-side` ends that
  side's argument, not the piece's.
- No false symmetry: don't pad the thinner case with restated evidence or trim
  the stronger one to match. Give each exactly what the record supports.

## Exemplars

## Council on Foreign Relations, "What Is the U.S. Federal Reserve?"
Source: https://www.cfr.org/backgrounders/what-us-federal-reserve
Craft:
- cadence: short declarative openers, then one dependent clause that does the
  defining work and stops.
- argument: builds from institutional fact to mechanism to consequence, never
  doubling back to re-explain a term already set.
- evidence: cites the statute and the historical episode (Volcker, the 1970s)
  rather than asserting the mechanism abstractly.
- stance: expository, no house position, because a backgrounder's only job is
  to leave the reader able to follow the live debate elsewhere.
- notice: flags the trade-off (recession now for credibility later) without
  ruling on whether it was worth it.
- diction: plain nouns for policy tools, no adjectives smuggled in to signal
  approval or disapproval.
- reader: assumes curiosity, not economics training; earns every acronym.
- missed axis — economy of definition: it defines federal funds rate once,
  inline, with an em-dash, and every later use of the term rides on that one
  sentence. No term gets defined twice.
Calibration: "A central tool in this process is the federal funds rate—the
rate at which banks borrow and lend to each other overnight."

## Joseph Brusuelas (RSM US), "A hawkish Fed holds rates steady amid growing dissent"
Source: https://realeconomy.rsmus.com/a-hawkish-fed-holds-rates-steady-amid-growing-dissent/
Craft:
- cadence: reports the vote first, then widens to the argument underneath it,
  sentence by sentence rather than in a preamble.
- argument: names the specific mechanism a hold requires believing — that a
  central bank can "look through" a supply shock — instead of asserting
  "the Fed is being cautious."
- evidence: ties the argument to the vote count and to what the dissenting
  bloc has been saying in public, not to the economist's own priors.
- stance: analytical and willing to name a tension (an inflation target
  serious people now question) without resolving it for the reader.
- notice: catches that the hawkish minority is growing, not just present, and
  says so with the count rather than an adjective like "significant."
- diction: technical terms (real yields, supply shock) appear once, used
  precisely, never restated for emphasis.
- reader: a professional audience, so mechanism is stated at full speed with
  no hand-holding — the model for the register, not the assumed background.
- missed axis — earned tension: the piece treats the political friction (a
  hawkish Fed against a White House that wants lower rates) as evidence of the
  decision's difficulty, not as a verdict on who is right.
Calibration: "the nature of the supply shock" gives the committee "space to
keep the policy rate on hold" because it can "look through such shocks."

## NPR, "Federal Reserve holds interest rates steady despite high inflation"
Source: https://www.npr.org/2026/07/30/nx-s1-5912203/federal-reserve-holds-interest-rates-steady-despite-high-inflation
Craft:
- cadence: decision, then pressure, then the chair's own words, then the
  market's skepticism — each beat one paragraph, no beat repeated.
- argument: builds the case for hiking through three separate channels (market
  pricing, the dissent count, the inflation record) instead of one summary
  line.
- evidence: quotes the chair directly on the committee's internal disagreement
  rather than paraphrasing it into "officials were split."
- stance: reports tension without adjudicating it, letting the market's
  skepticism stand next to the chair's confidence unresolved.
- diction: concrete numbers throughout (gasoline "back above $4 a gallon")
  instead of "elevated" or "high."
- reader: general audience; explains the September meeting's stakes without
  assuming the reader already follows FOMC calendars.
- missed axis — the asymmetry to avoid: the hold case is never argued in its
  own right, only assumed as the default the hike case pushes against. That is
  the exact failure this brief's two `nb-side` sections must not repeat — each
  side needs its own affirmative case, not one case and one rebuttal.
Calibration: "There was a lot of agreement that I heard that we have the
powers, the tools, also the authority to deliver stable prices."
