# Evidence — unbiased/should-the-fed-hike (01)

The core factual scaffolding in the commission checks out against the Fed's own
record: the July 29, 2026 FOMC statement, read directly, confirms the 9–3 vote,
the 3‑1/2 to 3‑3/4 percent target range, and the three named dissenters (Beth M.
Hammack, Neel Kashkari, Lorie K. Logan), each recorded as preferring a 1/4
percentage point hike. Both positions are backed by strong, direct, cited
statements from named holders: Lorie Logan's own July 16, 2026 speech
(dallasfed.org) is an unusually complete public record of a hawkish dissent
rationale, and Chair Kevin Warsh's press-conference transcript plus on-record
quotes from named economists (Mark Zandi of Moody's Analytics, former Fed Chair
Janet Yellen) give the hold side comparably strong material. One correction the
writer must build around: **the commission and brief both assume Chair Jerome
Powell gave the press conference. He did not.** Powell's term as Chair ended;
Kevin Warsh took the oath of office and was unanimously selected FOMC chairman
on May 22, 2026 (federalreserve.gov), and it was Warsh, not Powell, who ran the
July 29 meeting and press conference. This is verified directly against the
Fed's own site, not just reporting, and is flagged in full under Contradictions
below.

The record is thinner on one thing the brief hoped for: a clean, quotable
"hiking would be a mistake because this is a supply shock" line *from Warsh
himself*. His own transcript answers are more equivocal — he leans on financial
markets doing tightening work for the Fed and calls the hold "watchful
thinking, not watchful waiting," while personally sounding hawkish about
inflation credibility ("There is no soft inflation target... not on this
Committee's watch"). The clean, textbook articulation of the hold argument
(supply shock, monetary policy is the wrong tool, would risk the labor market)
comes more crisply from named economists than from the Chair's own words. Both
are included below so the writer can decide how to weight them; neither should
be put in the other's mouth.

The exact FOMC statement text attributes elevated inflation to "supply shocks
that have driven price increases in certain sectors, including energy" — it
does not use the word "tariff" in that sentence. Tariffs enter the picture
through Warsh's spoken opening remarks (which name tariffs alongside energy,
pandemic supply-chain strain, and AI capex as one of several shocks) and
through named-economist analysis (Moody's Analytics apportions roughly 0.66
points of year-end forecasted inflation to the Iran war and 0.17 points to
tariffs). This distinction — statement language vs. spoken remarks vs.
economist modeling — is precise enough that the writer should not casually
attribute "supply shocks... including energy" (the statement's own words) to
tariffs without sourcing that add-on separately.

## Contradictions

**Powell vs. Warsh (commission/brief assumption vs. verified fact).** The
commission and brief both instruct sourcing "Chair Jerome Powell's cited
statement" for the hold position. This is factually wrong for the July 29,
2026 meeting. Verified directly against the Fed's own press release (source
#4): Kevin Warsh took the oath of office as Chairman on May 22, 2026, after
Senate confirmation May 12–13, 2026, following nomination by President Trump
on March 4, 2026. Warsh, not Powell, ran the July 28–29 FOMC meeting and gave
the July 29 press conference (confirmed directly in the transcript, source
#3, and in every secondary source that names the meeting's chair). Powell was
still chair as recently as March 18, 2026, per contemporaneous reporting of an
earlier press conference (not separately verified here as it falls outside
this meeting's evidence, but the transition date is unambiguous from source
#4). The writer must use Warsh, not Powell, as the named holder for any
institutional/majority quote tied to this specific meeting.

**Warsh's own rationale is not a clean textbook "supply shock, don't hike"
argument.** The brief's Position B description (tariff/energy as
relative-price shocks that a rate hike "cannot fix and would worsen") is a
real, well-documented position — but it is best evidenced by named economists
(Zandi, Yellen, Roth, Goldman Sachs; source #8) rather than by Warsh's own
words. In the transcript, Warsh acknowledges supply shocks exist and "make
this job... a little tougher" but frames the hold primarily around not
overreacting to one data point, letting market-driven tightening do work
between meetings, and an active internal debate ("watchful thinking, not
watchful waiting") rather than a settled anti-hike doctrine. He explicitly
declines to characterize the case for hiking as wrong: "I'm going to let... the
dissenters speak for themselves." He also strikes a hawkish tone on the target
itself ("no soft inflation target"). The two "camps" as they actually spoke
are less symmetric in tone than the brief's framing implies — the writer
should represent Warsh's actual words rather than assign him the cleaner
economist argument.

**Tariffs are not in the FOMC statement's own attribution sentence.** The
statement's exact language is "supply shocks that have driven price increases
in certain sectors, including energy" (source #1) — it names energy but not
tariffs in that clause. Tariffs appear as one of several shocks in Warsh's
*spoken* opening remarks (source #3: "substantial increases in tariff rates")
and in Logan's own speech (source #5: "Developments such as tariffs and
conflict in the Middle East have raised inflation over the past 15 months")
and in economist modeling (source #8: Moody's attributes roughly 0.17 points
of forecasted year-end inflation to tariffs, versus roughly 0.66 points to the
Iran/energy shock — tariffs are the smaller of the two named drivers per this
one estimate). The commission's "tariff- and energy-driven price pressure"
framing is well supported across sources, just not by the four-sentence
economic-assessment paragraph of the statement alone.

**No contradiction found on the vote, range, or dissenter identities.** All 14
sources that address these facts agree without exception: 9-3 vote, 3-1/2 to
3-3/4 percent target range, Hammack/Kashkari/Logan as the three dissenters,
each preferring a 25bp hike. This is unusually well corroborated for a
single-meeting event.

**"Most dissents since September 2016" is accurately characterized, with a
precise historical nuance.** Source #11 (Tech Times) is the only source read
here that explains the 2016 comparison in detail: the September 2016 dissent
was also three regional presidents in the same (hawkish) direction. The
distinguishing feature reporters emphasize is not just headcount but unity of
direction and unity of timing — three presidents dissenting together, at the
same meeting, all preferring the same 25bp move. The commission's phrasing
("the most dissents in one direction since September 2016") is consistent with
this.
