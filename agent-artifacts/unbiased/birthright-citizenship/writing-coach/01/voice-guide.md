## How this piece should sound

This piece turns on one phrase in the Citizenship Clause, "subject to the jurisdiction thereof," and the orientation should give the reader that phrase, in the text it comes from, before it gives them either side's reading of it. Amy Howe does exactly this with the elections clause in the Moore v. Harper passage below: name the disputed clause, quote its own words, and only then hand the same clause to the two sides that read it differently. The Citizenship Clause deserves the same treatment once, early, so a reader meets "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" itself before the piece asks them to follow anyone's argument about what it means.

Howe's other habit worth carrying over is attaching every claim to the party that makes it, in that party's own brief, rather than to a floating "critics say" or "scholars believe." The commission already names the position-holders this piece needs — the plaintiffs and state attorneys general challenging the order, the administration and DOJ defending it, named scholars on each side — and Howe's flat, repeated verbs ("tell the justices," "argue," "explain," "say") are the model for keeping each claim traceable to the person who holds it, without the verb itself tipping which claim the piece finds more persuasive.

Precedent here is lopsided in a specific way: more than a century of Wong Kim Ark against a textual reading that credentialed scholars press in good faith. Howe's sentence that "the Supreme Court has never endorsed the independent state legislature theory in a majority opinion" shows how to state that kind of fact — once, plainly, as a fact about where the law currently stands, not as a verdict on which side should win. The piece can afford a sentence exactly that direct about Wong Kim Ark's holding and about how lower courts have so far ruled on the executive order, without that sentence also grading the argument it leaves on weaker footing.

Because the whole dispute hangs on what "jurisdiction" meant to the people who wrote it, Haley Proctor's habit of naming two distinct ideas before either does any work in her argument — the fixation thesis and the constraint principle, defined together, in one place — is worth borrowing directly for "subject to the jurisdiction thereof." Full political jurisdiction and jurisdiction as owed allegiance are not the same claim, and separating them once, by name, before either side's argument leans on the distinction, is what lets a reader keep track of which side is using which sense of the word for the rest of the piece.

Richard Re's essay on the amber metaphor never says whether the Court should have reached for it. It traces who used the phrase first, against whom, and what each later use did with it, and stops there — the interpretive work is in the tracing, not in a verdict laid on top of it. The orientation's own history — Wong Kim Ark, Elk v. Wilkins, and the drafting-era statements from Senators Howard and Trumbull — wants that same discipline: report what each source actually said or held, in the order each side reaches for it, and let the reader watch both sides return to the same 1898 opinion and the same 1866 floor debate without the piece leaning on the scale either time.

This is a longread with an orientation and two full side sections, which is more room than a single case preview like Howe's gets for any one clause. Where she compresses a side's textual and historical argument into a paragraph apiece, this piece can let the text, Wong Kim Ark, and the drafters' debate each earn their own development inside a position before the next ground opens. That room is for building out what each side's strongest form actually requires — not for saying the same settled point again in different words once it has already landed.

## Amy Howe, "In high-stakes election case, justices will decide validity of 'independent state legislature' theory"

Source: https://www.scotusblog.com/2022/12/in-high-stakes-election-case-justices-will-decide-validity-of-independent-state-legislature-theory/

> The independent state legislature theory rests on two provisions of the Constitution. The provision directly at issue in Moore, Article I's elections clause, says that the "Times, Places and Manner" of congressional elections "shall be prescribed in each State by the Legislature thereof." Similarly, Article II's electors clause says that states shall appoint presidential electors for the Electoral College "in such Manner as the Legislature thereof may direct." Proponents of the theory argue that, under the plain text of those two clauses, state courts are not authorized to supervise how state legislatures run elections for Congress or the president. Critics argue that neither clause was ever understood – including at the time of the Founding — to confer such unchecked authority on state legislatures.

Howe names the theory and quotes the two clauses it rests on before she paraphrases either side's reading of them, so the reader meets the actual constitutional text first. The last two sentences hand that same text to "proponents" and "critics" in matched grammar and the same verb, "argue," so neither reading arrives pre-weighted. Nothing in the paragraph signals what Howe herself thinks the clause means; the work is entirely in the attribution.

> In their brief on the merits, the Republican legislators – led by Timothy Moore, the speaker of the state's house of representatives – tell the justices that the question before them can be resolved based on the text of the Constitution. In the elections clause, they argue, the use of the phrase "the Legislature" means that only the legislature has the power to regulate elections, with no role for state courts.

The claim is traced to a named person and his exact title, and to the specific brief it comes from, so it belongs to someone rather than to "proponents" in the abstract. The verb Howe chooses, "tell," is plain and stays plain; she doesn't reach for "insist" or "claim" later in the piece when the same legislators make their next point.

> North Carolina and the original challengers to the Republican map offer a different interpretation of the text of the elections clause. Although a "legislature" is the representative body that makes the laws, they explain, when the U.S. Constitution was drafted it was also "universally understood" that the state legislature was created by the state constitution. Therefore, they say, the laws that the legislature passes – including election laws – must comply with the state constitution, and state courts can step in to enforce the limits imposed by the state constitution.

This side gets the same sentence structure and the same flat verbs — "explain," "say" — as the legislators did two paragraphs earlier, even though it appears second and closes the point. The paragraph states what this side's brief says the framing generation understood, in its own terms, without Howe asserting that understanding as her own finding.

## Richard Re, "A court trapped in amber"

Source: https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/08/a-court-trapped-in-amber/

> In dissent, Justice Thurgood Marshall, joined by Justice William Brennan, wrote: "But 'constitutional concepts of equal protection are not immutably frozen like insects trapped in Devonian amber.'" The quotation there is from Dillenburg v. Kramer, an influential 1972 U.S. Court of Appeals for the 9th Circuit ruling by Judge Shirley Hufstedler. In deeming the issue of felony disenfranchisement serious enough to convene a three-judge court, Hufstedler had argued that the equal protection clause encoded a dynamic principle whose implications could change over time, particularly with respect to voting rights. All three of these jurists – Marshall, Brennan, and Hufstedler – were leading liberals of the era.

Re traces the metaphor to three named judges, with a year and a case for each use, before adding the one fact that explains why they reached for it. That identifying detail — "leading liberals of the era" — comes after the sourced claim, not instead of it, so the reader has the citation before the interpretation built on it.

> The amber metaphor is powerful not just because it is vivid, but also because of its ambiguity. Of course, any metaphor invites interpretation. But the amber metaphor is extra indeterminate because the court deploys it negatively, yielding an indirect argument. The court tells us what the law is not, without quite telling us what it is.

Re states plainly what the metaphor does — tells the reader what the law is not, without saying what it is — rather than reaching for a judgment about whether that is good or bad legal reasoning. The sentence stays with the mechanics of the phrase itself.

> Because the amber metaphor operates indirectly, it invites the reader to imagine its antithesis. Presumably a law that is not "trapped in amber" is free and at large. It may be capable of moving, growing, reproducing, learning – even stinging. It is not just observed and beautiful but doing things. It is alive.

Re works out the metaphor's opposite one implication at a time — moving, growing, reproducing, learning, stinging — before naming what they add up to. He earns the final word, "alive," by building toward it rather than asserting it first and illustrating afterward.

## Haley Proctor, "What is 'trapped in amber'? An originalist explanation"

Source: https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/08/what-is-trapped-in-amber-an-originalist-explanation/

> Properly understood, originalism indeed holds that law is fixed – "trapped in amber," if you will. But the circumstances to which it applies are not. The problem with the amber metaphor is that courts increasingly use it without distinguishing law from its application.

Proctor states her own reading of the metaphor in one direct sentence and immediately names the specific confusion she thinks causes the trouble: courts not separating law from its application. The claim is committed to, not hedged into vagueness, and the sentence that follows tells the reader exactly what the rest of the piece has to do.

> As Barrett explained in her Rahimi concurrence, originalism "is built on two core principles: that the meaning of constitutional text is fixed at the time of its ratification and that the 'discoverable historical meaning … has legal significance and is authoritative in most circumstances.'" For short, scholars refer to these commitments as the "fixation thesis" and the "constraint principle."

Before using either term again, Proctor quotes the concurrence that supplies the working definition and names both terms in the same sentence, side by side. A reader can now hold the two ideas apart because each one got its own label, introduced once, together, rather than picked up piecemeal as the piece goes on.

> What is not "trapped in amber" is law's application in future circumstances. Here is how the court made the point in New York State Rifle & Pistol Ass'n v. Bruen: "Although its meaning is fixed according to the understandings of those who ratified it, the Constitution can, and must, apply to circumstances beyond those the Founders specifically anticipated." The court goes on, as it did in Rahimi, to note that the Second Amendment protects "modern instruments that facilitate armed self-defense," even if such instruments did not exist at the time of ratification. In short, to an originalist, the law is trapped in amber; its application is not.

Proctor quotes the case that supports her distinction before compressing the whole argument into a single closing sentence. The compression works because the reader has just seen the source it's drawn from; the short sentence is a summary of what came before it, not a substitute for it.
