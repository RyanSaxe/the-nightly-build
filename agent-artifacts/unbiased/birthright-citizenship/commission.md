## The contested question

Does the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause guarantee citizenship to
essentially everyone born on US soil, or does "subject to the jurisdiction thereof"
let the political branches deny it to the children of parents who are in the country
unlawfully or only temporarily? State it neutrally in the headline. This is a live
constitutional dispute driven by executive action and active litigation, not a
settled question with a fringe: one side rests on more than a century of precedent,
the other is argued in good faith by credentialed constitutional scholars and is
the basis of a sitting administration's policy.

## The timely event

The question is timely because of a current US event the researcher must pin down
precisely as of 11 August 2026: the Trump administration's executive order(s)
directing agencies to deny citizenship documentation to certain US-born children,
and the litigation around it (the nationwide-injunction posture after the Supreme
Court's 2025 decision in Trump v. CASA, and whatever merits ruling, cert grant, or
new order exists by that date). Establish the exact current posture from the court
record and reputable US reporting, and correct anything this commission states
loosely. A new headline alone is not enough; anchor the piece to the specific
recent development that reopened the fight.

## The two positions (frame, do not pre-judge)

Present each at full strength with its named holders and its best evidence, and be
honest that the precedent is lopsided even while the textual argument is seriously
advanced.

- Position one: the Clause guarantees near-universal birthright citizenship, so the
  executive order is unconstitutional. Grounds to develop from the record: the text
  ("All persons born or naturalized in the United States, and subject to the
  jurisdiction thereof"); United States v. Wong Kim Ark (1898) and its holding as to
  children of resident aliens; the common-law jus soli backdrop and the framers'
  debates; and the uniform district- and circuit-court injunctions against the order.
- Position two: "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" excludes children who owe
  allegiance elsewhere, so the political branches may narrow birthright citizenship.
  Grounds: the original-meaning reading of "jurisdiction" as full/allegiance-owing
  jurisdiction; the Wong Kim Ark dissent and the Elk v. Wilkins line; the drafters'
  statements (e.g. Senator Jacob Howard, Senator Lyman Trumbull) that the framers'
  camp reads as excluding aliens and foreigners; and the argument that the question
  as applied to unlawfully-present parents was not squarely decided by Wong Kim Ark.

Name real position-holders with standing and their exact titles: the plaintiffs and
state attorneys general challenging the order and their counsel; the administration
and DOJ; and constitutional scholars on each side (record affiliations exactly).
Cite the courts, the constitutional text, and the congressional record as
primaries, never as the house's own verdict. Steelman both. The piece takes no side
and reaches no verdict; the orientation gives the reader the context to weigh them,
including an honest statement of where precedent stands.

## Sources (strict)

order and the litigation, and scholarly commentary on each side. Classify each by
authorship and stake, not domain. Every URL resolves to the source's own page.

## Required contribution

The reader finishes able to state the question precisely, to see exactly which words
of the Clause and which precedents each side relies on, and to understand why the
"subject to the jurisdiction thereof" phrase carries the whole dispute. The piece
does what the sources alone do not: it sets the two readings of that phrase side by
side on the same constitutional text and record, precedent weighed honestly.
