# Commission: opinion/vaccine-schedule-order

## Assignment

One argued column for the Opinion desk. Template `opinion` (min 8 sources; the
`nb-position` card pins the paper's position at the top, and a counter section
giving the strongest opposing case its own terms is mandatory). Authorized
scheduled work from `nb duty`: open slot, one article. The paper takes a side and
can be held accountable for being wrong.

## The occasion and the position

On 11 August the administration issued an executive action narrowing the federal
childhood vaccine schedule to a named set of diseases and moving the rest to a
case-by-case decision with a physician, and the signing remarks invoked a
vaccine-autism link. That order is the occasion; the day after it is signed is when
an opinion desk weighs in. The Current Events brief reported the order as news. The
column's job is the argument the report did not make.

The position to argue, precisely: the order is indefensible on the two grounds
that matter for a policy that changes what shots children get. It rests on a
causal claim (vaccines and autism) that the evidence has closed, and it substitutes
executive fiat for the evidence-based, deliberative body the schedule is supposed
to run through. Argue the second ground as hard as the first, because it is the one
a reader who already knows the autism science has not been given: what the schedule
is, how it is normally set (the CDC's Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices
and its evidence review), what statutory and procedural authority the order does
and does not have to override that process, and why the mechanism of the change is
itself the problem. The writer determines the exact thesis sentence and the
article's shape from the evidence; this commission fixes the position and its two
load-bearing grounds, not the wording.

## What the column must do

Make the position precise and connect it to the public record: the order's actual
text and named scope, the normal schedule-setting process it bypasses, and the
state of the autism evidence. Show what follows if the paper is right (what changes
for coverage mandates, insurance, school requirements, and disease risk). Give the
strongest opposing case its own section in its own terms before answering it —
parental informed consent, the legitimacy of debating the timing or spacing of
specific vaccines, and distrust of a single centralized schedule are the honest
steelman, and the answer must engage them, not a strawman. Close on the evidence
that would change the paper's judgment: state the specific finding or procedural
fact that would make the paper wrong. Analysis is earned from cited evidence;
steelman before weighing; keep reported fact, estimate, and synthesis distinct.

## Boundaries with the rest of tonight's edition

The Current Events brief reported this order as an event on 11 August and is
instructed not to re-lead it today. This column owns the argument. Do not recap the
day's news; assume the reader knows the order was signed and spend the space on the
case. No other piece in tonight's edition touches this subject.

## Sources

`min_sources` 8. Anchor to: the executive order or proclamation text itself; the
CDC/ACIP child and adolescent immunization schedule and a description of the ACIP
evidence-review process and its statutory footing; the strongest primary evidence
closing the vaccine-autism question (the 2019 Annals of Internal Medicine Danish
cohort of 650,000+ children; the Institute of Medicine / National Academies
reviews; the retraction record of the 1998 Wakefield paper); the American Academy
of Pediatrics response; legal or administrative-law analysis of executive
authority over the immunization schedule and the Administrative Procedure Act
questions; and a credible source for the downstream consequences (coverage under
the ACA's preventive-services and Vaccines for Children mechanisms). Steelman
sources for the counter are required, not optional. Every URL resolves to the
source's own page.

## Required contribution

A reader who already accepts that vaccines do not cause autism still leaves with
something new: a clear account of how the childhood schedule is actually set, why
setting it by executive order rather than through the evidence-review body is the
deeper defect, what concretely changes downstream, and the exact evidence that
would change the paper's mind. The column earns accountability by naming that
evidence.
