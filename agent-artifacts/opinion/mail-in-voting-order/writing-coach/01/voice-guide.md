# Voice guide — opinion/mail-in-voting-order (01)

## Directive

Register: calm and precise, argued from structure and the record. Adjectives
never carry weight a citation should carry. When a paragraph starts to feel
forceful, check whether the force comes from the verb and the quoted material
or from a modifier stacked in front of them — cut the modifier.

Reader relationship: the position card has already disclosed the stance, so
write as if arguing to a reader who disagrees but is arguable with, not to a
jury already won. No paragraph should restate where the piece lands; each one
moves the argument one step further than the last paragraph left it.

Opening: because the verdict is disclosed before the prose starts, the
opening's job is to establish the mechanism the whole piece turns on — who
holds the power and under what clause — not to re-litigate whether the policy
is wise or to narrate the week's news. Let the constitutional text or a
court's own holding do the opening lift rather than a scene or a recap
sentence.

Building each argument section: write like counsel assembling a record, not
a reporter summarizing one. Quote the operative language — the order's own
text, a holding's own sentence, the clause itself — then spend one or two
sentences on what the quoted words do before moving to the next authority.
Precision of citation should replace certainty of tone; a quiet sentence can
still land hard if what it quotes is exact.

Steelman: write the counter in the vocabulary its own holders use for their
own reasoning — executive administration of federal law, an accurate
citizenship list, delegated Article II authority — never in the vocabulary
the piece's own position would choose to describe them. Give the counter its
own paragraph before any rebuttal appears; a counter that starts dying in the
sentence that states it was built to fall.

Close: end on what the argument earned, not a summary of it. Name, in one or
two concrete sentences, the specific ruling or statute that would flip the
judgment. No hedge standing in for that specificity, and no restatement of
the stance dressed as a conclusion.

Banned tells, and the specific risk here: caricature — every sentence naming
the other side's holders should be one their own side would sign; unearned
certainty — "clearly," "obviously," and "of course" standing in for an
argument the sentence hasn't made; and treating a jurisdictional question as
a partisan one — no vocabulary that sorts people by side. Write "the
executive branch" and "the states," not political labels. A reader should
not be able to guess the writer's ballot from the diction alone.

Recently used, do not reuse: the "Congress already wrote the rule" framing
that ran the last three Opinion pieces (end-the-emergency-tariffs,
saudi-nuclear-enrichment, france-under-15-social-media-ban). No
colon-subtitle headline. Vary this piece's section shapes from that run —
don't default to the same setup-counter-close skeleton twice in a row.

## Steve Vladeck, "177. The (Not-So-)Interim Docket"
Source: https://www.stevevladeck.com/p/177-the-not-so-interim-docket
Craft:
- cadence: a long sentence assembles the record, then a short sentence lands
  the point; the short sentences do the arguing, the long ones do the proving.
- argument: measures a claim by what it produced in the world, not by the
  label its defenders gave it — holds "interim" against the irreversibility
  of what the order actually did to real people.
- evidence: names the specific order and its concrete consequence (600,000
  people, immediate arrest and removal) instead of describing a category of
  harm.
- stance: openly critical without mockery; quotes the opposing public
  defense in full before showing the gap between it and the docket.
- notice: catches the discrepancy between what an institution says about its
  own process and what the pattern of its orders shows — the move is
  tracking a pattern across rulings, not building a case from any single one.
- diction: plain and professional, pointed once ("Grand Canyon-sized
  chasm") rather than as a running habit.
- reader: assumes a reader who already follows the Court closely; explains
  the newer procedural distinction, skips the basics.
- the move the axes miss: accountability aimed at conduct, not persons — the
  close reroutes a fight over terminology back to what the behavior itself
  should be, without attacking any justice by name.
Calibration: "When the Court allowed the Trump administration to revoke
Temporary Protected Status for 600,000 Venezuelan migrants, that subjected
many of those individuals to immediate arrest, detention, and removal from
the country — removal that will almost certainly be impossible to undo."

## Will Baude, "The Other Jurisdictional Question in Moore v. Harper"
Source: https://reason.com/volokh/2023/03/03/the-other-jurisdictional-question-in-moore-v-harper/
Craft:
- cadence: builds through named sub-questions, each given its own short
  paragraph, before advancing to the next.
- argument: isolates a narrower, prior question the public debate had
  skipped past, and argues that question on its own footing first.
- evidence: tests several candidate explanations for a puzzling doctrine
  against text and history, rather than picking one to argue against.
- stance: withholds a verdict until the reasoning has actually produced one,
  and says outright when confidence isn't earned yet.
- notice: notices what a case's own procedural history quietly assumed, and
  asks whether that assumption still holds.
- diction: technical terms (finality, certiorari practice) defined at first
  use; nothing left to do unexplained work.
- reader: a reader who wants to follow the reasoning step by step, not
  receive a conclusion.
- the move the axes miss: charitable multiplicity — laying out more than one
  honest account of a doctrine or an opponent's position before settling, if
  at all, on the likeliest one, rather than reaching for the version easiest
  to beat.
Calibration: "The question marks above are not rhetorical. I've been
puzzling over these questions for almost a month, and still am not
confident I understand what is going on here."

## Cass Sunstein, "Six Separations of Powers, Not One"
Source: https://casssunstein.substack.com/p/six-separations-of-powers-not-one
Craft:
- cadence: a long structural sentence assembles a claim, then a fragment or
  short sentence lands it ("One person is not allowed to make the law.").
- argument: builds the claim directly out of an authority's own language,
  then extends past what that authority stated to what its logic requires.
- evidence: anchors each structural claim in a specific clause or holding,
  never a paraphrase of the doctrine standing in for the doctrine.
- stance: willing to correct the canonical authority itself once the record
  no longer supports it, and says so plainly instead of working around it.
- notice: notices that a structure everyone assumes is symmetrical has in
  practice inverted, and names which part now predominates.
- diction: accessible within a scholarly argument; a plain register and an
  occasional fragment, never decorative wordplay.
- reader: a general but attentive reader unfamiliar with the technical
  doctrine, capable of following a structural argument built in front of
  them from first principles.
- the move the axes miss: treats the founding text as a structure to reason
  forward from, not a line to cite and move past — the argument is the
  structure, shown doing its work step by step.
Calibration: "The accumulation of all powers ... in the same hands ... may
justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny." Sunstein's own gloss
follows it rather than restating it.
