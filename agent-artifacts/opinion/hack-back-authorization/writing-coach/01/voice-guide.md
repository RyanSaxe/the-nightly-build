# Voice guide: opinion/hack-back-authorization

## How this piece should sound

Open with the stance stated once, plainly, the way Kerr opens his rebuttal to
Baker: a sentence that says what the other side claims, a sentence that says
this piece disagrees, and nothing hedging between them. The position card
already carries the one-sentence stance and its named holders; the prose that
follows should commit at the same pitch rather than re-approaching the
question from a neutral start.

When the memo's guardrails come up — vetting, per-operation pre-approval, DOJ
and DHS oversight — state them the way their own defenders would sign them
before turning to what they don't solve. Kerr does this with Baker's
authorization-follows-data theory: he lays out the full logic of the position
he's about to reject, in the terms its holder used, before he says it's wrong.
Goldsmith does the same with the case for restraint, giving international law
and the escalation risk their own paragraph, each treated as a real
constraint and not a straw man, before the piece returns to its verdict. The
strongest-case section this piece owes its critics deserves that same
full-weight treatment, not a summary thin enough to knock over.

Ground the two failure points — misattribution and escalation the approving
agencies can't control once a vetted firm is inside a foreign network — in a
worked example the way Chesney turns a "beacon" into a GPS tracker in a bag
of bank cash. The image doesn't replace the technical vocabulary; it sits
next to it so a reader who doesn't already know what a beacon or an
authorization chain is can still follow the mechanism. This piece has real
technical terms of art — CFAA exposure, misattribution, per-operation
authorization — that the declared reader may not hold on first use; each
earns one plain, concrete anchor where it's introduced, not a metaphor
repeated as a crutch.

Let the record do the arguing. Goldsmith builds his case by stacking five
years of officials' own words — Biden's, Blinken's, Bolton's, Obama's — before
he draws a single conclusion from the pile; the quotations carry the
argument, and his own sentences arrive only after the evidence is on the
table. This piece has the memo language, the March 2026 executive order, and
on-record statements from named critics and defenders to build the same way:
let the primary text and the named quotes accumulate before the piece states
what they add up to.

Where the piece reads the memo's own guardrail language closely — what
"per-operation pre-approval" actually covers, what CFAA exposure the memo
leaves standing after authorization — read it the way Chesney reads the
ACDC Act's sunset clause: catching precisely what a specific clause does and
doesn't reach, rather than gesturing at "the vetting process" or "the
oversight structure" in general terms. A close reading names the clause and
says what it does; it doesn't summarize the section's mood.

Close on the verdict the argument has earned, in one plain sentence the way
Goldsmith closes on "And it has clearly failed." No bookend device carries the
weight instead — the commission's note against repeating the recent record's
"What X would settle" closer and its nb-note-strong verdict box applies here.
The last sentence should read as the conclusion of the argument just made, not
as a box added after it.

## Orin Kerr, "The Legal Case Against Hack-Back: A Response to Stewart Baker"

Source: https://volokh.com/2012/10/15/the-legal-case-against-hack-back-a-response-to-stewart-baker/

> "My co-blogger Stewart Baker recently argued that it is legal to hack into
> the computer of someone who has hacked into your computer. Stewart says his
> analysis is "surely" right. I think it's obviously wrong. Here's why."

The first three sentences give the reader the opposing claim, the writer's
claim, and the disagreement between them, in that order, before any argument
starts. Nothing is held back for a later reveal. The fourth sentence is four
words and does one job: it tells the reader an argument is coming, not a
survey.

> "Stewart offers a novel way to get around this and read the statute
> allowing hacking back. He posits that rights to control authorization go
> with ownership of data stored on a particular machine. More specifically,
> Stewart argues that the CFAA is so vague as to whether it protects computer
> or data that the rule of lenity requires courts to adopt the view that any
> person pursuing their stolen data is authorized in their conduct. In his
> view, you can't really rule out that the theft victim controls
> authorization — and if you can't really rule it out, you must rule it in.
> Thus anything victims do must be authorized because they themselves have
> authorized it."

Kerr spends four full sentences building Baker's argument to its strongest
form, including the step of Baker's reasoning ("if you can't really rule it
out, you must rule it in") that Kerr is about to attack. He does not
paraphrase it down to something easy to knock over. The rejection, when it
comes in the next paragraph, is aimed at the argument actually made.

> "The rule here is the same rule that is used in real property law: The
> owner/operator of the property controls who has access to it. The fact
> that your neighbor borrowed your baseball glove and you want it back
> doesn't give you a right to break into everything your neighbor owns on
> the theory that you can authorize yourself to go anywhere to get your glove
> back. The same goes for computers."

A statute's abstract "authorization" clause becomes a borrowed glove and a
neighbor's house. The image is not decoration on top of the legal point; it
is how the legal point gets checked. A reader can test the analogy against
their own intuition about property before Kerr even states the rule again in
computer terms, and the four-word sentence that closes it ("The same goes for
computers") is doing real work rather than restating what came before.

## Jack Goldsmith, "Empty Threats and Warnings on Cyber"

Source: https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/empty-threats-and-warnings-cyber

> "On July 9, President Biden warned Russian President Vladimir Putin that
> the United States will take "any necessary action," including imposing
> unspecified "consequences," if Russia does not disrupt ransomware attacks
> from its soil. The problem with this warning is that the United States has
> been publicly pledging to impose "consequences" on Russia for its cyber
> actions for at least five years—usually, as here, following a
> hand-wringing government deliberation in the face of a devastating cyber
> incident. This talk has persisted even as adverse cyber operations have
> grown more frequent and damaging. It is ineffective and, in the aggregate,
> self-defeating."

The news event and the argument arrive in the same paragraph. Goldsmith
doesn't report the warning and then pivot to analysis; the second sentence
already says why the warning is empty, and the two short sentences that close
the paragraph state the thesis the rest of the piece has to earn. Everything
that follows is the five years of evidence for a claim already on the page.

> "The United States could, of course, do much more, but at least two major
> hurdles stand in the way. One, the less serious hurdle, is international
> law, which limits U.S. options, at least those involving forcible measures,
> in the face of the Russian operations below the threshold of uses of force
> or armed attacks. Second, the more serious hurdle, is the escalation
> threat."

Before the piece can fault the government for talking instead of acting, it
names the two real reasons acting is hard, and ranks them by seriousness
instead of listing them as equal. Naming the harder objection first, and
saying so, is what makes the eventual verdict about empty rhetoric land as
earned rather than as a cheap shot at an obviously difficult problem.

> "And so the United States remains stuck in response to these
> ever-more-menacing cyber operations. It cannot defend its networks from
> increased attacks. And it cannot credibly threaten greater consequences for
> the attacker, thereby deterring the attacker. The government has worked
> very hard on both of these approaches. And it has clearly failed. But it
> sure is talking a good game."

Five short sentences, each one a plain declarative clause, close the piece.
None of them summarizes what was already said; each adds the next fact in
the indictment. The last sentence is seven words and carries no more freight
than the sentence before it — it doesn't reach for a bigger, quotable finish,
it just states the last true thing.

## Robert Chesney, "Hackback Is Back: Assessing the Active Cyber Defense Certainty Act"

Source: https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/hackback-back-assessing-active-cyber-defense-certainty-act

> "The "hackback" debate has been with us for many years. It boils down to
> this: Private-sector victims of hacking in some instances might wish to
> engage in self-defense outside their own networks (that is, doing some
> hacking of their own in order to terminate an attack, identify the
> attacker, destroy stolen data, etc.) but for the prospect that they then
> would face criminal (and possibly civil) liability under 18 USC § 1030 (the
> Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, or CFAA). A tricky question of policy
> therefore arises: Should the CFAA be pruned to facilitate hackback under
> certain conditions? On one hand, this might produce significant benefits in
> terms of reducing harm to victims and deterring some intrusions. On the
> other hand, risks involving mistaken attribution, unintended collateral
> harms and dangerous escalation abound."

One paragraph states what the debate is, why the law currently blocks it, and
both sides of the policy tradeoff, each side given a full clause rather than
a token phrase. A reader who has never heard of the CFAA can follow every
sentence, because each term (the statute, the liability, the tradeoff) is
defined in the sentence where it first appears rather than assumed.

> "This section is meant to facilitate the use of beacons. "Beacon" can mean
> various things, but the basic idea is simple: A potential victim includes
> code in a file on their own system, and in the event someone copies and
> exports the code, it will not only attempt to phone home to the victim but,
> in doing so, will also convey some amount of forensic detail regarding its
> current location. Think of it like a bank that sticks a GPS tracker, and
> maybe also a camera or recording device, in a bag of cash that might get
> stolen from the bank vault."

Chesney explains the technical mechanism first, in full and without
shortcuts, and only then hands the reader an image to hold it by. The GPS
tracker doesn't replace "attributional data" or "phone home" — those terms
stay in the paragraph — it just gives the reader something concrete to check
the technical description against.

> "The statute includes a two-year sunset, which is smart, but notice that
> the sunset is framed in a funny (and arguably limiting) way: It refers only
> to the "exclusion from prosecution created by this Act" as opposed to the
> act as a whole or to, say, all of Sections 3 through 6. ... I suspect the
> sunset is meant to cover both, but as currently written it might be
> construed to reach only Section 3 (the much-less controversial part of the
> ACDC Act). Easily fixed, of course."

This is a close reading of the bill's actual words, not a comment on its
general spirit. Chesney names the specific section, quotes the specific
phrase, and states exactly which parts of the bill the phrase does and
doesn't reach. The judgment ("easily fixed, of course") is short precisely
because the close reading in front of it already did the work of proving the
gap is real.
