# Evidence: opinion/hack-back-authorization (researcher/01)

The record strongly supports the commission's intended stance and gives it more
precision than the commission draft states. The memorandum's own text, read
against the March EO and the CFAA, shows the guardrails are real but narrower
than they sound: the "control and oversight" language rests on stretching 18
U.S.C. § 1030(f)'s law-enforcement exemption to private contractors doing the
hands-on hacking, a reading no court has tested — a point made explicitly, in
these words, by two separate Lawfare legal analyses written by practicing
cyber-law attorneys. Named critics on the record (a former Cyber Command
official, a former FBI intelligence official, a former CISA official, and a
sitting Ranking Member) converge on exactly the two failure modes the
commission names: misattribution and uncontrolled escalation once a private
actor is inside a foreign network. The pro-program case is also well
evidenced and textually stronger than a strawman: named administration
officials and industry supporters argue capacity (FBI is outnumbered),
private-sector technical edge, and structural safeguards (per-operation
written approval, a $1 million forfeitable bond, DOJ review of anything
touching a U.S. person). Where the record is thin: there is no independent
verification of the Wray "50-to-1" statistic beyond a single reporter's
attribution, the classified annex (which reportedly holds the deconfliction
and adjudicatory framework) is not public, and no source establishes whether
any operation has yet been proposed, approved, or executed under the Program
— the piece cannot claim a track record either way. The Washington Post's
Aug. 14 piece, listed in the commission's source list, could not be retrieved
despite repeated attempts (see Discarded); the CNN, CyberScoop, Federal News
Network, The Record, and Lawfare material substitute for it and independently
satisfy the named-holders-on-both-sides requirement.

## Contradictions

- **Name spelling, the same former Cyber Command official, across outlets.**
  CNN (direct fetch) spells the name "Jason Kikta" and independently, third-
  party biographical sources (Automox, the Institute for Security and
  Technology) confirm "Jason Kikta" as CISO/SVP Product at Automox, formerly
  of U.S. Cyber Command. CyberScoop's own article spells the same person
  "Jason Kitka" in both the day-one story and its own topic tag. This is very
  likely the same person misspelled by CyberScoop; the writer should use
  "Kikta" (confirmed against the subject's own employer bio) and should not
  cite CyberScoop's spelling without correction.
- **Company name for Josh/Joshua Steinman's firm.** CyberScoop's first
  article and third-party sources (Crunchbase, the company's own "About Us"
  page, a congressional witness bio) confirm "Galvanick." CyberScoop's second
  article spells it "Gavalnick" in the same piece that elsewhere (in its own
  topic tags) does not repeat the error. Use "Galvanick."
- **How settled the CFAA question actually is.** The memorandum's own Sec.
  2(b) asserts the Program operates "in accordance with" and "including"
  18 U.S.C. § 1030, phrasing that presents CFAA compliance as achieved by the
  Program's structure. Both Lawfare legal analyses, published by practicing
  attorneys with federal prosecution backgrounds, state plainly that whether
  the CFAA's law-enforcement exemption actually extends to a private
  contractor performing the intrusion (rather than merely assisting
  government agents) is untested in any court. Tonya Ugoretz, a former senior
  FBI official, independently says the same thing in her own words: "the memo
  doesn't directly address legal liability." This is not a disagreement
  between sources so much as a gap between the memo's confident framing and
  every independent legal read of it — worth stating plainly as the article's
  central tension rather than as a "some say" hedge.
- **How novel the private-sector role actually is.** The March Lawfare piece
  and Federal News Network both note the August memo builds on, and is less
  novel than, some coverage implies: EO 14390 already authorized government
  use of "threat intelligence... from commercial cybersecurity firms," and
  Sean Cairncross said as early as March that the administration was "not
  asking the private sector to conduct offensive cyber operations." The
  August memo is the first document to authorize companies to conduct the
  operations themselves (not merely supply intelligence) — a real escalation
  from March to August, not just an extension, and worth being precise about
  in the timeline section.
- **Optimism about a "bounded" program vs. concrete gaps named by its own
  supporters.** Ari Redbord (TRM Labs, pro-Program) and Robert Graham (Errata
  Security, skeptical) both, independently, flag that the memo is silent on
  what happens to seized assets and how victim compensation would work —
  a supporter and a critic naming the same gap from different angles
  strengthens, rather than undercuts, the point that "bounded" undersells how
  much is left to the 60-day implementing guidance and the classified annex.
