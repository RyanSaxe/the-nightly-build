# Commission: opinion/hack-back-authorization

## The occasion and why now

On August 12, 2026, the President signed a National Security Presidential
Memorandum creating the first formal US program that authorizes vetted private
firms to conduct offensive "hack-back" operations against foreign criminal
networks, under Department of Justice and Department of Homeland Security
oversight. The memo does not create a general right for companies to retaliate
on their own initiative: it establishes a program in which vetted firms receive
written, per-operation pre-approval before acting against a specific target, and
companies that hack back without that specific authorization remain exposed to
the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act. The signing is the occasion that forces the
choice; timing explains why the argument belongs now, not why to believe it.

## The paper's position (the stance to argue)

The paper argues against building national cyber-defense on privatized offensive
operations: the program's guardrails (vetting and per-operation pre-approval) do
not solve the two problems that make private-sector offense dangerous, which are
misattribution striking the wrong systems and escalation the approving agencies
cannot control once a private actor is inside a foreign network, and the CFAA
exposure the memo leaves in place is an admission that the line it draws is thin.
This is the intended landing. Refine the precise claim to the record the
researcher establishes, but do not soften it into a survey or a contrarian pose
without a case. The opinion template requires the position be one real people
with standing hold: cite the named critics (cyber-law scholars and former
officials raising oversight, liability, attribution, and escalation concerns in
the on-record coverage) whose position the card summarizes.

## Build the argument and the counter

Make the position precise, connect it to the public record (the memo, the March
2026 fraud-focused executive order it builds on, the CFAA), and show what follows
if it is right. Then give the strongest opposing case its own terms, cited to its
named holders: transnational criminal fraud is sustained and large, the military
and intelligence community cannot scale to it, and vetting plus per-operation
approval is a real guardrail, so a bounded private-sector program is a
proportionate tool. State that case as its own believers would sign it, then
answer it. Close on what the argument earned and, where honesty allows, name the
evidence that would change the paper's judgment (for example, a track record of
authorized operations with auditable attribution and no collateral escalation).

## Sources to begin from (researcher confirms and reads the primary)

Primary: the memorandum text or the White House fact sheet, and the March 2026
fraud executive order it cites. On-record coverage carrying named holders on both
sides: the Washington Post (Aug 14), CyberScoop, The Record, Lawfare's analysis
of the administration's private-sector cyber strategy, Federal News Network on
the oversight and liability questions, and Reason. Get the actual memo text as
primary; use coverage for the named positions and reactions.

## Furniture and template

The `nb-position` card is required and opens the piece: the stance in one
sentence with the named holders cited under it. The title and dek sell the
question or the consequence and must not restate the card. A short timeline of
the policy path (March 2026 fraud EO to the August 12 memo) may help if the
evidence supports it. No court docket (this is not a case).
