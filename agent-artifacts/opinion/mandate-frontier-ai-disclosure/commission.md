## The occasion (why now)
On 2026-08-02 the EU AI Act's obligations for general-purpose AI (GPAI) models
became applicable, and the European AI Office moved to enforce them against
frontier developers (model evaluation, systemic-risk mitigation, incident
reporting, transparency), operationalized through the GPAI Code of Practice
whose signatories include the major frontier labs. In the US there is still no
federal frontier-AI law; the only enforceable domestic regime is California's
SB 53 (Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act), effective
2026-01-01. The contrast is now live, and it forces a choice for US policy.

## The position (what the paper argues)
Provisional thesis to defend: **The United States should enact a narrow federal
transparency-and-incident-reporting floor for frontier-model developers —
essentially SB 53's disclosure core raised to a national standard — rather than
leave frontier safety to a state patchwork or to nothing.** The argued position
must be one that a real, named holder with standing actually advocates (the
`opinion` template requires the position card to name cited holders — e.g.,
lawmakers, the SB 53 authors, named policy institutions or researchers who back a
federal transparency floor). The piece advocates a documented position; it does
not invent one.

This is a *precise* claim, not "regulate AI": a **disclosure floor** (published
safety frameworks, catastrophic-risk assessments, defined critical-incident
reporting, whistleblower protection) — NOT capability licensing, NOT pre-release
government approval, NOT the EU's full structural apparatus. Say exactly what is
in and out.

If, after research, the evidence genuinely does not support this side, the writer
may file the opposite position with the same rigor — but that is a real
reversal to flag in the handoff, not a hedge. Commit to the synthesis the
evidence supports; the popular read is usually the shallow one, so hold the
thesis to a higher bar wherever it agrees with the crowd.
