# Evidence record: opinion/right-to-worship-act (01)

## What the evidence supports, and where it is thin

The single most consequential finding cuts against the commission's lean as framed.
The Right to Worship Act's operative provision, as described by the lead sponsor's
own release and by reporting that quotes the bill, is **a scienter-based
anti-disruption / anti-access-obstruction rule, not a McCullen-style flat buffer
that excludes speakers from a 100-foot zone.** The "100 feet" fixes the geographic
reach of the prohibition; the thing prohibited is *knowingly* disrupting a scheduled
service or *knowingly* impeding, intimidating, or obstructing people trying to attend.
Sen. Slotkin's office and the Times of Israel both state the bill "would not bar
people from 'freely speaking on any subject'" within the zone and targets "conduct
rather than the content of protesters' speech." That structure is materially closer to
the less-restrictive alternatives *McCullen* itself praised (the FACE Act, obstruction
statutes, targeted injunctions) than to the 35-foot categorical exclusion *McCullen*
struck down. A writer defending the commission's "cannot survive *McCullen*" lean
against *this* text would be arguing against the wrong statute. The honest opinion the
record supports is narrower and more contingent: the constitutional risk lives in the
bill's *vagueness and breadth* ("meaningfully interferes," "unreasonably hinders or
deters," "reasonable fear," religious-service timing kept private), not in a fixed
buffer that bans peaceful presence.

The record is **thin in two places the writer must flag**. First, no Congress.gov page
with enacted bill text was locatable as of the research date (Aug 8, 2026); the bill was
introduced ~48 hours earlier and text was not yet posted under that title. The operative
language below is drawn from the lead sponsor's official release and from reporting that
purports to quote the bill, not from a canonical statutory text. Second, sources
disagree on whether enforcement is **civil-only or civil-and-criminal** (see
Contradictions). Both gaps should be stated plainly in the piece rather than papered over.

## Contradictions

- **Buffer ban vs. conduct rule (the decisive one).** Several outlets and the commission
  frame the Act as a "100-foot buffer zone ... where protests would not be allowed"
  (jpost/Times of Israel headline register; commission's McCullen framing). The lead
  sponsor's release, the operative language quoted by Times of Israel, the ADL, and
  even the NYCLU's read of the parallel NY law all describe a *conduct* rule keyed to
  "knowing" disruption/obstruction/intimidation, expressly preserving free speech within
  the zone. The primary/quoted-text sources win: it is not a flat exclusion buffer. This
  reversal is the article's central factual pivot and must be stated prominently.
- **Civil vs. criminal enforcement.** Times of Israel and the Slotkin release describe a
  *civil* scheme (tiered fines $2,500/$5,000/$10,000; suits for court orders and damages).
  The Newsday/Suozzi item says the Act makes it "a federal crime to knowingly intimidate,
  obstruct or harass" *and* adds civil fines. Unresolved against posted text; record both
  and note it turns partly on the (unavailable) enrolled bill.
- **Zone size mismatch with the "mirror."** The commission says the federal bill mirrors
  a NY statute; the NY *state* law is 50 feet (the NYC Council ordinance was separate and
  "watered down"). The federal bill is 100 feet. It parallels NY's *structure* (conduct-
  based, "reasonable fear," civil/criminal) at *twice* the distance. The larger radius is
  the sharper tailoring vulnerability, and it is not identical to the enacted NY model.
- **Introduction date.** Sources variously say the bill was introduced/"to be introduced
  Thursday" Aug 6, 2026 (JTA/Newsday, dated Aug 6) vs. Aug 7, 2026 (Slotkin release date;
  commission). Minor; Aug 6, 2026 was a Thursday. Use "introduced the week of Aug 6-7,
  2026" unless enrolled text fixes the date.
- **How far McCullen wounded Hill.** McCullen did not overrule Hill and reaffirmed
  content-neutrality; Scalia's concurrence argued the majority had undercut Hill "sub
  silentio." So "Hill is dead" overstates; "Hill is contested and unextended" is accurate.
  The counter section should not lean on Hill as if it were secure, nor bury it as if
  overruled.

Caveat on the McCullen "unanimous" point the commission relies on: the *judgment* was
unanimous but the reasoning was not — four Justices (Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Alito
in part) would have gone further and found the law content-based. Describing McCullen as
"a unanimous Court" is true as to result and misleading as to doctrine; say which.
