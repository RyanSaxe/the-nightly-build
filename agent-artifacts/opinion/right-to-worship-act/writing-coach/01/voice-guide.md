# Voice guide: argued constitutional-law opinion (01)

## Directive

Write for a rigorous reader who is not a lawyer: the assumed reader can follow a
proof but has not read the cases. Treat legal argument the way that reader treats
a derivation. Each step is an entailment from something on the page — the bill's
operative words, a holding, a standard — and every link is shown, not asserted.
When a step needs a term the reader does not hold (content-neutral, narrow
tailoring, facial versus as-applied challenge, scienter), define it in the
sentence it first does work, then use it exactly that way every time after.

The register is a serious legal essayist arguing a contested question, not a
technical writer explaining a settled system. That means two things the calm
house default does not by itself supply. First, force comes from structure, not
from adjectives: the persuasion is carried by the test the argument runs the bill
through and the narrower instruments it names, so the verdict can be stated flat
and short because the section before it did the work. Second, fairness is
adversarial-grade: the opposing case is built in the sponsors' own doctrinal
terms and cited to people who actually hold it, strong enough that a reader might
be persuaded by it, before it is answered on the ground the argument chooses.

Do not hedge the legal conclusion and do not soften it with atmosphere. State
what the text says, what the standard requires, and where the two meet or part.
Where the argument turns on a specific fact about the record that could come out
either way, say which fact and which way — that is the honest version of holding
force, not a retreat from it.

Two things to keep out of the prose. Certainty theater: piling "plainly,"
"clearly," "obviously" onto a conclusion the analysis has not yet earned. And the
motive read: arguing that a rule is bad because of why its sponsors wanted it.
Argue the words and the standard; leave the sponsors' hearts alone.

## Licenses

form: the argued-from-text paragraph
move: Volokh hinges a paragraph on the verbatim operative clause ("for the purpose
  of ... protest, education, or counseling") and shows the classification those
  exact words make; Roberts quotes the governing standard ("narrowly tailored to
  serve a significant governmental interest") and tests the Act against its words.
bar:  the quoted words must be the ones the conclusion turns on, and the sentence
  must show the line, threshold, or gap those exact words create. A quotation that
  decorates a claim the writer would make anyway is cut.

form: the flat verdict, earned in place
move: Roberts writes "The buffer zones burden substantially more speech than
  necessary"; Feldman writes "I think these decisions were clearly wrong." A short
  declarative characterizes the legal conclusion with no hedge and no cushion.
bar:  the sentence sits after the analysis that earns it, in the same section, and
  states only what the cited record supports. A verdict placed ahead of its support,
  or reaching past it, is cut.

form: the steelman a believer would sign
move: Kerr states the co-blogger's theory on its own terms before parting from it;
  Feldman grants the opposing view its intellectual lineage and internal coherence
  before answering. The other side arrives in the form its own advocates use, with
  its own strongest authority cited.
bar:  the counter names the sponsors' best actual authority and frames the claim as
  an advocate would recognize it. If the answer would still land against a weaker
  version of the case, the steelman is too weak and must be rebuilt stronger.

form: the narrower-instrument enumeration
move: Roberts answers a tailoring failure not by assertion but by naming specific
  tools that burden less speech — an existing anti-disruption provision, a
  FACE-style access statute, police dispersal orders.
bar:  each instrument named must actually exist or be plainly enactable and burden
  less speech than the challenged rule. A gestured "narrower options exist" with no
  instrument named is cut.

## Chief Justice Roberts, McCullen v. Coakley (majority)
Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/12-1168
Craft:
- cadence: long clause that states the standard, then short flat clauses that apply
  it. "Even though the Act is content neutral, it still must be narrowly tailored"
  pivots the whole section on one short sentence.
- argument: runs the statute through a named test in order, settling the easier
  question (content neutrality) before spending the hard one (tailoring), so the
  reader carries a resolved point into the contested one.
- evidence: the statute's own words ("public way[s]," "sidewalk[s]") and prior
  holdings quoted as the operative units; the interest stated in the state's terms.
- stance: decides plainly ("burden substantially more speech than necessary")
  without a raised voice; the conclusion is a finding, not an accusation.
- notice: notices that the state never tried its narrower tools first, and makes
  that failure the hinge rather than the statute's intent.
- diction: institutional and exact; terms of art are used, not defined, because the
  audience holds them — invert this for a lay reader without losing the exactness.
- reader: assumes a reader who will hold the government's interest as legitimate;
  concedes it fully, then wins on fit rather than on whether the interest counts.
- the important move: separates "is the interest real" from "does this rule fit it,"
  concedes the first, and stakes everything on the second. The concession is what
  makes the tailoring verdict land.

## Orin Kerr, "No, Trump Does Not Have a First Amendment Defense..." (Volokh/Reason)
Source: https://reason.com/volokh/2024/05/04/no-trump-does-not-have-a-first-amendment-defense-in-his-new-york-criminal-case/
Craft:
- cadence: conversational and short. Claims land in one clause; the next sentence
  tests them rather than qualifying them.
- argument: isolates the opponent's proposition, grants its strongest reading, then
  shows the single move where it fails ("that was not charged as a crime").
- evidence: doctrine invoked by shape rather than string-cited, kept honest by
  admitting the edge of his own knowledge ("As far as I know").
- stance: disagrees flatly ("I do not agree") while separating the disagreement
  from the parts he concedes ("I have mixed views").
- notice: notices what was actually charged versus what the opposing argument
  answers, and makes the mismatch the whole answer.
- diction: plain, occasionally colloquial, never loose about the legal point.
- reader: trusts the reader to follow a distinction stated once, without repetition
  or scaffolding.
- the important move: concedes on everything that is not load-bearing so the one
  real disagreement stands alone and undefended by noise.

## Noah Feldman, "The Last Bulwark" (New York Review of Books)
Source: https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2025/05/15/the-last-bulwark-noah-feldman/
Craft:
- cadence: analytical sentences broken by a short declarative that resolves them
  ("A compromise may resolve the crisis, or one of the two sides may prevail").
- argument: builds a doctrine from its principle for a lay reader, by contrast —
  what an authority may do against what it may not — before applying it.
- evidence: the opposing view given a genealogy and a coherent internal logic, not
  a caricature, and then met.
- stance: states judgment in the first person and unhedged ("clearly wrong, in some
  instances disastrously so") without turning the judgment into invective.
- notice: notices the reader's likely resistance and names it ("Like it or not")
  rather than arguing past it.
- diction: no jargon; complex doctrine rendered in plain declaratives a non-lawyer
  reads once.
- reader: assumes intelligence without legal training, and never talks down while
  never leaving a term undefined.
- the important move: keeps full authority while conceding the reader may dislike
  the conclusion, so the commitment reads as earned rather than imposed.

## Eugene Volokh, "Justice Thomas Calls Again for Overruling Hill v. Colorado" (Reason)
Source: https://reason.com/volokh/2025/02/24/justice-thomas-calls-again-for-overruling-hill-v-colorado/
Craft:
- cadence: quote the operative clause, then one sentence that reads the line it
  draws. The rhythm is text, then consequence.
- argument: shows a statute's character from its own words — which speakers it
  reaches and which it lets pass — rather than from its label or its purpose.
- evidence: the exact statutory language and the precedent's own stated reasoning,
  both quoted before either is challenged.
- stance: characterizes precisely and firmly, and reserves the strongest phrasing
  for the point the reading has already established.
- notice: notices the carve-out ("any message except one of protest, education, or
  counseling") that a paraphrase would smooth over, and makes it decisive.
- diction: precise, graduated when the point is contested ("undermined, if not
  completely eroded"), flat when the text is plain.
- reader: shows rather than tells, letting the quoted words do the classifying so
  the reader reaches the conclusion a half-step ahead of the sentence.
- the important move: reads a distinction off the statute's own words instead of
  arguing about them, so the classification looks like observation, not advocacy.
