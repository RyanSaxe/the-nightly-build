# Editorial review: opinion/right-to-worship-act (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: Congress should not pass the Right to Worship Act as written, because
its operative verbs are unconstitutionally vague and its 100-foot, schedule-keyed
zone reaches substantially more protected speech than stopping disruption
requires. The load-bearing claims:

1. The Act is a scienter-based conduct rule, not a McCullen-style flat exclusion
   buffer, so it sits doctrinally with Hill.
2. The operative terms ("meaningfully interferes," "unreasonably hinders or
   deters") are undefined and hand enforcers open-ended discretion (vagueness),
   and reach expression that unsettles without blocking (overbreadth).
3. The zone is twice New York's 50-foot radius and switches on during services
   whose schedules need not be published, compounding the tailoring/chill defect.
4. Under McCullen's narrow-tailoring test, narrower instruments (obstruction
   statute, FACE-style law, injunctions, dispersal orders) achieve the same
   protection with less burden.

I pushed hardest where the piece is most exposed: the legal characterizations.
Each was checked descriptor by descriptor against the cited primaries.

- McCullen: verified against LII (law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/12-1168).
  35-foot FIXED buffer — correct. Content-neutral but not narrowly tailored,
  "burden[s] substantially more speech than is necessary" — correct and the
  quote is exact. Unanimous in RESULT, split in rationale (Scalia with Kennedy
  and Thomas, plus Alito, concurring only in the judgment and arguing the law was
  content-based) — the article states this precisely ("unanimous only in its
  result: four Justices would have struck the buffer as content-based"), which is
  the corrected framing the commission update required. The verbatim obstruction
  quote the counter turns on ("knowingly obstructs, detains, hinders, impedes or
  blocks another person's entry") is confirmed verbatim in the opinion; the
  article truncates at "entry," which is a faithful cut, not a misquote. The
  less-restrictive-alternatives menu is confirmed.

- Hill: verified against LII (text/98-1856). 8-foot FLOATING no-approach zone
  within 100 feet of the entrance, without consent, to leaflet/sign/counsel —
  correct. Upheld 6-3 as content-neutral — correct. The article does not overstate
  Hill's authority: it frames it as "the sponsors' best and most exposed" precedent
  and "likeliest to fall," citing the Clement/Murphy petition and Thomas's
  "aberration." The three-rule table represents Hill's outer 100-foot boundary in
  the Zone column with the 8-foot floating rule stated in the "what it forbids"
  cell; accurate, not misleading.

The position is the pivoted, narrower one. The piece never argues "cannot survive
McCullen against a flat buffer"; it affirmatively concedes the buffer attack fails
("A writer who insisted this bill 'cannot survive McCullen' would be answering a
statute the sponsors did not write") and wins instead on vagueness/tailoring. The
pivot is presented as an evidence-driven legal distinction (conduct rule vs.
exclusion), not as narration of an internal commission decision — no prompt leak.

Sourcing and hrefs. I opened every load-bearing href and confirmed each lands on
the source and carries the quoted language: Slotkin release (operative "knowingly
disrupts or impedes access... within 100 feet," the two-prong definition), Times
of Israel ("would not bar people from 'freely speaking on any subject,'" the
$2,500/$5,000/$10,000 fine schedule), NYCLU ("does not establish uniform,
demonstration-free buffer zones" and "could make people censor themselves"), JTA
(NY 50-foot figure), FIRE ("all but interred," Thomas's "aberration"), and both
LII opinions. The nb proof ran link-checked to BLOCK: 0, covering resolution of
the remainder. data-nb-kind labels are honest: the two SCOTUS opinions and the
sponsor release are primary; the sponsor-hosted Newsday item is correctly tagged
secondary (hosting does not confer authorship); ToI, ADL, NYCLU, JTA, FIRE
secondary.

Evidence gaps are stated in the prose, not papered over: the "What the record
cannot settle yet" note discloses that no enrolled Congress.gov text existed at
publication and attributes operative language to the release and reporting, and it
records the civil-only vs. civil-and-criminal conflict rather than silently
resolving it — then turns that unresolved conflict into part of the vagueness
argument, which is the honest use.

No claim broke. One point to log for its owner (below), not a break: the
"schedules need not be published" fact underpins the title and one overbreadth
step. It is documented in the researcher's evidence record from the release's
bill-summary section, but the release's summary model did not surface it on my
read of the page. The evidence record and the link-checked citation support it;
recording it as a reconfirm, not a defect.

## Cut

The prose is already lean; the two cuts are pure deletions of moves that grade or
signpost rather than argue.

- Removed the section-opening "That distinction decides the constitutional
  question." It announced the stakes before the paragraph built them; the
  paragraph now opens on "The Supreme Court's speech cases split buffer rules into
  two kinds," and the section's own payoff ("On its face this Act is the second
  kind" / "The Act sits with Hill, not with McCullen") carries the stakes without
  the pre-announcement.

- Removed "Which one governs sharpens or eases everything below" from the
  enforcement note. "Everything below" is a self-reference to the article's own
  structure, and the very next paragraph already establishes that the
  civil/criminal ambiguity feeds the vagueness argument, so the sentence was both
  a signpost and redundant.

Density warnings adjudicated (all three earn their length; I concur with the
writer): the 66-word verbatim two-prong statutory quote must stay intact —
splitting it would misquote the operative text the section exists to read; the
Hill holding and the doctrine sentence are parallel enumerations the argument
needs stated in one breath. No run-ons, no semicolon chains, zero em-dashes
confirmed; the two semicolons in the piece each join tightly bound independent
clauses and are within standard.

Worst tell found: the removed stakes-announcement. No repeated ending shape or
heading/dek formula — the headline avoids the banned "Congress already wrote the
rule this X breaks" frame, and the dek ("copies New York's buffer statute, then
draws its zone at twice the 50-foot radius") is a two-clause factual claim, not a
semicolon reversal, suspended question, or comma triad. The one earned hedged
contrast (the bill "is not what its critics and its own press describe") corrects
a real, widely-held mischaracterization and is the piece's licensed central pivot.

Furniture holds. The position card, the three-rule table (the argument's spine,
done on the page not asserted), the evidence-gap note, and the Verdict block each
carry evidence or the argument's landing. The McCullen docket card is deliberate
emphasis on the single controlling case for a lay reader, not decoration; it
earns its place.

## Reader

What the piece gives beyond its sources: a sponsor release, two Supreme Court
opinions, and advocacy commentary do not, on their own, tell a non-lawyer where
this bill lands doctrinally. The article does — it maps the Act onto Hill's
conduct/consent logic, retires the obvious-but-wrong McCullen-buffer attack,
locates the real defect in the undefined verbs plus the doubled, undisclosed-
schedule radius, and answers the steelman on the tailoring ground McCullen itself
supplies. That synthesis is the original-work sentence in the draft handoff, and
it is delivered on the page (the three-rule table and the "Twice the zone"
section) rather than announced. The prose sits with the voice-guide exemplars —
Roberts's concede-the-interest-then-win-on-fit structure, Volokh's read-the-line-
off-the-text move, the flat earned verdict — not with a median summary.
