# Commission: opinion/right-to-worship-act

## Subject
The **Right to Worship Act**, the federal buffer-zone bill introduced Thursday,
August 7, 2026 by Sens. Ted Cruz (R-TX) and Elissa Slotkin (D-MI) with Reps.
Brad Knott (R-NC) and Tom Suozzi (D-NY). It would create a 100-foot buffer zone
around houses of worship during religious services and let individuals (and
state attorneys) seek federal court orders and civil damages against those who
knowingly disrupt services. The paper takes a side on whether Congress should
pass it as written.

## The occasion and why now
A bill introduced this week, with named sponsors and a text, is exactly the kind
of occasion the opinion desk exists for: the paper can be held accountable for
being wrong, and the stakes are a concrete choice a legislature will make. It
mirrors a New York statute already in force, so the constitutional question is
live, not hypothetical.

## The position to take (writer commits after evidence; this is the commission's
## considered lean, to be defended or revised by the record)
Lean: **the Act as written cannot survive First Amendment scrutiny and should
not pass in its current form.** The governing precedent is *McCullen v. Coakley*
(2014), where a unanimous Court struck a 35-foot fixed buffer around abortion
clinics as insufficiently tailored even though it was content-neutral; a
100-foot fixed zone around a broader class of buildings is more vulnerable, not
less. *Hill v. Colorado* (2000), which upheld a floating 8-foot zone, is the
sponsors' best precedent and its authority is widely questioned after *McCullen*
and *Dobbs*; the counter section must state the strongest version of the
sponsors' case (a content-neutral, conduct-focused "knowing disruption" rule
protecting free exercise) and answer it on tailoring and the "knowing
disruption" scienter, not on motive.
If the record on the actual bill text defeats this lean (e.g. the operative
provision is a narrow anti-disruption rule materially unlike a McCullen buffer),
the writer commits to the side the record supports and says why. The position
must be precise, tied to the bill's operative text and the case law, and it must
state what evidence would change the paper's judgment.

## Commission update (post-evidence, orchestrator)
The researcher searched for what breaks the initial lean and found the record
defeats it as framed: per the sponsors' own release and reporting quoting the
bill, the operative provision is a **scienter-based anti-disruption rule**
("knowingly disrupts or impedes access") that expressly preserves speech within
the zone — not a McCullen-style flat 100-foot speech-exclusion buffer. So a
"cannot survive McCullen" argument targets the wrong statute; that structure
looks more like the less-restrictive alternatives McCullen praised (FACE Act,
obstruction statutes, injunctions). Per the license in this commission, the
paper commits to **the narrower position the record supports**: the real First
Amendment problem is **vagueness and overbreadth** — the operative terms
("meaningfully interferes," "unreasonably hinders or deters"), the 100-foot zone
at twice the mirrored New York law's 50 feet, and services whose schedules need
not be disclosed — and McCullen was unanimous only in result, not rationale. The
counter section must steelman the content-neutral, conduct-focused free-exercise
case at full strength before answering it on vagueness/tailoring. Open evidence
gaps to handle honestly: no Congress.gov enrolled text as of 2026-08-08 (bill
~48h old), so operative language rests on the sponsor release plus reporting; and
sources conflict on civil-only vs. civil-and-criminal enforcement. Re-check
Congress.gov for an assigned bill number/text before publication and flag any
residual uncertainty in the prose.
