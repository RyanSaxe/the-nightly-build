# Voice guide: tech-news brief (skeptical technology front page)

Write each item as a skeptical wire clause for an engineer who can and will
open the primary. The house register is already calm, precise, and argued from
first principles; keep it. What this brief adds is a discipline of provenance:
in this domain almost every development arrives as a number a party wants
believed, so the sentence that reports it must also settle who measured it and
whether anyone else has. Two moves change sentences here that the house default
does not force.

First, every quantitative result wears its source and its verification status in
the clause where it lands, not in a later hedge. A figure the owning lab reports
is grammatically a claim ("the lab reports", "on its own benchmark", "an
estimated"); a figure an independent party reproduced can stand as a bare
assertion. The reader must be able to tell measured from claimed without leaving
the sentence.

Second, the significance judgment presses exactly as far as the sourcing
licenses and stops. A result confirmed by an independent account earns a
committed verdict; a single-source claim earns a verdict about what it would
mean if it holds, with the missing confirmation named. Confidence is carried by
understatement and by the number's own comparison, never by an intensifier.
"Remarkable", "stunning", and "impressive" describe the writer's reaction, not
the result.

Do not narrate the newsroom, do not close an item by handing the point back to
the reader, and do not let the skepticism curdle into a house sneer. The stance
is a working engineer's, fair to a strong result and unmoved by a marketed one.

## Licenses

form: the attributed figure
move: Willison never lets a vendor number pass as fact; he attaches "estimated"
  or the leaderboard to it, and reserves the bare claim for what he ran himself.
  Deploy it on any benchmark score, cost, or capability number an interested
  party is the source of.
bar:  from the clause alone the reader can name who produced the number and
  whether an independent party has confirmed it. A score stated without its
  owner, or a lab claim phrased as settled fact, fails.

form: the sourcing-scaled verdict
move: Clark's "why it matters" commits to one consequence for the field in a
  sentence or two, at the strength the evidence carries. Use it as the line that
  earns each item its place: what changes for practice, not what happened.
bar:  a verdict on an independently confirmed result may assert the consequence;
  a verdict resting on one self-report must be conditional and name what would
  confirm it. A significance line that would read the same whether the result
  were verified or merely claimed fails.

form: the steelmanned alternative reading
move: Clark, facing a result with two honest interpretations, states the
  stronger rival reading and then lands on one. Editorial standard endorses
  steelmanning before weighing; this licenses its compact, single-item form.
bar:  the alternative is the reading a skeptical expert would actually hold, and
  the item commits after naming it. It is not the invented "not X but Y" foil
  the house bans, and it appears only where the interpretation is genuinely
  live. If more than one item this edition reaches for it, the cadence has
  stamped and it must vary.

## Simon Willison, "Things we learned about LLMs in 2024"
Source: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/31/llms-in-2024/
Craft:
- cadence: short declaratives that state the fact, then a clause that places it;
  the qualification rides inside the same sentence rather than trailing after.
- argument: significance is shown by ranking against an independent yardstick
  (models "on the Chatbot Arena Leaderboard that rank higher than the original
  GPT-4"), not by repeating the launch.
- evidence: prefers what he verified himself ("I can now run a GPT-4 class model
  on my laptop") and the third-party leaderboard over the vendor's own chart.
- stance: enthusiastic where he has checked, exacting about cost and provenance
  the same breath (o3's ARC-AGI result "albeit one that likely involved more
  than $1,000,000 of compute").
- notice: the price and the compute behind a headline result, which most
  coverage drops.
- diction: "estimated", "likely", "class" do precise work; hedges are load-
  bearing, not throat-clearing.
- reader: a practitioner who wants to know whether it is real and what it cost,
  not whether it is exciting.
- the important move: the verification status travels attached to the number, so
  a claim never has to be walked back in a later sentence.

## Jack Clark, "Import AI 466: The bitter lesson for robotics"
Source: https://importai.substack.com/p/import-ai-466-the-bitter-lesson-for
Craft:
- cadence: the development in one attributed sentence ("Epoch and METR have
  released MirrorCode..."), then a "Why this matters" that turns to consequence.
- argument: the item exists for its significance line; the facts are premises
  for a claim about what the field can now do.
- evidence: names who built the benchmark and who estimated the human baseline,
  keeping the measurer visible ("METR and Epoch believe would take a human 2-17
  weeks").
- stance: reads a result for its second-order meaning ("AI systems can self-
  orient with regard to their environment") rather than its leaderboard rank.
- notice: what a capability implies for a downstream field, e.g. robotics
  inheriting gains from general models "as a natural dividend".
- diction: plain and consequence-facing; the significance verb ("shows",
  "tells us") is measured to the evidence.
- reader: someone tracking the frontier who needs the "so what", stated once.
- the important move: when a result reads two ways he names the stronger rival
  reading and then commits, instead of hedging between them.

## Dan Luu, "Keyboard latency"
Source: https://danluu.com/keyboard-latency/
Craft:
- cadence: unhurried, evidence first; the judgment arrives late and small.
- argument: distrusts impressions and marketed categories, measures, and lets
  the measurement decide ("I never trust feelings like this... so got a high-
  speed camera").
- evidence: re-verifies an implausible result before reporting it rather than
  accepting a surprising number.
- stance: understatement as force; a cautious frame delivers a claim that
  contradicts a whole product category ("it appears that gaming keyboards aren't
  generally faster").
- notice: the gap between what is marketed and what instruments show.
- diction: "preliminary", "appears", "I'd really like to test more" bound the
  claim precisely without weakening the finding under it.
- reader: an engineer who trusts a bounded, measured claim over a confident one.
- the important move: the size of the claim is fitted to the evidence, so the
  restraint is what makes the verdict credible.
