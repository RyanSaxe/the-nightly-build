# Voice guide: tech-news brief, 2026-08-09 (01)

## How this piece should sound

You are writing a compressed daily technology brief for a reader who already
saw the headline and holds degrees in mathematics and computer science. The
item's job is to add what the headline dropped: the number, the version, the
caveat. The register is calm and unimpressed, precise with technical terms, and
skeptical wherever a vendor's framing runs ahead of the result. The three
writers below all work in that register; the notes under each passage say what
in the writing carries to this brief.

Where an item opens, it can land the development itself first — who did what —
before any read of what it means. Clark opens the MirrorCode item by naming METR
and Epoch and stating what the benchmark measures, so the reader has the fact
before the interpretation. An item that begins with its own significance has
skipped the step the reader came for.

Where an item turns on a figure, the figure can ride inside the sentence rather
than stand as a separate claim, and where a comparison is available it can carry
the multiple or baseline the reader already holds. Willison never gives a price
without one: GPT-4o mini is stated at $0.15/mTok and in the same clause as 200x
cheaper than GPT-4. Clark folds a doubled forecast — 15% to 30% — into the
sentence that reports it. A number with nothing to measure it against asks the
reader to supply the scale.

Name the exact version, model, or benchmark the item rests on rather than a
category. Willison writes o3 and ARC-AGI, not "a reasoning model" and "a hard
benchmark"; Lambert names LMArena, not "the leaderboards." Where an item depends
on a benchmark result, the board can be named so the reader can weigh it.

Where a vendor's claim outruns what the result shows, the item can report the
result and size it plainly. Lambert reports the strongest fact he has, a sweep
to #1 on LMArena, and in the same passage calls the release a solid step on the
trend line rather than a leap. Willison reports the ARC-AGI result and lets the
seven-figure compute bill ride in behind an "albeit," so the reservation costs
the sentence nothing extra. Skepticism can sit in an aside; it does not need its
own verdict line.

## Simon Willison, "Things we learned about LLMs in 2024"

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/31/llms-in-2024/

> "Today $30/mTok gets you OpenAI's most expensive model, o1. GPT-4o is $2.50 (12x cheaper than GPT-4) and GPT-4o mini is $0.15/mTok—200x cheaper than GPT-4, nearly 7x cheaper than GPT-3.5 and massively more capable than that model."

The passage carries five prices and never states one without a multiple the
reader can hold: 12x, 200x, 7x. Willison is visible in the aside that GPT-4o mini
is "massively more capable" than the model it undercuts, a working user's
judgment set down mid-list rather than argued in its own sentence.

> "The sequel to o1, o3 (they skipped "o2" for European trademark reasons) was announced on 20th December with an impressive result against the ARC-AGI benchmark, albeit one that likely involved more than $1,000,000 of compute time expense!"

The development, the date, and the version land in the first clause, and the
reservation rides in behind "albeit" instead of taking a sentence of its own. The
person shows in the parenthetical about the skipped "o2" name and the exclamation
at the compute bill: he reports the result and is plainly unimpressed by its
price at once.

## Jack Clark, "Import AI 453: Breaking AI agents; MirrorCode; and ten views on gradual disempowerment"

Source: https://jack-clark.net/2026/04/13/import-ai-453-breaking-ai-agents-mirrorcode-and-ten-views-on-gradual-disempowerment/

> "AI measurement organizations METR and Epoch have built MirrorCode, a benchmark meant to test out how well AI models can autonomously reimplement complex existing software. The results show that AI systems are more capable than most people think at certain types of coding task, suggesting AI progress may be even faster than we previously thought."

The first sentence names who built the benchmark and what it measures before any
comment on it, so the reader has the development before Clark's read of it. Clark
is visible in the second sentence, where he states the consequence he draws in
plain words instead of hedging it behind attribution.

> "Ryan Greenblatt, an AI researcher and forecaster, believes AI progress in 2026 will be faster than in 2025, and he now has doubled his estimate from 15% to 30% of the chance that by the end of 2028 it'll be possible to fully automate AI research itself."

The whole update sits in one sentence: who, what he now believes, the old figure,
the new figure, and the claim being forecast. Clark puts the 15%-to-30% jump
inside the clause rather than announcing it, which is how a changed forecast
reads as news and not as a headline about itself.

## Nathan Lambert, "GPT-5 and the arc of progress"

Source: https://www.interconnects.ai/p/gpt-5-and-bending-the-arc-of-progress

> "GPT-5 is a good AI system. It's right in line with best-in-class across pretty much every evaluation, while being cheap enough to serve the whole world."

Two short declaratives deliver the verdict and its two grounds — even across
evaluations, cheap enough to serve everyone — with no modifier reaching past what
the evidence gives. Lambert is visible in the flat "a good AI system," a
deliberately small phrase for a release the vendor sold as a leap.

> "There are a few places that we can see that GPT-5 represents a solid step on the performance trend line, but nothing like a step change. First, on LMArena, GPT-5 is fantastic, sweeping the board to #1 on all categories."

He reports the strongest fact he has, a clean sweep to #1 on LMArena, and still
sizes the release as a solid step rather than a leap. The writer is present in
that proportioning: the impressive result and his measure of how big it is stay
in one view, and he names the specific board rather than "the benchmarks."
