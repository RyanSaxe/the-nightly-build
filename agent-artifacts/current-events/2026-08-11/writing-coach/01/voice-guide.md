## How this piece should sound

The reader here already has the headline. What earns their minute is the number
the headline dropped and the fact that changes how much the development
matters, and the item should get to both fast. Hoffman's opening line does that
compression in a single sentence: it names the company by what it does, not by
a label the reader is assumed to already hold, and lands on the live question
before the paragraph is over. A brief item rarely has room for the paragraph of
throat-clearing that would normally lead into that question, so the compression
matters more here than it does in her piece.

Chivers' Iran item is the clearest model for what this brief calls its own
required contribution: state the development in one sentence, then put the
number and the caveat that qualifies it in the clauses right after it, rather
than in a separate paragraph. Oil held steady and one strait stayed blocked
while another stayed open — both facts sit inside two sentences because they
qualify each other. An item that reports a ruling, a filing, or a data release
should look for that same adjacent pairing: the figure, and the fact beside it
that decides how much the figure means.

Recent items in this desk's own record have leaned on a superlative-plus-number
mold for their deks. Chivers' Samsung/Meta item shows a different way to let a
number carry the point: two companies' results, reported straight, placed next
to each other, with the contrast between them doing the work an adjective would
otherwise do. Nothing is called dramatic or historic. The 250-fold profit
increase sits next to an 11% share drop and the reader draws the line between
them.

Where an item needs to carry a verdict about what a development means rather
than just what happened, Borenstein's piece shows one way to do it without the
reporter editorializing: put the judgment in a named source's mouth. Masters
gets the strongest lines in that piece — "we're in deep trouble" — and they
read as earned because they are his, on the record, not the writer's own
assessment dressed as reporting. This brief's items, cut to a paragraph or two
each, may have room for one such attributed line where a source hands the desk
a verdict worth keeping in full; it should stay in the source's own words
rather than getting smoothed into house style.

The AlphaFold item is worth a second look for a different reason: it defines
its one technical term inside the sentence that first uses it, not before and
not after. A reader who already saw the headline still may not know what
"protein-folding" means, and the piece hands them the definition on the way
past rather than assuming it or stopping to explain it separately. Any item
here that reaches for a term of art — a legal standard, an agency process, a
data-release methodology — has the same choice to make.

## Seth Borenstein, "Contiguous US breaks its record for hottest month ever, NOAA says"

Source: https://apnews.com/article/hottest-month-july-climate-change-4a9d612281485356103631f0f09b61af

> "KENSINGTON, Maryland (AP) — The contiguous United States sizzled to its hottest month ever last month, according to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. July averaged 76.89 degrees Fahrenheit (24.94 degrees Celsius) across the Lower 48 states, eclipsing the Dust Bowl's July 1936 by an eighth of a degree. Records go back to 1895."

The record is stated as a number against a named prior record, not as a
superlative standing on its own. "Eclipsing the Dust Bowl's July 1936 by an
eighth of a degree" tells the reader how close the margin actually was, and the
line after it tells them how far back the comparison reaches. Each of the three
sentences adds one new fact; none repeats what the last one established.

> "'It's not good when you're breaking records from the Dust Bowl, which we are in this case,' said meteorologist Jeff Masters of Yale Climate Connections. 'And that was a crazy sort of situation where you put tens of millions of people in motion. You had this great migration because of the drought and the heat that hit the center of the U.S. — just a cataclysmic event in U. S. history. If we're exceeding records from The Dust Bowl, that says that we're in deep trouble.'"

The strongest claim in the piece — "we're in deep trouble" — belongs to Masters,
not to Borenstein. Putting the verdict inside a named, on-the-record quote lets
the story carry a judgment stronger than the reporter could write in his own
voice, and the quote stays in Masters' own words, migration and all, rather
than getting cleaned up into something flatter.

> "The biggest driver of the record heat was nighttime lows that smashed the old mark for hottest minimums by 0.7 degrees, NOAA said. Hotter nights are a classic sign of human-caused climate change, according to scientists and several studies in peer-reviewed literature."

The second sentence reads like a conclusion, but it is sourced to named
studies and scientists rather than asserted by the writer, which is what keeps
it reporting instead of the writer's own inference. The number in the first
sentence is the specific fact the second sentence goes on to explain, and the
two arrive in that order.

## Tom Chivers, "Semafor Flagship: The bitter lesson"

Source: https://www.semafor.com/newsletter/07/30/2026/semafor-flagship-the-bitter-lesson

> "The US launched a "heavy wave" of attacks on Iran as fighting in the Gulf intensified. Oil prices held broadly steady overnight; traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remained disrupted, whereas tankers were still moving through Bab el-Mandeb."

The item states the event in one sentence, then gives the number that tells the
reader whether markets registered it and the caveat that qualifies it: one
strait still blocked, a different one still open. The semicolon holds a single
comparison together rather than joining two separate thoughts.

> "The South Korean giant's chipmaking division posted a 250-fold increase in quarterly operating profit as demand for semiconductors soared. Meta, however, announced lower-than-anticipated revenue forecasts and an $8 billion hit to free cash flow as it predicted over $130 billion in capital expenditure this year, sending shares tumbling 11%."

Both sentences report one company's results each, and the contrast between them
carries the point without an adjective doing that work. Each figure sits beside
its own consequence, in the same sentence, rather than in a separate summary
line at the end.

> "AlphaFold staggered biologists in 2020 when it solved the protein-folding problem: Predicting proteins' 3D shape from their sequence of chemical building blocks, thus revealing their roles in the body, something that had evaded researchers for 50 years. … AlphaFold's shutdown suggests DeepMind has learnt AI's "bitter lesson," that general-purpose systems backed by huge amounts of compute often outperform bespoke smaller projects, as evidenced by its own AlphaZero, a general-purpose game-playing AI, becoming the best chess engine back in 2017."

The colon defines the term for a reader who might not know it, inside the same
sentence that first uses it, instead of assuming it or explaining it later. The
closing sentence states a judgment about what the shutdown means, but it names
the specific mechanism behind that judgment and points to a second, dated
example rather than just asserting the pattern.

## Liz Hoffman, "Bloomberg's post-Mike moves are in motion"

Source: https://www.semafor.com/article/07/28/2026/bloombergs-post-mike-moves-are-in-motion

> "In the decades since Michael Bloomberg founded his bond-pricing data provider and built it into a media and markets giant, the question has swirled on Wall Street: What does he plan to do with it?"

One sentence compresses decades of history and lands on the live question the
piece goes on to answer, with no separate paragraph of setup ahead of it. The
company is named by what it does, not by a label the reader has to already
hold.

> "The 84-year-old Mr. Bloomberg recently transferred some of his personal stock in Bloomberg LP to Bloomberg Philanthropies, other people familiar with the matter said — a move that would minimize taxes if the company went public or sold a stake."

The fact, its sourcing, and its significance sit in one sentence: what he did,
who confirmed it, and what it would accomplish if a sale happened. The age at
the front is doing real work, since succession is the piece's subject, not a
decorative detail.

> "The former presidential candidate, meanwhile, remains ever-present at Bloomberg LP, where he routinely arrives by 7 am at its Midtown headquarters, regularly chatting in the pantry with some of his 26,000 employees and appearing at company events."

Concrete specifics — 7 am, Midtown, the pantry, 26,000 employees — stand in for
the abstract claim that he is still hands-on, so the sentence shows the fact
rather than asserting it.
