# Editorial review: current-events/2026-08-11 (editor/01)

## Skeptic

The brief runs four items and makes no single overarching argument beyond the
desk's selection; each item is its own claim, so I tested each on its display
text, its one primary and independent secondary, and its figures against the
owning source across both evidence passes.

**Item 1 — vaccine order.** The item stands on three claims: the order narrows
universal recommendation to 11 diseases; the administration reaches its "18"
baseline by counting 17 diseases plus the RSV monoclonal-antibody immunization,
while others report 17 to 11; and the autism rationale came from Trump's spoken
signing remarks, absent from the order and fact sheet. I read the White House
fact sheet and NPR directly. The fact sheet says the 2024 schedule reached "84
vaccine doses in at least 57 shots for 17 diseases, plus the RSV monoclonal
antibody immunization" — so the dose count is tied to 17 diseases, not 18. The
draft's dose-history sentence read "84 or more vaccine doses in 57 or more shots
for 18 diseases," attaching the dose count to the wrong denominator. The primary
governs; I corrected 18 to 17 so the figure matches its source. NPR confirms the
autism framing was spoken at the signing, that NPR calls the vaccine-autism
theory "thoroughly researched and debunked," the AAP rejection, and the two
measles deaths; all check out.

Two display-text failures broke here. The dek asserted "The order's case against
the schedule rests on an autism link the president asserted only in his signing
remarks." That is self-contradicting and unsupported: if the link appears only
in the remarks, the order's own case does not rest on it, and the body correctly
says the order and fact sheet never mention autism. The order's stated case is
the dose-growth and peer-nation argument. I rewrote the dek to attribute the
autism justification to Trump rather than to the order. The item headline read
"Most childhood vaccines move to a case-by-case call with a doctor." The evidence
supports a minority moving, not most: 11 diseases stay universally recommended,
down from 17 (18 with RSV), so roughly six diseases move to shared clinical
decision-making, and no dose-level breakdown supports "most." I rewrote it to
"Vaccines beyond 11 diseases move to a case-by-case call with a doctor."

**Item 2 — ballroom ruling.** Claims: a 2-1 D.C. Circuit panel held the president
very likely lacks authority to raze and rebuild the East Wing as a
90,000-square-foot, $400 million ballroom without Congress; Millett and Garcia in
the majority, Rao dissenting; National Trust brought the suit; 14-day stay; Trump
to appeal. CBS confirms every one, including the "temporary tenant, not the
owner" quote and the vote split. All display text is accurate. Note that the
primary (s3, the CADC opinion) resolves to a live PDF on the court's own media
server but was machine-unreadable to both researcher and me, so the holding and
the quotation rest on the secondary (CBS), which carries them verbatim. The
citation honestly points the quote at where it was read. Not blocking; the
primary is the genuine owning record and its link resolves.

**Item 3 — National Guard.** Every figure — 9,700 troops from 20-plus states, the
initial 800 on 11 August 2025, nearly 2,000 armed, CBO's ~$55M/month, the
comptroller's ~$1.43B through January 2029, Niskanen's $607/day, property crime
down nearly a quarter, violent crime up 3%, the task force unable to tie the
troops to any decline, and the pre-deployment 30-year violent-crime low — checks
out against Stars and Stripes (s6), which I read. The caveats survive: the mixed
crime record and the fact that the steep violent-crime decline predates the
troops both remain in the prose. But the sourcing has a hole. The cited primary,
s5, is the Senate HSGAC report; it resolves to a live PDF but was never read by
the researcher (pass 01 discarded it as unreadable) or, on the record, by the
writer, and Stars and Stripes attributes each figure to a different owner (MPD,
CBO, the Pentagon comptroller, Niskanen, the U.S. Attorney's Office), not to the
HSGAC report. So the item's data (verified) rests entirely on one secondary, and
its labeled primary owns none of the cited figures on the record before me. That
is a missing-owning-primary gap, routed below.

**Item 4 — HUD vacatur.** Claims: McElroy set aside the June Continuum of Care
overhaul (~$4B program) on 7 August for an APA notice-and-comment violation under
the McKinney-Vento Act; the $1.3B set-aside steered money to transitional housing
and treatment; she vacated the notice in full but denied a permanent injunction,
leaving HUD free to reissue after proper procedure; the ~97,000 figure is the
plaintiffs' estimate the court recorded but did not decide. Researcher pass 02
read the ten-page order in full and I confirmed the independent secondary (Rhode
Island Current, Nancy Lavin, at the Yahoo syndication) reports the ruling, the
APA violation, the injunction denial as a "drastic remedy," and the ~97,000
estimate attributed to the National Alliance to End Homelessness. Every caveat
the brief flagged survives in the prose: vacatur not injunction, reversible after
notice-and-comment, and 97,000 as a plaintiffs' estimate. The primary's
data-nb-kind (primary) is correct — the court owns the order regardless of who
hosts the copy. The href resolves to a live copy of the order. The problem is
where it lands: the copy is hosted by the New York Attorney General, a plaintiff.
That is a party's transport of a contested ruling, not the document's own page,
and it is routed to the writer below with the docket named.

## Cut

The prose is disciplined and close to the voice guide; the slop pass turned up
few failures. Four sentences drew scrutiny at the edges and inside survivors.

- "The crime record is mixed" (item 3) reduced to "The X is Y" under the
  placeholder test — an empty topic sentence the following data proves on its
  own, since property crime falling while violent crime rises already shows the
  mix, in the voice guide's own straight-contrast manner. Cut.
- "which leaves the agency free to pursue the overhaul by the proper route"
  (item 4) restated the clause immediately before it ("HUD may reissue the same
  conditions once it completes the notice-and-comment it skipped"). Redundant;
  trimmed the tail and kept the caveat.
- The semicolon in item 1 ("...RSV monoclonal-antibody immunization; NPR...")
  joined two independent counts that a period separates more plainly; changed to
  a period per the punctuation standard.
- The semicolon in item 2 ("...had brought the suit; the court stayed...")
  chained two unrelated facts; changed to a period.

One clarity repair: item 4's "put the number at risk of losing housing at about
97,000 people" misparsed; changed to "put the number of people at risk of losing
housing at about 97,000."

No prompt leakage, no borrowed phrasing from the voice-guide exemplars, and no
formula against the recent-pattern notes: no bare-date headline, no
superlative-plus-number dek, and item construction varies (a reframed claim, a
ruling, a number-led line, a procedural verb). The edition reads as one desk's
judgment of the day, not a wire scrape.

## Reader

Read straight through, the brief gives a reader what the four source sets alone
would not: each development paired with the one fact that decides how much it
means. The vaccine cut is set beside the disclosure that its debunked
justification was spoken, not written into the order; the ballroom win beside its
2-1 split and pending appeal; the Guard's troop-and-cost totals beside a crime
record the deployment cannot claim; the HUD vacatur beside its own reversibility
and the unproven 97,000. That inline weighing is the desk's contribution and it
matches the draft-handoff's original-work sentence. The prose sits closer to the
voice-guide exemplars than to a median summary. The lead headline is accurate as
the largest claim: the schedule is narrowed to 11 diseases.
