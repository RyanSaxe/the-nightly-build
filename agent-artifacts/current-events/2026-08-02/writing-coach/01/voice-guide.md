# Voice guide: current-events/2026-08-02 brief (01)

## Directive

The reader already read the headline elsewhere today. Every item's first
sentence has to start past it: not the news restated, but the number, the
actor, or the mechanism the headline had no room for. If a sentence could
run in any outlet's push notification, cut it.

Build the why-it-matters clause and its evidence as one move, not two. Don't
assert a consequence and then support it two sentences later — put the
number or the mechanism inside the sentence that makes the claim, so the
claim and its grounds land together. An item has room for one such sentence
done well, not a paragraph of qualification around it.

When the honest state of a story is that a mechanism hasn't been resolved
yet — who pays whom, under what rule, by when — naming that unresolved
mechanism precisely, with its figure, is the analysis. It is not the
brief's banned reader-handoff closer (a mood, not a mechanism), and it does
not replace the item's own stated judgment of what moved; it sits inside
the item, never as the last line.

The item heading is a claim at brief length: shorter than a front-page
headline, because this is one of several items, not the lead. Subject, then
a plain active verb, then the claim — no colon, no adjective pair, no
question. Aim for the clip of "Supreme Court strikes down tariffs," not a
label for what follows.

## Licenses

```text
form: the concrete unresolved mechanism as the stated stake
move: Axios's tariffs-refund coverage and SCOTUSblog's tariffs opinion
      analysis both close their consequence claim not on a resolved
      verdict but on a named, quantified mechanism with no answer yet —
      Axios: "Roughly $126 billion nationwide could be subject to
      refunds. But it's unclear who would get the money, or when."
      SCOTUSblog: the Court "did not weigh in... on whether or how the
      federal government should provide refunds to the importers who
      have paid the tariffs, estimated in 2025 at more than $200
      billion." The figure carries the size of the stake; naming what's
      missing carries the judgment.
bar:  The unresolved element must be a specific mechanism or process
      attached to a real figure, not a mood ("what happens next remains
      to be seen"). It supplements the item's own stated judgment of
      what moved — it cannot be the entire why-it-matters content of an
      item, and it cannot be the item's final sentence.

form: a clause of the primary source's own language, quoted inline in
      place of paraphrase
move: SCOTUSblog states the ruling's holding in its own voice, then
      drops in the Chief Justice's exact sentence to carry the
      reasoning's precision: "Based on two words separated by 16 others
      in IEEPA — 'regulate' and 'importation' — the President asserts
      the independent power to impose tariffs on imports from any
      country, of any product, at any rate, for any amount of time.
      Those words cannot bear such weight." Paraphrase would have lost
      the "16 others," which is the textual argument.
bar:  Only when the source's exact wording states the claim more
      precisely than paraphrase would, never as color or borrowed
      authority. A clause or one sentence, cited, not a block quote.
```

## Recently used, do not reuse

- Leading on the same macro/institutional spine the last several briefs
  carried (Q2 GDP and Fed dissents, the AG nomination stall, the Iran
  strikes) unless today materially advances it — and if it does, lead on
  what is new, not the standing situation.
- Dek and heading molds already banned for this series: the semicolon
  reversal ("X did A; Y refuses B"), the suspended "...and the real
  question is whether," and the comma triad closed with "and."
- Any item heading that functions as a scaffolding label ("Implications,"
  "Fallout," "What's Next") instead of the item's own claim.

## Axios, "Trump's tariffs were voided, starting a new fight over refunds"
Source: https://www.axios.com/2026/02/20/trump-scotus-tariff-refund-battle
Craft:
- cadence: Short declaratives, then one sentence lets out its full length to
  carry a causal chain: "The bottom just fell out of the administration's
  economic — and in many cases, geopolitical — agenda." The rhythm doesn't
  build gradually toward that line; it just states it.
- argument: The headline already carries the item's judgment (tariffs
  voided, refund fight begins). The body doesn't re-argue that — it extends
  it one step further, to what the fight will actually turn on.
- evidence: One dollar figure ($126 billion) stands for the scale of the
  dispute. No second number competes with it for the reader's attention.
- stance: States the practical upshot flatly rather than hedging toward
  "could reshape" or "may signal."
- notice: The news is treated as the gap between a legal outcome and its
  administrative mechanics — the ruling exists, the refund process doesn't,
  and that gap is the story, not an afterthought to it.
- diction: Plain verbs replace the ruling's own register — "voided," "fight,"
  "windfall" stand in for "invalidated," "dispute," "benefit."
- reader: Assumes the reader knows the ruling happened and spends every
  sentence on what happens because of it.
- sequencing (the missed axis): the consequence is stated before the
  mechanism producing it is explained; the machinery fills in after the
  claim, never before it.

## Amy Howe, "Supreme Court strikes down tariffs"
Source: https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/02/supreme-court-strikes-down-tariffs/
Craft:
- cadence: Opens on one unhedged sentence carrying the holding, the actor,
  the day, and the vote count together, then slows to unpack the reasoning.
- argument: Settles the ruling as fact first, then isolates one open
  practical question — refund mechanics — as the analytical payload. The two
  never blend into a single hedged sentence.
- evidence: Quotes the opinion's own operative sentence for the legal
  reasoning; uses a dollar estimate ($200 billion) for the practical stake.
  The evidence type changes with the kind of claim it's serving.
- stance: Neutral on the ruling's merits, exact about its scope — states
  what the Court decided and, with equal weight, what it declined to decide.
- notice: A decision's silence on a mechanism (refunds) is reported as news
  in its own right, not folded into the holding as a footnote.
- diction: Legal terms (IEEPA, "major questions") are named once and reused
  exactly, never re-glossed or swapped for a synonym on repeat.
- reader: Assumes legal literacy — doesn't define a 6-3 vote, but does spell
  out which two words in the statute the whole ruling turned on.
- attribution discipline (the missed axis): the Chief Justice's reasoning
  runs in his own words, in quotation marks, exactly where paraphrase would
  have lost the specific "16 others" that makes the textual argument
  legible.

## Tyler Cowen, "Mexico (Taiwan) fact of the day"
Source: https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2026/08/mexico-taiwan-fact-of-the-day.html
Craft:
- cadence: One or two sentences total. The fact and its scale arrive in the
  same breath, with no windup before either.
- argument: The comparison figures are the argument. Taiwan's Mexico-trade
  rank moving from 8th (2022) to 3rd (now) states the shift; no separate
  sentence asserts "this is a big shift."
- evidence: Two dollar totals set against each other ($46.9B from Mexico
  vs. $53.5B from Taiwan) let the reader do the ranking. A directly quoted
  outside economist stands in for editorializing, rather than a
  characterization in the writer's own voice.
- stance: Withholds a verdict on whether the shift is good policy; commits
  only to the fact that it's real and to its size.
- notice: Notices a structural reordering — server assembly now
  outranking automotive exports in the trade relationship — buried inside
  what most coverage would flatten into a single top-line GDP-style figure.
- diction: No adjective does work a number could do instead. "Quietly
  become a cornerstone" is the one evaluative phrase in the item, and it's
  earned by the two figures that follow it, not asserted ahead of them.
- reader: Assumes the reader can hold two dollar figures and a rank change
  without a restated "in other words" translating them back down.
- economy of trust (the missed axis): the item ends the instant the number
  has made its point. It never adds a closing sentence to reassure the
  reader that the point landed.
