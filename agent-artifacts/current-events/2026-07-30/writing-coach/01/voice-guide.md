# Voice guide — current-events/2026-07-30 (news brief)

## Directive
Write as a wire desk that has read the primary document. Register is a serious
paper's overnight file: calm, exact, unhurried even under a war headline. The
reader saw the headline already; your job is the sentence the headline could not
hold — the vote split, the dollar figure, the clause in the statement that
changes what the event means.

Open each item on the development itself, in the present tense, with the actors
named. No scene-setting, no "amid," no throat-clearing. The first sentence
reports what changed; the second says why it matters and to whom; a third may
carry the number or the caveat. Stop there. An item that reaches a fourth or
fifth sentence is usually reaching for an essay the brief does not want.

State the judgment as a fact about the world, not as a verdict on your own
selection. "The decision keeps the benchmark at its lowest since 2022 while
three governors wanted it higher" is a judgment. "This matters because" is
self-grading; cut it and let the sentence carry the weight. Never close an item
on a line that hands the point back to the reader ("what happens next is
anyone's guess"). End on the concrete consequence, then stop.

Carry every number with a comparison the reader already holds: a premium against
its dollar change, a strike against what triggered it, an index against the
level that historically signals trouble. A bare figure is a fact the reader
cannot use.

Four items must read as one paper. Keep the same sentence temperature across
all four: the household-economy item and the war item get the same flat,
verified voice. The war does not get louder verbs; the survey does not get
softer ones. One name per thing (the Fed, not "the central bank" three lines
later). Let the facts rank themselves by what they are, not by adjectives.

When an item extends a story the paper has covered, say so in a clause and add
only the new development — do not re-explain the whole matter.

Recently used, do not reuse: the "officials say" / "according to a person
familiar" hedged-attribution opener; the emergency-application explainer shape
("An emergency application is not a ruling on the merits...") that framed a
recent legal item; any colon-subtitle headline. Vary the opener across the four
items so they do not all begin with a subject-verb-object of the same length.

## The Economist, "The world this week" (Politics/Business this week)
Source: https://www.economist.com/the-world-this-week
Craft:
- cadence: one development per short paragraph; sentences short, front-loaded
  with the event, no windup.
- argument: the paragraph implies the judgment through selection and the one
  fact it chooses to include, rather than announcing significance.
- evidence: a single hard number or named actor per item does the anchoring.
- stance: dry, unexcited; the same temperature whether the item is a coup or a
  merger.
- notice: what to include is the whole craft — the clause it keeps is the one a
  reader could not have guessed from the headline.
- diction: plain, concrete, Anglo-Saxon verbs; no house catchphrase.
- reader: assumes the headline is known; never recaps it.
- the move the axes miss: ruthless compression — every item could be twice as
  long and is deliberately not.
Calibration (texture, not a quote): each entry reads like a fact filed by
someone who already weighed ten others and kept this one; the flatness is the
authority.

## Matt Levine, Bloomberg "Money Stuff"
Source: https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/authors/ARbTQlRLRjE/matthew-s-levine
Craft:
- cadence: conversational but disciplined; a claim, then the mechanism that
  makes it true.
- argument: takes a filing or a number and explains the incentive or
  consequence a press release hides.
- evidence: quotes the primary document's own words, then reads them plainly.
- stance: skeptical of official framing; trusts the document over the summary.
- notice: fastens on the one clause in a filing that the filer wishes were less
  legible.
- diction: precise nouns, no euphemism ("the subsidy that cut premiums," not
  "a support mechanism").
- reader: assumes a numerate reader; explains the mechanism, not the vocabulary.
- the move the axes miss: turns a dry document into a judgment by reading it
  literally and following the money.
Calibration (texture, not a quote): the voice earns trust by quoting the source
against its own spin, then saying the obvious thing the spin avoided.

## David Leonhardt, The New York Times, "The Morning"
Source: https://www.nytimes.com/newsletters/the-morning
Craft:
- cadence: a lead sentence that states the stakes, then supporting facts in
  descending order of importance.
- argument: "why it matters" is built into the ordering of facts, never labeled.
- evidence: comparisons the reader already holds (a change against a baseline,
  a figure against last year).
- stance: measured, explains without condescension.
- notice: picks the number that reframes the story.
- diction: clean, unshowy; no jargon left undefined.
- reader: a generalist who wants to understand, not be impressed.
- the move the axes miss: makes significance felt through structure, so the
  piece never has to assert its own importance.
Calibration (texture, not a quote): the paragraph tells you what to think by the
order in which it tells you what happened.
