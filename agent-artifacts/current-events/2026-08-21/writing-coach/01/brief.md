# Writing-coach brief — current-events/2026-08-21

## What this edition has to avoid

The commission names five patterns visible across the last two weeks of
briefs, and this edition is graded against them specifically:

- **Dek.** No two-clause dek joined by "and," no "on a day that also…" tail,
  no "ruling that…" tail. One sentence, one fact, no suspended question, no
  semicolon reversal, no comma triad closed with "and."
- **Openers.** No item may open on "[Official] [did X] on [date]." That
  construction reports paperwork, not the event. Lead with what changed, who
  it changed for, or the number that carries the story; let the date land as
  supporting detail a sentence later, not as the subject of sentence one.
- **Closers.** No item may end on an "X, and Y" synthesis sentence that hands
  the point back to the reader as a summary tag. The analysis has to land
  inside the item's body, not get stapled to the end as a moral.
- **Headlines.** Recent items nearly all restate the dek as
  actor-verb-object, naming the official ("Trump approves…," "Judge Leon
  denies…"). At most one or two of this edition's five headlines may take
  that shape. Try a number-led headline, a "what X couldn't do" construction,
  a noun-phrase-plus-contrast, before reaching for actor-verb-object again.
- **Running tallies.** No "as of [day]" framing and no casualty/outage counts
  carried over from a prior day's item. Every fact in this edition has to be
  this date's own development, not a restatement of one already in the
  library.

## What earned analysis looks like here

This is a brief: one to three sentences of prose per item, so there is no
room for analysis that doesn't do real work. The required contribution is a
premise-to-consequence move in a sentence or two — not a recap dressed as a
takeaway. Before an item ships, it should pass this test: delete the last
sentence. If nothing is lost, the sentence was decoration; write a
replacement that says something the cited record actually supports, or cut
it. Three of tonight's items involve a court declaring something broadly and
then granting relief narrowly — that gap is real and checkable in the primary
documents, not an inference. Say what the ruling actually binds, not what the
headline about it implies.

## The slop test, applied to a five-item brief

Run the placeholder test from `spec/slop.md` on every sentence that sounds
like the best line in its item, especially the first and last sentence of
each item and of the whole piece. A brief has five first sentences and five
last sentences plus the dek — six edges per proof-read, not one. Watch
specifically for: decorative analysis verbs ("underscores," "reflects,"
"highlights") standing in for a real claim; vague attribution ("experts say,"
"observers note") where a named source is available and should be used
instead; and puffery on routine developments — a court ruling is not
"landmark" or "sweeping" until the prose has shown the reader why, and this
edition's rulings are the opposite of sweeping in their actual relief, so
that language would be wrong twice over.

## Register

Wire-service, per the brief template's identity: write for a reader who
already saw the headline elsewhere tonight and needs the number or the
caveat that headline dropped. No throat-clearing, no scene-setting, no
address to the reader. Get to the fact.
