## Item 2 — Fourth Circuit, ICE at houses of worship

**Primary:** U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit, *Philadelphia
Yearly Meeting of the Religious Society of Friends, et al. v. U.S.
Department of Homeland Security*, No. 25-1512 —
https://www.ca4.uscourts.gov/opinions/251512.P.pdf (fetched and read in
full, 40 pages)

- Argued May 6, 2026; **decided August 18, 2026**. Panel: Agee, Harris
  (Circuit Judges), Keenan (Senior Circuit Judge, wrote the opinion). Agee
  filed a separate concurrence. Appeal from D. Md. (Judge Theodore D.
  Chuang), No. 8:25-cv-00243-TDC.
- Plaintiffs: Philadelphia, New England, Baltimore, Adelphi, Richmond, and
  New York Yearly/Monthly Meetings of the Religious Society of Friends
  (Quakers); Sikh Temple Sacramento; Cooperative Baptist Fellowship (a
  network of "more than 1,400 individual Baptist congregations").
- Background: in 2021 DHS Secretary Mayorkas issued a policy limiting
  enforcement "to the fullest extent possible" at protected/sensitive
  locations including houses of worship. On January 20, 2025, Acting
  Secretary Benjamine Huffman rescinded it, replaced by discretion and
  "common sense." A DHS press release quoted in the opinion: *"This action
  empowers the brave men and women in CBP and ICE ... Criminals will no
  longer be able to hide in America's schools and churches to avoid arrest.
  The Trump Administration will not tie the hands of our brave law
  enforcement, and instead trusts them to use common sense."* (J.A. 173,
  quoted at op. 7.)
- Injury shown: one CBF congregation's ESL program attendance fell 66
  percent (op. 11); Sikh Temple Sacramento serves roughly 30,000-person
  Sacramento-area Sikh population, ~90% first/second-generation immigrants
  (op. 11).
- Holding: plaintiffs have standing (op. 13–22); district court did not
  abuse its discretion finding plaintiffs likely to succeed on their RFRA
  claim (op. 22–33) — DHS's policy substantially burdens religious exercise
  and the government **waived** its compelling-interest/strict-scrutiny
  defense by failing to raise evidence or argument for it in the district
  court (op. 29–30, 38–40: "It's very hard to win a case without evidence or
  legal argument," quoting oral argument).
- Scope, footnote 4 (op. 13) and Agee concurrence (op. 36–40): the district
  court **denied a nationwide injunction** and denied a warrant requirement;
  neither is at issue on appeal. The injunction "enjoins 'potential or
  actual immigration enforcement actions in or near' the identified list of
  places of worship connected to the plaintiffs" only — it does not restrict
  DHS at any other house of worship or at other "protected areas" (schools,
  hospitals) the 2021 policy also covered. It does **not** enjoin
  warrant-authorized arrests (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(f)(1)), and it
  preserves the 2021 policy's own exigent-circumstance exceptions (national
  security threat, imminent risk of harm, hot pursuit, risk evidence will be
  destroyed).

(confirms date, outcome, and plaintiff description for independent
corroboration).

## Item 3 — Seventh Circuit, Planned Parenthood Indiana speech case

**Primary:** U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, *Planned
Parenthood Great Northwest, Hawai'i, Alaska, Indiana, Kentucky, Inc. v.
Commissioner of the Indiana State Department of Health, et al.*, No.
24-2219 —
https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026%2FD08-18%2FC%3A24-2219%3AJ%3AHamilton%3Aaut%3AT%3AfnOp%3AN%3A3593042%3AS%3A0
(fetched and read in full, 54 pages including concurrence; the served PDF
carries a stray header line before the `%PDF-` marker that has to be
stripped for a PDF reader to open it — the URL itself resolves at HTTP 200
and is the court's own served document)

- Argued September 9, 2025; **decided August 18, 2026**. Panel: Rovner,
  Hamilton (wrote the majority opinion), Scudder (concurring in the
  judgment only, on narrower grounds). Appeal from S.D. Ind. (Judge Sarah
  Evans Barker), No. 1:17-cv-01636-SEB-CSW.
- Indiana's 2017 "aid-or-assist" law (SEA 404 § 5, Ind. Code
  § 16-34-2-4.2(c)) bars anyone from knowingly aiding an unemancipated
  pregnant minor in obtaining an abortion without the same parental
  consent/judicial-bypass process (§ 16-34-2-4(b)) required for the abortion
  itself: notarized written consent, government-issued ID, and evidence of
  the parental relationship. As applied here, that process was required
  before a physician could tell a minor about, or refer her to, a legal
  out-of-state abortion provider.
- Since Indiana's post-*Dobbs* near-total abortion ban took effect in 2023,
  Planned Parenthood no longer performs abortions in Indiana, so the
  Commissioner of the Indiana Department of Health was dropped as a
  defendant as moot; the Medical Licensing Board remains the defendant
  because it can still discipline physicians' licenses.
- Holding: providing information/referrals about legal out-of-state
  abortion care is speech (not "speech integral to unlawful conduct," not
  "incidental to conduct"); the law is a content-based and viewpoint-based
  restriction subject to strict scrutiny; Indiana's asserted interest in the
  parent-child relationship is legitimate but the state offered **no
  evidence** the restriction serves it and the law is both over- and
  under-inclusive (it doesn't apply to any other pregnancy-related medical
  information, and the information itself is freely available online).
- Scudder's concurrence in the judgment (op. 47–54): agrees only on the
  narrow ground that the law's **notary requirement** is overinclusive —
  "Notaries seem to be going extinct in today's digital age" — while arguing
  Indiana does hold a compelling interest in parental involvement even at
  the information-gathering stage, and would uphold the consent-and-judicial-bypass
  framework itself. This is a substantive split in reasoning behind a
  unanimous result, not just a stylistic disagreement.

## Item 4 — Census Bureau noncitizen-voting brief

**Primary:** U.S. Census Bureau, "Noncitizen Voting in the 2020 Election, A
Beginning Analysis," August 2026 —
https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2026/dir/noncitizen-voting-in-the-2020-election.pdf
(fetched and read in full, 7 pages)

- No author is named anywhere in the document (unusual for a Census Bureau
  publication).
- Method: matches the **DataClear 2020 commercial voter file** (a "national
  compilation of state registration rolls and vote-history extracts") to
  federal noncitizen-status records (USCIS, State Department, ICE, SSA
  citizen codes, ITIN records) via the Bureau's probabilistic
  Person-Identification-Validation System, then screens for citizenship
  evidence (dated naturalization oath, U.S. birth record, dated passport).
  A person with no citizenship signal that can be dated at or before the
  vote defaults into the noncitizen count.
- Coverage check against the official benchmark (p. 4–5, Table A): DataClear
  captured 146,700,000 of 159,934,200 ballots the states certified to the
  federal Election Assistance Commission for 2020 — **91.7 percent**
  nationally. State range: **74.5 percent in Utah** (low) to 97.1 percent in
  New Jersey (high); most states fall 87–94 percent.
- Result: "over 24,000" of "over 128 million" readily analyzed voter records
  indicate noncitizen status "with high confidence." 32 million voter
  records remain unanalyzed. Table 1 breaks the 24,000 by state (California
  4,300 highest; several states "N < 15").
- 24,000 / ~160,000,000 total 2020 ballots ≈ 0.015 percent (computed
  independently from the report's own totals, not stated in the report
  itself).

**Secondary:** NPR, "Experts raise alarms over Census Bureau report Trump is
touting on noncitizen voting" —
https://www.npr.org/2026/08/19/nx-s1-5938799/trump-census-bureau-noncitizen-voting

- Trump's claim, via social media: the report "proves that over 24,000
  Noncitizens voted illegally" in 2020 and the number will "explode" once
  the remaining 32 million records are checked; he ties this to his
  long-running claim that he "actually won" the 2020 election.
- Named critics: Beth Jarosz (Georgetown), on the missing author and
  methodology detail; Terri Ann Lowenthal (former House subcommittee staff
  director), on the report falling "beneath the Census Bureau's stellar
  reputation"; Justin Levitt (Loyola Law School), on the 24,000 figure
  potentially falling within the process's own margin of error.

The published item's analytical claim — that the report's own 91.7 percent
national match rate (as low as 74.5 percent in one state) implies a
miss/misassignment rate many multiples of the 0.015 percent fraud figure —
is derived directly from the numbers in the primary document, not
paraphrased from the NPR piece or its named critics.

## Item 5 — N.D. Texas, ATF ghost-gun rule

**Primary:** U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas, Fort
Worth Division, *Defense Distributed, et al. v. Todd Blanche, in his
official capacity as Attorney General of the United States, et al.*, No.
4:22-cv-00691-O, Opinion & Order —
https://www.ammoland.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Defense-Distributed-opinion-8.19.26.pdf
(fetched and read in full, 29 pages; hosted copy of the filed court document
— PACER Doc. 330 — confirmed by internal case caption, docket number,
judge's signature block, and filing stamp "Case 4:22-cv-00691-O Document 330
Filed 08/17/26" on every page; resolves at HTTP 200; the court's own
`courts.mo.gov`/`uscourts.gov`-style direct hosting was not locatable within
the research window, so this hosted copy of the filed document is the
resolving primary link used)

- **Filed/signed August 17, 2026** by Chief U.S. District Judge Reed
  O'Connor.
- Background: challenge to ATF's April 2022 "Final Rule" (87 Fed. Reg.
  24,652) redefining "frame or receiver" under the Gun Control Act to cover
  partially complete/unfinished firearm parts and kits ("ghost guns").
  Originally *VanDerStok v. Garland*; the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in
  *Bondi v. VanDerStok*, 604 U.S. 458, rejected the original plaintiffs'
  facial APA challenge and upheld the ATF's basic authority to regulate at
  least some partial frames/kits. On remand, only Defense Distributed and
  the Second Amendment Foundation pursued the remaining constitutional
  claims.
- Holding: the court **grants summary judgment to plaintiffs** on their
  Second Amendment and Fifth Amendment void-for-vagueness claims (and to
  defendants on the plaintiffs' APA claims — failure to consider, change of
  position, delegation/major-questions — which the court rejected).
- Vagueness: the rule's "readily" standard for when unfinished material
  becomes a regulated frame/receiver "is left to the subjective
  determination of the ATF and does not provide fair notice to the reader
  about when a component becomes subject to enforcement" (op. 13).
- Second Amendment: applying *Bruen*/*Rahimi* history-and-tradition
  analysis, the court found the government's cited colonial-era
  barrel-proofing and gunpowder-storage laws addressed product/public safety
  ("why") through inspection/marking/storage rules ("how"), not crime
  tracing through component regulation, and are not historically analogous;
  self-manufacture of firearms was a widespread, largely unregulated
  American tradition through the 19th century (op. 17–21).
- Remedy (op. 27–29): the court **declares** 27 C.F.R. § 478.11 and §
  478.12(c) unconstitutional under the Second Amendment and void for
  vagueness under the Fifth Amendment, and **enjoins** enforcement only
  "against Defense Distributed, SAF, or any of SAF's current or future
  members with respect to Defense Distributed's M1911 80% Frames and G80
  Build Kit, Unfinished Receiver, and Grip Module" — four named products,
  not a nationwide vacatur.
