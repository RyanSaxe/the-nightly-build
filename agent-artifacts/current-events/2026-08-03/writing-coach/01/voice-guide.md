# Voice guide: current-events/2026-08-03 brief (01)

## Directive

Register: calm, exact, quietly opinionated. Write to a peer who already read the
day's headlines and wants the one judgment a wire will not make. The house
baseline (Hashimoto-calm, argued from first principles) still governs diction and
punctuation. What a brief needs on top of it is compression discipline the
default does not teach, because the default is tuned for a long teaching piece
and here every item is a single compressed judgment.

Four moves will change sentences in this article:

- **Spend the item's first words on the consequence or the complication, not the
  event.** The reader has the event; the headline restates it. The sentence that
  follows either says what the development changes or names the fact that
  complicates the obvious read. An item that opens by re-narrating what happened
  has wasted its only sentence.
- **One item, one judgment.** When an item runs to two or three sentences, each
  later sentence earns its place by adding a fact the first implied but did not
  state (the number, the dropped caveat, the counter-fact), never by restating
  the first in new words. If a sentence could be cut and the reader loses no
  fact, cut it.
- **Reach for the specific verb and the exact figure before any modifier.** "Rose
  250-fold" over "surged"; the dollar cost over "costly." A precise verb lets you
  delete the adjective that was propping up a vague one.
- **Compress by dropping what the reader knows, not by packing clauses.** A short
  sentence here is short because the recap is gone, not because two ideas were
  crushed into one. Never let compression become a clause chain.

Do not use bolded inline labels to structure an item ("Why it matters," "The big
picture," "Between the lines"). That furniture is a brief-genre tell; make the
sentence itself carry the turn.

## Licenses

```text
form: pointed consequence sentence (a verdict-shaped clause)
move: the strongest daily briefs close an item on what the development does, not
      on grading it — Semafor lands the item on a specific effect (oil held
      steady, the Strait stayed disrupted) rather than on a stakes announcement.
bar:  the clause states one specific, sourced effect a reader could check. If it
      announces importance ("this matters because," "the real story is") instead
      of naming the effect, cut it. One verdict per item, and it is the item's
      only job, not an add-on after a recap.
```

```text
form: the complication turn (a "but/yet" clause that undercuts the obvious read)
move: the best briefs surface the fact that complicates the wire framing — a
      loosening "though experts questioned whether it changes much," a profit
      that is "the same bet expressed in multiple ways." The turn carries a real,
      named counter-fact.
bar:  the clause after the turn is a specific fact from the cited record, not a
      hedge the writer invented to sound balanced. If the thing being corrected
      is a strawman no one claimed, delete the turn. At most one per item.
```

```text
form: dry wit anchored in the story's own nouns
move: the Economist can afford a witty head only because the line is built from
      the story's own material and the prose beside it states the point plainly;
      Levine's humor rides the specifics of the deal, never a generic quip.
bar:  the wit uses only nouns already in the item, and the item still delivers
      its full point with the wit removed. No pun on an abstraction, no house
      catchphrase, no wordplay standing in for the consequence. At most one such
      touch across the whole brief; if two items reach for it, the drier one wins.
```

## Recently used, do not reuse

- The semicolon-reversal dek mold ("X did A; Y refuses B").
- The comma-triad dek mold (three clauses joined by commas and closed with "and").
- The paired-adjective-triad headline ("Faster Models, Firmer Rules, Tighter
  Supply") that performs comprehensiveness instead of committing to one
  development.
- Bolded axiom labels inside items ("Why it matters," "The big picture"), a
  scannability tell borrowed from feed-style briefs.

## Semafor, "Flagship: The bitter lesson" (daily global brief)
Source: https://www.semafor.com/newsletter/07/30/2026/semafor-flagship-the-bitter-lesson
Craft:
- cadence: two sentences per item at most; the first states the development in a
  full clause, the second adds one consequence or contrast. No sentence runs
  long enough to need a comma splice.
- argument: each item is a claim about what moved, carried by the lead clause;
  the follow-on sentence supplies the fact that makes the claim non-obvious.
- evidence: a single hard particular per item does the work (a 250-fold profit
  rise, oil holding steady, tankers still moving through one strait but not
  another). The number or the named place is the payoff, not decoration.
- stance: present but under the surface; the writer chooses which second fact to
  print, and that choice is the opinion. It never states a stance as such.
- notice: pairs a development with the thing it complicates in the same breath (a
  reform "though experts questioned whether it will make a difference").
- diction: concrete nouns, plain verbs, no intensifiers. "Loosened
  restrictions," "shuttered," "floated" carry the weight without adverbs.
- reader: assumes the reader knows the running story and needs only what is new
  on this day; spends zero words re-establishing context.
- the move the axes miss: the item ends on the consequence, never on a line that
  hands the point back to the reader. There is no "what this means for you."

## The Economist Style Guide (craft doctrine, as distilled by Evan Hahn)
Source: https://evanhahn.com/economist-style-guide-book-takeaways/
Craft:
- cadence: short sentences by default, because they are more memorable and easier
  to read; length varies only when a longer sentence is in full control.
- argument: one thought per paragraph, so a reader extracting the point of each
  paragraph reconstructs the whole. In a brief, one thought per item.
- evidence: the specific word replaces the vague word plus its modifiers — "strut"
  over "walk," "murmur" over "say" — so a precise verb lets you dispense with
  adjectives entirely.
- stance: clarity of writing follows clarity of thought; decide the point first,
  then say it as plainly as possible, rather than hedging on the page.
- notice: do not spend sentences setting the scene or sketching background; open
  on the matter itself.
- diction: prefer the plain word to the grand one — "get" not "obtain," "make"
  not "manufacture," "give up" not "relinquish."
- reader: treated as intelligent and impatient; the writing respects their time
  by cutting throat-clearing.
- the move the axes miss: the colon is reserved for setup and delivery — the
  clause before it stands alone and promises the payoff after. Use it for that,
  not as a general connector.

## Matt Levine, "Money Stuff" method (as described in Harvard Magazine)
Source: https://www.harvardmagazine.com/2025/07/harvard-bloomberg-column-matt-levine
Craft:
- cadence: conversational and unhurried inside a tight word budget; the sentence
  sounds spoken but every clause is load-bearing.
- argument: takes a handful of headlines and reframes each so the point rings
  true to a specialist while showing an angle the specialist, in the weeds, had
  missed. The reframe is the value, not the summary.
- evidence: grounds an abstract move in the concrete specifics of the actual
  deal or filing, so the mechanism is visible rather than asserted.
- stance: openly holds a view and reasons to it in the open; the humor is the
  argument compressed, not a garnish on top.
- notice: catches the part of a story everyone repeated without examining, then
  says what is actually strange about it.
- diction: precise about the thing itself, casual about the framing; the exact
  term when it matters, plain speech around it.
- reader: assumes fluency and rewards it by skipping the primer and going
  straight to the interesting problem.
- the move the axes miss: the wit is always built from the story's own material
  (the specific dispute, the specific dodge), never a detachable joke; strip the
  humor and the point survives intact.

## Calibration note

The house default would push a brief toward either flat wire neutrality or
full-length analysis. This article is neither. It is a set of compressed
judgments for a reader who has the news and wants the consequence: the headline
commits to the development, the one to three sentences spend the reader's minute
on what it changes or what complicates it, and the through-line dek is a stance,
not a table of contents. If a writer following this guide produced prose that
recaps events, stacks clauses, or grades its own stakes, the guide was not
followed.
