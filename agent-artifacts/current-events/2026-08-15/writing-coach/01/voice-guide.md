# Voice guide: current-events/2026-08-15

## How this piece should sound

The reader of this brief already saw the headline somewhere else today. The
job of each item is what the AP Business Highlights digest and the Semafor
Flagship item on NATO do below: supply the figure or the exact phrase the
headline had no room for, and stop. Neither writer below tells the reader
that a development is significant before showing why; the fact, stated once
and precisely, carries that weight itself.

Chivers's NATO item is the direct model for any item built around an
official's own words. He doesn't characterize Trump's answer on Article 5 as
evasive or alarming and then supply the quote as evidence — the quote itself,
"depends on your definition," is the entire evidence, dropped straight into
the sentence that reports the story. Where this brief has an on-the-record
line from a court, an agency, or an official that carries the story's
ambiguity or its commitment, that line belongs in the sentence, not
summarized around.

The two AP passages below are the model for numbers. "Shares of WeWork,
which cost more than $400 two years ago, can now be had for less than $1"
and "Her plea sent her to jail for 27 days of a 180 day sentence" both do the
thing `spec/editorial.md`'s numbers section asks for without naming it: a
figure the reader can't independently scale gets set next to a comparison
the reader already holds, in the same sentence, so no adjective has to do
that work. This brief will have dollar figures, vote counts, docket numbers,
and dates that mean nothing on their own; give each one something to sit
next to, the way both passages do, rather than a word like "sharply" or
"significant" standing in for the comparison.

Duara's opening two sentences on the Hernandez case are the model for how an
item starts: on the person, the document, or the action, never on a
category. "There's no question that Angela Hernandez signed a form" commits
to a fact in its first clause. An item here that opens on a category
("Federal courts continued to grapple with...") is the dangling-referent
failure `spec/slop.md` names, since the specific actor the sentence needs
hasn't appeared yet. Every item's own actors — the agency, the plaintiff, the
member of Congress — belong in that first sentence, doing the subject-verb
work the headline standard already asks of the headline itself.

None of the passages below close on a sentence that reflects on what the
development means for the reader; the WeWork item ends on the $1 share
price, the RFK item ends on the study that didn't exist, the Hernandez item
ends on the detention at the airport. That is the shape for this brief's own
items, which the template and the commission both already require: end on
the fact the item was reporting, at whatever point that fact runs out,
rather than a line that hands judgment to the reader or restates the
headline. Where an item is a story this paper has covered before and the
15th moves it forward, say what moved in that same register — a new filing,
a new figure, a new ruling — rather than a sentence announcing that the
story has developed.

## Tom Chivers, "Semafor Flagship: Throwing off old leaders"

Source: https://www.semafor.com/newsletter/06/25/2025/semafor-flagship

> "Iran's brief but brutal war with Israel has left its theocratic regime
> facing an uncertain future. Israeli strikes killed many of Ayatollah Ali
> Khamenei's key commanders, with Iranian political and military figures
> apparently plotting to sideline him. "Everybody knows Khamenei's days are
> numbered," one alleged conspirator told The Atlantic. An Iranian reformist
> told the Financial Times that the Islamic republic would likely endure but
> be profoundly reshaped by the short conflict: "It won't change in name but
> will change in manner.""

Two outlets' sourced lines sit inside four sentences without either being
introduced as "notably" or "importantly." The second quote is doing real
work: it doesn't restate the first, it gives the opposing prediction, so the
item holds a live disagreement about the region's future in the space most
writers would spend on one clean claim.

> "US President Donald Trump alarmed NATO allies by once again hinting he
> may water down Washington's commitment to mutual defense. Ahead of meeting
> other leaders in The Hague, Trump said Washington's willingness to uphold
> Article 5 of the alliance's founding treaty "depends on your definition,""
> a remark that could undermine the organization's entire purpose."

The quote is the caveat the headline verb ("alarmed") couldn't carry. Chivers
doesn't tell the reader Trump was evasive; the four words Trump actually said
are the evasion, on the page, and the sentence after it states the stakes as
a fact about the alliance's charter rather than as the writer's opinion of
how alarming the remark was.

> "Kennedy is battling the scientific community on several fronts: A report
> into thimerosal by an ally of his, published on a government website,
> cited a study that did not exist, Reuters reported."

One sourced fact, placed at the end of the item, outweighs the paragraph of
context around it. The sentence doesn't editorialize about what a fabricated
citation means for Kennedy's credibility — it names the citation, names who
reported it, and lets the reader do that arithmetic.

## Nigel Duara / CalMatters, "Court rules defendants must 'meaningfully understand' plea deals" (The Associated Press)

Source: https://apnews.com/article/california-supreme-court-ruling-plea-deals-e4b5f05d717327bae3559defa5b14393

> "At her trial, there's no question that Angela Hernandez signed a form
> acknowledging that she could be deported after a criminal conviction. An
> interpreter certified that they had translated the whole form and her
> attorney was confident Hernandez understood it."

The first sentence commits to a fact about one named person before the piece
has told the reader who the ruling affects in general. Nothing here is
scaffolding for the news to come; the specific paperwork and the specific
interpreter are already the story, and the reader learns what the case is
about by learning what happened to her.

> "But the California Supreme Court said in a ruling handed down Thursday
> that it's not enough to check a form's boxes — a defendant has to
> "meaningfully understand" the legal consequences of a plea deal."

The court's own two words carry the new legal standard, quoted rather than
paraphrased as "a higher bar" or "stricter requirement." The sentence before
the dash states the old, inadequate standard concretely — checking a form's
boxes — so the new one has something specific to be higher than.

> "Her plea sent her to jail for 27 days of a 180 day sentence. Two years
> later, she obtained permission from the state probation department to
> travel to Mexico. When she returned to Los Angeles International Airport,
> she was detained by a customs official, who initiated removal proceedings
> against her."

The sentence gives the served time against the full sentence, not "part of
her sentence." The consequence that made this case worth a ruling — the
airport detention — arrives as a plain sequence of what happened, with no
sentence stepping back to call it a turning point.

## The Associated Press, "Business Highlights" (Nov. 6, 2023)

Source: https://apnews.com/article/business-highlights-stock-market-ai-google-fortnite-a43a2bbff8a7d07f95b77710513dae22

> "Virginia and Georgia dropped the algorithm after trial runs, noting its
> inability to produce adoptions. Tennessee scrapped the program before
> rolling it out, saying it didn't work with their system, and social
> workers reported mixed experiences in Florida."

Four states, four distinct outcomes, in two sentences, with no sentence
generalizing them into "several states have had problems." Each verb is
different — dropped, scrapped, reported — because each state's experience
actually was different, and the item trusts the four facts side by side to
make the pattern visible without naming it.

> "The S&P 500 rose 7.64 points, or 0.2%, to 4,365.98. The Dow Jones
> Industrial Average gained 34.54 points, or 0.1%, to 34,095.86. The Nasdaq
> composite added 40.50 points, or 0.3%, to 13,518.78. The Russell 2000
> index of smaller companies fell 22.77 points, or 1.3% to 1,737.94."

Four indexes, four exact figures, and one of the four moved the opposite
direction from the other three — the item reports that without flagging it
as a surprise. Nothing here is rounded to "modest gains"; the point level and
the percentage appear together every time, so a reader with no context for
"4,365.98" still has the percent to hold onto.

> "Shares of WeWork, which cost more than $400 two years ago, can now be had
> for less than $1."

One sentence, one comparison, and the item ends there. The $400 figure does
the work that an adjective like "collapsed" would otherwise have to do, and
because the comparison is to the same stock at an earlier point, a reader
who has never priced a share of anything still knows exactly how far it
fell.
