# Voice guide: current-events/2026-08-14 (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a brief: four to six items for a reader who already saw the headline,
each one a judgment about what moved and why, not a recap. The items will cover
things like a court ruling, an agency's compliance deadline, a confirmation, a
monthly data release — developments with a named actor and a mechanism, not a
mood. Land the significance in the shortest sentence the specifics allow, after
the specifics, the way Allen's "Why it matters: A more conservative Supreme
Court may approve." arrives only once five bullets of state-by-state detail
have already done the work. The item shouldn't need a labeled sentence to do
this — this template doesn't bold "Why it matters" the way Axios does — but the
move of compressing the payoff to the fewest possible words, placed last, is
what to take from it.

Reach for a number before an adjective. Dolan anchors "unnerve...markets" to
"almost twice what it was last year" instead of leaving the adjective to carry
the claim alone, and Allen lets "$39,000 for one day of shopping" replace
"lavish" entirely. This brief's items will have figures available — a payout,
a vote count, a caseload, a percentage change — and the house numbers standard
already asks for the sourced figure and a comparison the reader can hold over
a bare magnitude word. These two instructions are the same instruction; use it.

Put the actor and the mechanism in the sentence that states what happened, not
a sentence later. Capelouto's FTC sentence names the agency, the businesses it
binds, and the 180-day trigger in one clause. This brief's items involve named
institutions — a court, an agency, a chamber of Congress — and since the
template forbids a dangling reference for a reader arriving from a link, that
actor belongs in the opening clause, not established after the fact.

Where a development's meaning is best carried by someone who can be named on
the record, let their words close the item rather than folding their verdict
into the brief's own sentence. Allen ends the Trump-economy item on Bruce
Mehlman's quote instead of writing the conclusion himself. This brief already
requires an independent account alongside the primary source for each item;
where that account states a judgment plainly, quoting it can do work a
synthesized closing sentence would only repeat.

When the reporting shows a sequence without yet showing causation, mark the
sequence and stop, the way Capelouto's "Hours after his criticism, Indian
officials rejected the auction route" states an order of events and lets the
reader draw the inference, rather than asserting that the criticism caused the
reversal. Some of this brief's items will have that shape — a ruling, then a
response; an announcement, then a reaction — and the plain time-marker is
often the honest version of the sentence a writer is tempted to write with
"because."

None of the three exemplars avoid addressing their reader directly or
narrating their own newsletter — Allen's "Thank you for reading Axios on
Mother's Day" and Dolan's "I'll get into that and more below" are built into
their form. This brief's template forbids both: no self-reference, and no
closing line that hands the point back to the reader. That constraint comes
from the brief template and the house slop standard, not from any exemplar
here, and it holds regardless of how naturally a personal aside would close an
item.

## J.D. Capelouto, "Semafor Flagship: We all read newspapers, right?"

Source: https://www.semafor.com/newsletter/10/16/2024/semafor-flagship-we-all-read-newspapers-right

> "Sluggish retail spending and an exodus of capital have slowed Hong Kong's post-pandemic economic comeback, and Chief Executive John Lee used his annual policy address to say the city must "embrace changes.""

Two causes are named before the official's phrase arrives, so by the time the
quote lands, the reader already knows what "embrace changes" is standing in
for. The attribution is specific — the annual policy address, not "officials
said" — which is where Capelouto's own reporting is visible: he has gone to
find the actual occasion for the remark rather than a generic hook to hang it
on.

> "Hours after his criticism, Indian officials rejected the auction route that would have likely favored domestic players. While revenue for the Indian space broadband market could eventually total $1 billion annually, one industry expert said the latest spat is simply an "ego battle.""

The sequence is marked with a plain clock ("hours after"), not a causal claim.
The dollar figure sizes the stakes before the quote undercuts them, so scale
and skepticism sit next to each other in the same short passage instead of the
writer picking one and asserting it.

> "Following an increase in complaints about hard-to-cancel services, the Federal Trade Commission's new rules — which take effect in 180 days — will require businesses ranging from gyms to streaming sites to simplify canceling subscriptions so people aren't locked into paying for services they don't want."

One sentence carries the actor, the number that tells a reader when the rule
starts to bind, the range of businesses it covers, and the plain reason it
exists — in that order, with nothing held back for a separate sentence to
announce that the rule matters.

## Mike Allen, "Axios AM: Mike's Big 6"

Source: https://www.axios.com/newsletters/axios-am-43dd2dc7-b8ac-4afa-b4d5-0843aae0cda7.html

> "Leaked NRA expense documents show Wayne LaPierre billed the group's ad agency $39,000 for one day of shopping at Zegna in Beverly Hills, and had the agency cover $13,800 in rent for a female summer intern."

No adjective does the work here. The two dollar figures and the named store
are left to speak for themselves; Allen states what the documents show and
stops, trusting the numbers over a word like "lavish" to make the reader react.

> "Republican legislatures are on a course to virtually eliminate abortion access in large chunks of the Deep South and Midwest... Why it matters: A more conservative Supreme Court may approve."

After five bullets of state-by-state detail (which law, which state, what
stage each is at), the significance line is six words and names the actual
mechanism to watch — a possible Supreme Court ruling — rather than asserting
in general terms that the story is important.

> ""Many voters are willing to forgive the noise (political incorrectness, tweets, Mueller) as long as the signal (economy) stays strong," says lobbyist Bruce Mehlman, who explores this trend in his quarterly slide deck."

The item's closing argument belongs to a named, identified source, not to the
newsletter's own voice. Allen's contribution is finding the quote and knowing
where to put it — last, as the item's payoff — not paraphrasing a verdict into
a sentence of his own.

## Mike Dolan, Reuters, "Morning Bid: Chip rout snowballs"

Source: https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/morning-bid-chip-rout-snowballs-103825154.html

> "The chip stock correction snowballed again overnight amid growing concerns about rising competition and circular financing."

The opening sentence names the specific mechanism a market reader needs —
"rising competition and circular financing" — rather than a mood word like
"worries" or "fears." The cause is as concrete as the effect.

> "One was a sharp drop in shares of U.S. chip giant Nvidia - partly on reports of another round of circular financing with OpenAI. Another possible trigger was the blowout IPO of China's CXMT on Monday - a first-day jump of nearly 470% that unveiled a new chip giant ready to soak up investment funds."

Two competing causes are set side by side, each with its own named actor and
its own number, instead of Dolan picking one explanation and asserting it as
the reason. The reader is handed two facts to weigh rather than a verdict.

> "The scale of the debt being incurred is now starting to unnerve stock and credit markets alike, with borrowing from Big Tech firms this year now almost twice what it was last year. Higher capex from here will likely mean even higher debt loads."

The judgment — that the debt is starting to unnerve markets — is immediately
anchored to a comparison the reader can hold ("almost twice what it was last
year") instead of resting on the verb alone. The next sentence draws the
consequence and stops there.
