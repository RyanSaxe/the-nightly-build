# Voice guide — current-events/2026-08-01 (writing-coach/01)

## Directive

Register: wire-service, addressed to a reader who already has the headline.
Every sentence has to earn its place by adding what the headline could not
carry: a figure, the caveat that qualifies it, or the mechanism that produced
it. A sentence that restates the headline in other words has failed no matter
how well it is turned.

Headline: name the actor and the finding in the first four words, present
tense, with a verb that reports the event from the side that felt it rather
than the side that filed it — an agency approves a rule; the people it binds
feel the approval, and that is usually the sharper verb. Cut every colon. If a
second clause is reaching for one, it is either the real headline (fold it in
and drop the first half) or belongs in the dek, not stapled on with a colon. A
number opens a headline only when the number is the surprise the item exists
to report, not decoration on a claim that would stand without it.

Judgment: the item's one to three sentences carry a claim about consequence in
the same clause that states the fact, not a description followed by a hedge
tacked on after. Name what changed and for whom, using the sourced detail as
the reason inside that sentence — don't defer the "why it matters" to a
second sentence a reader could skip without loss. The last sentence closes on
the fact that makes the consequence concrete. It never opens a question back
to the reader ("what this means going forward," "time will tell," "for now").
If the honest judgment is that the outcome is unresolved, say what specifically
is unresolved and by when it resolves — that is a fact, not a shrug.

Shape: let the number of moving parts in a story set the item's shape, not a
template. A single ruling can close in two sentences. A dispute between two
parties needs a third sentence to hold the second position, not a subordinate
clause squeezing it into the first two. Before filing, read the four to six
items as one unit: if two in a row share sentence count, clause order, or
opening-word class, restructure one so the brief doesn't read stamped, even if
each item alone is sound.

Cut at this length: transitional throat-clearing ("Meanwhile," "In related
news"); an adjective standing in for a number that is available ("sweeping,"
"significant," "major"); any clause that hands interpretation back to the
reader; a close engineered to sound quotable. A caveat is a fact with a
source, never a hedge word.

Recently used, do not reuse: colon-subtitle headlines; an item that closes by
handing the point back to the reader; a forced through-line dek that claims
one theme ties unrelated items together — name the night's real center of
gravity if there is one, otherwise keep the dek plain; repeating the same item
order or headline shape from one brief to the next.

## The Associated Press, "US stocks rise to finish a wild July as Amazon soars, Apple sinks and inflation worries worsen"
Source: https://cdapress.com/news/2026/jul/31/us-stocks-swing-as-amazon-leaps-apple-sinks-and-rising-oil-prices-add-to-worries-about-inflation/
Craft:
- cadence: one dense lede sentence stacks three simultaneous movements on
  parallel verbs joined by "as," then sentence length drops hard into short,
  numbered declaratives.
- argument: no argument is asserted; the ranking of facts by size and the
  order they arrive in does the judging.
- evidence: every claim is a specific instrument-level number attached
  directly to the noun it modifies, never parked in a separate sentence.
- stance: zero evaluative adjectives; the verb choice carries all the
  judgment ("leaped," "sank," "careening").
- notice: watches for reversal inside the day — a rally that "briefly" lost
  its gains, a move that happened "despite" the expected headwind.
- diction: Anglo-Saxon verbs of motion do the work that adjectives would do
  in weaker copy.
- reader: assumes someone checking the tape, not seeking reassurance, so the
  number leads and the color follows.
- the missed move: causation gets exactly one subordinate clause and never
  its own sentence — "which executives pinned on a supply crunch in
  components getting vacuumed up in the AI boom" — so the mechanism is
  attributed to a named source inside the same breath as the fact, never
  asserted as the wire's own claim.
Calibration: "U.S. stocks rose Friday to finish a wild July for Wall Street
as Amazon leaped, Apple sank and rising oil prices worsened worries about
inflation staying high."

## NPR Up First, "Up First briefing: U.S. war on Iran; Congress; U.K.'s Andy Burnham"
Source: https://www.npr.org/2026/07/20/g-s1-134553/up-first-newsletter-iran-us-service-members-hunter-biden
Craft:
- cadence: a bolded topic sentence functions as the item's own headline,
  followed by one or two plain sentences of consequence; length flexes with
  the story's weight rather than a fixed count.
- argument: states the news, then names the institutional stakes — what a
  body must do and by when — sequenced by consequence, not by chronology.
- evidence: uses an actor's own stated agenda as evidence of stakes ("aiming
  to secure... advance... extend") rather than defaulting to a raw count.
- stance: understated; the verb carries the judgment ("carried out,"
  "aiming to") with no adjective grading it.
- notice: tracks institutional mechanics — a deadline a legislature is
  racing, a casualty count as an escalation marker — over color or reaction.
- diction: plain, short words; a title is used once, then the surname
  carries the rest of the item.
- reader: written for someone who has thirty seconds and nothing else, so
  the bold sentence alone has to carry the full development.
- the missed move: the bold/plain sentence pairing is a built-in hierarchy
  inside a single paragraph — a headline-weight sentence and a body-weight
  sentence share one block, which is how the item stands alone without a
  separate headline doing the summarizing.
Calibration: "The U.S. military carried out a wave of airstrikes in Iran
over the weekend after announcing that a third U.S. service member had been
killed in military operations since Friday."

## Semafor Flagship, "Semafor Flagship: The bitter lesson"
Source: https://www.semafor.com/newsletter/07/30/2026/semafor-flagship-the-bitter-lesson
Craft:
- cadence: three beats per item — the fact, a figure or phrase pulled from a
  primary source mid-paragraph, then the analytical consequence as the
  closing clause.
- argument: names the "so what" as its own clause instead of implying it,
  and lets that clause come last, earned by the evidence stated before it.
- evidence: one exact figure and one exact sourced phrase per item, never a
  stacked list of numbers; the phrase does double duty as citation and as
  the item's texture.
- stance: more interpretive than a pure wire — willing to name a pattern
  across separate actors — but the interpretation is reasoned from the
  stated evidence, not asserted ahead of it.
- notice: catches when several actors' separate moves are the same
  underlying bet made in different forms, a structural read rather than a
  single-actor report.
- diction: reaches for one compressed term already native to the field's own
  vocabulary per item, instead of inventing a phrase to decorate it.
- reader: assumes a subject-fluent, globally literate reader and skips
  defining terms of art that reader already owns.
- the missed move: the causal clause is withheld until after the numbers
  land, so the item ends on interpretation the preceding facts have already
  earned, rather than opening on a thesis and spending the rest of the item
  proving it.
Calibration: "$8 billion hit to free cash flow" / "often the same bet
expressed in multiple ways."
