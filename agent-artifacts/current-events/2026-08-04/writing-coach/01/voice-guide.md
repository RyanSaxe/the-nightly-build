# Voice guide: current-events/2026-08-04 (brief)

Register: wire, not column. Every item is a report, not an essay in miniature.
The reader relationship is a colleague checking what moved before a meeting,
not a student being taught a field. Write as if the fact and its stake were
reported in the same breath by the same source, because they were: do not
let the "why it matters" arrive as a second, separate move tacked on after
the news. Fuse them in the sentence that states the finding, since the
template forbids a labeled subhead to do that fusing for you.

Two sentence-architecture habits carry the rest of this guide and should
change actual sentences in the draft:

1. **Fact leads, source trails.** Put the reported action in the subject-verb
   spine of the sentence. Attribution ("according to," "the agency said")
   goes at the end, not the front, unless the source's identity is itself
   the news.
2. **Name the actor by what makes them newsworthy.** A public official gets
   a name. A role player who matters because of function or jurisdiction
   gets identified by that function, in the clause that explains why the
   development involves them at all — not as a bare title dropped in for
   formality.

## Licenses

```text
form: consequence fused into the lead sentence, no labeled subhead
move: the exemplars' "why it matters" habit, done without their typographic
      label — state the finding and, in the same sentence or the one directly
      after, name concretely who is affected and how, using a clause the
      reported fact actually supports
bar:  the stake must be traceable to a clause already in the item; if the
      consequence needs a new fact to justify it, source that fact too,
      don't assert the stake bare
```

```text
form: role-based actor identification
move: identifying an actor by function or jurisdiction (a federal judge, the
      agency's inspector general, the state's utility regulator) instead of
      title alone, when that function is the reason the development is
      newsworthy
bar:  use only when the role itself carries the consequence; a recognizable
      public figure is named, never described around
```

```text
form: inline exact quotation, unblocked
move: lifting a short exact phrase from a primary document, order, or
      official statement in place of a paraphrase that would blur or
      overstate the specific claim
bar:  the quote must carry a claim paraphrase would lose or distort; it is
      load-bearing evidence, not color or texture, and it stays short enough
      to read as part of the sentence, not a pull-quote
```

```text
form: trailing attribution
move: placing the source of a claim at the end of the sentence so the fact
      reads first, in the shape "X happened, [consequence], according to Y"
bar:  use when the event outranks the source in newsworthiness; flip the
      order only when the source's identity or authority is itself part of
      what the item is reporting
```

Recently used, do not reuse: colon subtitles; the comma-triad dek; the
semicolon-reversal dek ("X did A; Y refuses B"); the suspended-question dek;
scaffolding subheads inside an item (Background, Implications, Key
Takeaways); a closing line that hands the point back to the reader instead
of finishing the item's own consequence.

## Associated Press, wire lede (craft reconstruction)
Source: https://ryanbeckwith.medium.com/deconstructing-an-associated-press-lede-7adab722c351
Craft:
- cadence: one sentence, 25-40 words, subject and strong verb arrive inside
  the first seven words; a subordinate clause trails as a "caboose" carrying
  circumstance, and attribution closes the sentence last of all.
- argument: none by design — the lede reports, it does not interpret; any
  claim beyond the observed fact is pushed into attribution.
- evidence: attribution is a fixed closing formula ("authorities said"), never
  fronted, so the reported action is never diluted by a hedge before the
  reader reaches it.
- stance: flat. No adjective is doing evaluative work; the verb alone carries
  the charge of the sentence.
- notice: when the actor's name would not mean anything to the reader yet,
  AP substitutes the circumstantial detail that does mean something — "a
  judge walking from his car into the county courthouse" instead of a name
  the reader can't place.
- diction: plain, declarative verbs. No qualifier stacked in front of the
  subject.
- reader: assumes zero prior context; the lede has to work as the reader's
  first exposure to the event.
- the move the axes miss: sentence order is the argument. Putting the verb
  before the qualifying clause means the news lands before any reason to
  doubt or contextualize it arrives — the architecture itself is the
  non-sensational instinct, not a tone choice layered on top.

## Semafor, "Flagship: The bitter lesson"
Source: https://www.semafor.com/newsletter/07/30/2026/semafor-flagship-the-bitter-lesson
Craft:
- cadence: a short, plain headline distinct from the item's own prose, then
  a compressed narrative that moves from event to its immediate financial or
  political knock-on inside two or three sentences.
- argument: each item ends by naming the general pattern the specific event
  is an instance of (a shutdown becomes evidence for AI's "bitter lesson"),
  earned by a second data point in the same item, not asserted cold.
- evidence: named institutions and their own modeling or numbers embedded
  mid-sentence (HSBC's modeling, a hedge fund's specific losses) rather than
  cited afterward.
- stance: allows a labeled interpretive frame when the item has supplied two
  facts that triangulate it, not one.
- notice: catches the second-order consequence sitting one layer past the
  headline fact — a nuclear deal's momentum becomes a fighter-jet sale becomes
  a congressional fight over regional military balance, all inside one item.
- diction: a precise phrase lifted in quotation marks when paraphrase would
  soften the claim ("often the same bet expressed in multiple ways").
- reader: assumes global-affairs and markets literacy; no term is defined,
  no acronym expanded on first use.
- the move the axes miss: each item is written so the consequence clause
  could be deleted and the fact would still stand alone as news — but with it
  present, the fact reads as already-interpreted rather than raw.

## Axios, "U.S. wildfire season exceeds 10-year average as 99 large fires burn"
Source: https://www.axios.com/2026/08/03/us-wildfire-season-large-fires-10-year-average
Craft:
- cadence: opening sentence is pure observation (evacuations, outages, acres
  burned); the very next unit states the comparison that makes the number
  mean something, without narrative connective tissue between them.
- argument: keeps report and interpretation typographically separate (a
  labeled "Why it matters" unit) — a move this brief cannot copy directly
  since the template bans subheads inside an item, but the discipline behind
  it (say the stake in its own sentence, not folded into color) transfers.
- evidence: named source institution for the aggregate figure (National
  Interagency Fire Center) sits beside the number it produced, not in a
  trailing citation.
- stance: the interpretive line stays inside what the cited numbers support;
  it does not reach past the National Interagency Fire Center's own framing.
- notice: catches the baseline that turns a single season into a trend — a
  bare fire count means little; "exceeds the 10-year average" is the fact
  that makes it news.
- diction: exact counts, not rounded color (45,835 fires, 5.2 million acres),
  and a direct quote from the governor's emergency declaration where
  paraphrase would have flattened the specific conditions cited.
- reader: general public; every figure is given its comparison in the same
  breath it's reported, so no figure sits alone asking to be judged without
  context.
- the move the axes miss: state-level detail (Washington's 17 fires,
  Oregon's 32 and its acreage record) is used to make the national aggregate
  concrete, rather than the aggregate being left to stand for the whole
  country unillustrated.

## Self-test
A writer following only the house default already avoids fluff, cites every
claim, and gives numbers their comparisons — none of that needed restating
here. What this guide adds: it tells the writer where in the sentence the
consequence goes (fused into or immediately following the fact clause, never
a separate scaffolded beat), where attribution goes (trailing, so the fact
leads), when to identify an actor by role instead of name, and when a lifted
exact phrase earns its place over a paraphrase. Those are sentence-level
instructions a writer could not derive from the default alone, and they are
exactly the instructions this brief's "state the finding, say why it
matters, no scaffolding" requirement needs to become actual sentences.
