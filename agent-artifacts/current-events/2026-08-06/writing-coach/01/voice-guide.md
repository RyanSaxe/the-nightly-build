# Voice guide: the itemized news brief

## Directive

The house voice is calm, precise, argued from first principles, and it teaches
by building a concept before the sentence that needs it. That calibration is
written for an essay that has a whole piece to build in. This is not that. The
unit here is the item, roughly a paragraph, and it has no room to build toward
its point. Shrink the discipline to fit: land the judgment inside the reported
fact rather than reasoning up to it.

Write for a reader who already holds the headline and wants three things in
order: the concrete change, the mechanism that carries it, and your read of the
consequence. Deliver all three and stop. The finished item leaves the reader
complete, needing no longer article to get the point.

The one move that decides these sentences is reach. Press the consequence
exactly as far as this item's primary record and its independent account
jointly support, and state it flat. Short of that reach, the item is wire copy
that repeats the headline. Past it, the item is a hot take wearing attribution.
This brief lives on the line between, and the two-source floor is where the line
sits.

## Licenses

form: the compressed verdict — one sentence of consequence stated plainly, without the intervening reasoning a longer piece would show its steps for
move: the strongest daily briefs end an item on what to make of it, the judgment in a bare declarative, structure and not adjectives doing the persuading. They commit to one real read of the development and then stop, rather than gesturing at several.
bar: the consequence must follow from the item's own cited primary and independent sources in a single inference the reader can reconstruct, and a reader who accepts every fact could still hold the opposite. If reaching the verdict needs a fact the item does not cite, cut back to what the two sources hold. If the sentence could sit unchanged under a different item, it is filler, not a verdict.

form: the load-bearing caveat — a single concessive clause that changes what the finding means
move: a strong brief supplies the fact the headline dropped (the base rate, the countervailing figure, the condition the number depends on) and lets it revise the reader's conclusion, instead of staging a contrast for rhythm.
bar: the caveat must name a real countervailing fact carrying its own source, and it must change how the item reads. A concessive that only softens tone, or that corrects a misconception the sentence itself invented, is the hedged-contrast reflex and comes out.

## The Economist, "The World in Brief" (Espresso gobbets)
Source: https://www.storybench.org/economists-espresso-app-goes-beyond-headlines-provide-daily-shot-news-analysis/
Craft:
- cadence: actor and load-bearing verb arrive in the same breath, the substance and its counter folded into one sentence with no run-up. A specimen line runs "Donald Trump called Iran 'unbelievably duplicitous' for denying that it is negotiating with America" — subject, verb, the charge, and the thing being denied, all before any framing.
- argument: one facet is never enough; the editors' stated aim is to contextualize as deeply as the word count allows, so the item carries the development and its meaning together.
- evidence: the concrete particular (the figure, the exact charge, the named party) does the work; there is no room for a second example, so the one chosen must be load-bearing.
- stance: committed but unshowy. The item takes a position on what the development means and states it as a plain fact of consequence.
- notice: what deserves attention is placed first, not announced. The surprise occupies the opening words.
- diction: specific nouns and verbs, no hedging softeners, no throat-clearing openers.
- reader: assumes the headline is already known; every word spent re-approaching it is a word wasted.
- the move the axes miss — finishability: the item is engineered so the reader closes it feeling done, not sent onward. The ceiling of roughly 75 words is a completeness constraint, not just a length cap: everything the point needs is inside, and nothing that needs more space is attempted.

## Semafor, the "Semaform" (The News / The Reporter's View)
Source: https://www.cjr.org/the_media_today/semafor_launch_review.php
Craft:
- cadence: the format deliberately breaks the reported fact and the analytical read into separate registers, so the reader can feel where reporting ends and judgment begins.
- argument: the analytical line is meant to add a claim the reporting alone does not make, an actual view held by someone who knows the beat.
- evidence: reporting carries sourced fact; the analytical line carries the writer's inference from it, and the two are not supposed to trade places.
- stance: the reporter's read is licensed to be a real position, not a neutral restatement.
- notice: the value is in what the analyst sees that the wire summary would miss.
- diction: the reporting register stays plain; the analytical register is where a point of view is allowed to show.
- reader: treated as an adult who knows reporters have views and would rather have them labeled than smuggled.
- the move the axes miss — the failure mode to avoid: the CJR review found the analytical section often read as "a continuation of the reporting," not a point of view at all, and where fact and analysis blur the separation adds confusion rather than clarity. The lesson for our compressed verdict: if the consequence sentence merely re-describes the event, it is not a verdict and should be cut. Keep the read distinguishable from the report.

## Axios, "Smart Brevity" (the "Why It Matters" stake)
Source: https://tedium.co/2022/07/13/axios-smart-brevity-alt-story-form-critique/
Craft:
- cadence: the stake is isolated as its own beat so it cannot be skimmed past. The structure is the point: news, then consequence.
- argument: naming the consequence explicitly is the whole value of the form; the reader is told why the item earns their minute.
- evidence: at its best the stake organizes the facts that follow it; the critics' complaint is that the structure often "falls apart halfway in," the consequence line no longer governing anything.
- stance: the form insists on a "so what," which is the right instinct.
- notice: the consequence is elevated to a first-class element, not buried.
- diction: punchy and declarative, which is a strength until it becomes a house tic.
- reader: assumed to be skimming and to want the payoff surfaced.
- the move the axes miss — the boundary this brief must not cross: reviewers charge that the labeled stake "steamrolls nuance in favor of sounding smart at a cocktail party," and that "so what" lines added to fill a quota feel imposed rather than earned. So take the instinct (state the consequence) and reject the execution (a bolted-on label, a stake per item whether or not one exists). Our stake carries no label, uses the item's own nouns, and appears only when the sources support one.
