# Editorial review: current-events/2026-08-28 (editor/01)

## Skeptic

A brief carries no single thesis; the load-bearing judgment is the selection —
that these four are the day's most consequential US developments — plus the one
claim each item stands on. I took the four items as four claims and tested each
against the cited sources, opening every href.

- **Item 1 (China-hacking seizure).** Claim: DOJ/FBI seized QScan and QTRouter on
  August 26, traced to Nanjing Xinjiuwei Network Technology Company, whose named
  customers are the Ministry of State Security and the People's Liberation Army,
  with named federal targets and activity since at least 2018. The DOJ primary
  (s1) returns HTTP 403 to automated fetch but is the owning page a reader
  reaches in a browser; the proof treats the 403 as non-blocking and the
  researcher documented it as gated, not dead. Reuters (s2) opened and confirms
  every figure: date, platforms, the Nanjing firm, both customers, the federal
  target list, the four unnamed US and South Korean companies, and "since at
  least 2018." Display text (this item's h3 is also the h1 and nb-meta title, and
  the dek) checks out descriptor by descriptor: targets, customers, and the 2018
  dating are all as sourced. Held.

- **Item 2 (BEA PCE + UMich sentiment).** Claim: PCE +0.2% m/m and +3.7% y/y,
  core +0.2% and +3.3%, core holding 3.3–3.4% since April; sentiment 51.0 from
  55.2, below the 54.5 forecast, with Hsu tying part of the drop to the Iran
  conflict. BEA (s3) confirms the four PCE figures exactly. The Reuters/Quartz
  secondary (s4) confirms 51.0/55.2/54.5 and the Hsu quote about Republican
  sentiment 19% below pre-conflict levels and lowest since the 2024 election. The
  round-focus contested point is handled correctly: the item reports the BEA PCE
  headline and explicitly separates it from the CPI "quoted more widely," so the
  Fed-preview "3.4%" never stands in for the headline. The one "3.4%" in the body
  is the top of the April–July core range, sourced to BEA, not the preview
  figure. Held.

- **Item 3 (Hormuz).** Claim: CENTCOM's Adm. Cooper said on August 27 that US
  forces cleared Iranian mines from the strait's recognized lanes; the claim is
  contested. The DVIDS primary (s5) opened and confirms the video, commander, and
  date; gCaptain (s6) opened and confirms Cooper's quote, the methods, and the
  ~1,500-transit / ~750M-barrel figures; Al Jazeera (s7) opened and confirms
  Iran's four conditions and the Rezaei quote (verbatim in the note). One break:
  the body prints the JMIC residual risk as "a risk outside the cleared corridor"
  (s6), but s6, read twice, places the residual mine risk "in and near the TSS" —
  the cleared lanes — and says authorities are "not yet treating the broader
  waterway as mine-free." The evidence record (both rounds) paraphrases it the
  article's way ("outside cleared lanes"), so the record and the source I opened
  disagree on a load-bearing detail of a contested-claim item. The article's
  version understates the dispute (it concedes the lanes are clean when the source
  says mines still threaten them). Because this is a record/source conflict on a
  contested claim and I read the source only through a summarizing fetch, I route
  it for confirmation rather than settle it (see Required work). Two display-text
  labels I could fix from the sources at hand, I fixed: the h3 said Iran and
  monitors "reject" the claim, which overstates JMIC (it qualifies with a SEVERE
  rating; it does not deny the clearing) and Iran (it disputes the strait's open
  status, sets conditions) — changed to "dispute," the position the two
  secondaries actually support and the writer's own word for it. The body said
  Iran will "reopen the lanes"; Iran's conditions and the quoted Rezaei line are
  about the strait, so I set it to "reopen the strait." Held after fixes, with the
  JMIC location routed.

- **Item 4 (ballroom stay).** Claim: Roberts alone stayed on August 21 the
  preliminary injunction Judge Leon entered April 16, letting construction
  continue pending the full court. The SCOTUS docket primary (s8) opened and
  confirms the parties, the August 21 order text, and the April 16 injunction
  date. SCOTUSblog (s9) opened and confirms the plaintiff and December filing, the
  90,000 sq ft, 65% complete, the "250-person crew working 20 hours a day," and
  Judge Leon. The round-focus date correction holds: April 16 appears in both
  places the item names it, and no "March" survives (s9 itself carries no
  contradicting date). This item is a week stale for a Friday brief; the writer
  and researcher both flagged it as the most disposable, kept to reach the
  four-item floor, and it is honestly pegged to ongoing construction under a
  temporary, non-merits stay. Within editorial judgment; held.

Round focus overall: no item sources Warsh's undelivered speech (absent
entirely); contested claims are attributed (BEA PCE, and the Hormuz clearing as a
disputed US claim); the mail-in-voting order and the August 27 USPS TRO do not
appear (unbiased owns them). All five confirmations pass except the JMIC-location
detail routed above.

## Cut

The prose is lean; the slop pass found little to remove. I ran the placeholder
test on every edge sentence — the first and last of each paragraph, each item,
and the article — and read the edges out of order. The topic sentence of item 1's
second paragraph ("The list of named targets is almost entirely federal")
survives: it carries the item's synthesis (the target set is the federal
government itself) and supplies the antecedent the enumeration that follows leans
on. Item 3's "The claim describes the lanes, not the strait" is negative
parallelism, but the earned kind: it corrects a real over-reading the headline's
"Strait of Hormuz" invites, and the lanes-versus-strait distinction is the crux
of the item. It stays.

One delete-test cut: item 4 closed on "The order only lets the work continue. The
justices have not yet decided whether building over Judge Leon's April 16
injunction was lawful." The first sentence is a signpost restating the stay's
scope, which the first paragraph ("while the full court weighs") and the
following sentence already carry. Removing it loses no fact and lands the item on
the substantive close — construction races ahead while lawfulness is undecided.

No prompt leakage: the article states no selection rule, never narrates itself,
and does not claim it fulfilled its assignment (the original-work sentence's
framing lives in the handoff, not the prose). No borrowed phrasing from the
voice-guide exemplars — the technique is emulated, the wording is the article's
own. No comma-triad dek, no reuse of the "event + institutional consequence"
headline mold, no national-debt frame; the four headlines are built four
different ways. Punctuation is clean (the proof cleared em-dash and banned-term
counts at BLOCK: 0). Furniture earns its place: the stat strip carries three
heterogeneous headline numbers each cited in nearby prose, and the note promotes
Iran's own terms as a labeled quotation. Roughly two edge sentences drew scrutiny
under the slop test; one was cut, one held.

## Reader

Read straight through as the paper's declared reader, who followed the day's
headlines: what the article gives beyond the sources is the ability to tell an
owned figure from an asserted one. It pins inflation to the BEA PCE index and
names it as the Fed's gauge distinct from the more-quoted CPI, so the previews'
"3.4%" cannot pass for the headline; it sets CENTCOM's cleared-lanes claim beside
Iran's four conditions and JMIC's SEVERE rating, so "open" reads as an assertion,
not a settled fact. That answer matches the original-work sentence, and both
survive. The prose sits closer to the voice-guide exemplars than to a median
summary: the caveat arrives early, each term of art is defined where it lands,
and the items end on a dropped fact (the 2018 intrusions and unnamed victims; the
Republican-sentiment figure; the undecided merits) rather than a summarizing
line. The largest claim, the h1, is supported by the two national-security
sources.
