# Voice guide — current-events brief, 2026-07-31

Register: wire-desk economy, not feed voice. The reader already has the
headline; every sentence in the item must earn its place by adding something
the headline didn't say. Write to a reader who will read this once, fast, and
not come back.

Moves that change sentences in this article:

- Open each item with a finite verb close to the front: actor, action, what
  changed. No dependent clause before the verb ("In a move that...", "Amid
  growing pressure..."). The AP and Reuters ledes below put the news in the
  main clause of sentence one and nowhere else.
- Put the dropped number or caveat in the sentence right after the lede, tied
  to a comparison the reader can size against something known (a prior
  figure, a forecast, a date). A bare number reads like data; a number next
  to what it beat or missed reads like news.
- Fold attribution into the sentence that carries the claim rather than
  appending it: "the committee said" or "Reuters found" sits inside the
  clause making the point, not tacked on after a period. This is what lets an
  item carry both a primary and a secondary source without turning into a
  citation list.
- Vary how items open. Not every item should be actor-first. Rotate between
  actor-first, number-first, and consequence-first openings across the 4-6
  items so the brief doesn't scan as one template run six times.
- End an item on its last concrete fact or its direct, sourced consequence,
  never on a reflective line that turns the point back to the reader ("what
  happens next remains to be seen"). The Economist item below ends on the
  cause of the move, not a gesture at its meaning.
- Do not build a "why it matters" clause, a "the bottom line" tag, or any
  named scaffolding phrase. If the consequence is real, it is already in the
  lede or the second sentence, in plain prose, not flagged with a label.
- Skip the hedged-contrast dek ("not X, but Y") unless a specific, named
  misconception is actually being corrected. Most of the time it is a
  strawman built for cadence.

Recently used, do not reuse: this desk's recent headlines are single
declarative sentences naming the actor and the action ("Rogoff sues Trump
over the 54-minute firing"). Keep that specificity in headlines and item
openings, but do not let every item's opening sentence fall into the identical
actor-verb-object shape back to back — that reads stamped even when each
sentence is individually sharp. No "Top stories" or other list-scaffolding
header. No manufactured-punchline closer engineered to sound quotable.

## Associated Press, Blanche AG nomination story
Source: https://spectrumlocalnews.com/us/snplus/politics/2026/07/30/senate-panel-delays-todd-blanche-attorney-general-nomination-vote
Craft:
- cadence: one main clause carries the news; modifiers trail it instead of
  stacking in front of the verb.
- argument: the fact of the threatened withdrawal is the whole item; the
  piece does not editorialize about what it means for the nomination's odds.
- evidence: the audit-immunity deal is named specifically, not gestured at as
  "a controversial arrangement."
- stance: flat third person, no adjectives of judgment on either side.
- notice: the caveat (why Trump might pull the nomination) rides inside the
  same sentence as the news, as a purpose clause, not a second paragraph.
- diction: "may pull," "conceding," "demands" — plain, active verbs over
  abstractions like "tension" or "controversy."
- reader: assumes the reader knows who Blanche is and why he was nominated;
  does not reintroduce him before the news.
- the move the axes miss: the dateline-style opener (WASHINGTON —) is doing
  scene-setting work in two characters, freeing the sentence that follows to
  start directly on the verb.
Calibration: "WASHINGTON — President Donald Trump said Thursday he may pull
acting Attorney General Todd Blanche's nomination for the full-time post to
avoid conceding to Senate Republicans' demands over a sweeping tax audit
immunity deal benefiting the president and members of his family."

## Reuters, jobless-claims wire brief
Source: https://www.business-standard.com/article/reuters/u-s-jobless-claims-drop-to-5-1-2-year-low-113080101085_1.html
Craft:
- cadence: two-sentence unit. Sentence one states the finding as news;
  sentence two supplies the exact figure and its record-setting context.
- argument: the direction of the number ("fell unexpectedly") is the story;
  the raw count is evidence for that direction, not the lead itself.
- evidence: the comparison point (lowest since January 2008) does the work a
  reader's own memory can't — it converts a number into a scale.
- stance: no adjectives beyond what the data supports ("steadily improving,"
  sourced to the trend, not to opinion).
- notice: the source of the number (the Labor Department) is named in the
  same sentence as the number, closing the loop on where it came from.
- diction: "touching," "dropped," "the lowest level since" — motion verbs
  and dated comparisons instead of "significant" or "notable."
- reader: assumes the reader knows what jobless claims measure; spends no
  words defining the indicator.
- the move the axes miss: the second sentence is built entirely out of
  numbers and one attribution, with no connective tissue wasted on framing —
  it just states the data and stops.
Calibration: "The number of Americans filing new claims for unemployment
benefits fell unexpectedly last week, touching a 5-1/2 year low, suggesting a
steadily improving labor market. Initial claims for state unemployment
benefits dropped 19,000 to a seasonally adjusted 326,000, the lowest level
since January 2008, the Labor Department said on Thursday."

## The Economist, "The World in Brief"
Source: https://www.economist.com/the-world-in-brief
Craft:
- cadence: one sentence per item, built as a single main clause with a
  trailing cause or comparison clause, not two sentences.
- argument: the item picks one number and one cause and commits to both; it
  does not survey several possible causes.
- evidence: the index is defined in three words of apposition ("a tech-heavy
  index of American stocks") so a reader without market background isn't
  lost, without spending a separate sentence on the definition.
- stance: dry, compressed, no verdict on whether the move is good or bad.
- notice: the caveat (what specifically hurt semiconductor-makers) is folded
  into the same clause as the headline number, so the item doesn't need a
  second sentence to be complete.
- diction: "fell by around 1%" — a rounded, honest figure rather than false
  precision, paired with the two named causes.
- reader: assumes global financial literacy but not US-specific index
  knowledge, hence the one-clause definition.
- the move the axes miss: compression comes from cutting causes down to the
  two that matter, not from cutting words out of one cause — the sentence is
  short because the reporting was narrow, not because the prose was trimmed
  after the fact.
Calibration: "The NASDAQ 100, a tech-heavy index of American stocks, fell by
around 1%, as concerns over AI-related spending and Chinese competition hurt
semiconductor-makers."

## Axios AM, "Thune's next nightmare" (caution, not model)
Source: https://www.axios.com/2026/07/29/thunes-majority-leader-trump-conference-maga
Craft:
- cadence: a labeled "Why it matters" paragraph interrupts the narrative to
  restate the stakes the preceding sentences already implied.
- argument: the analysis is correct but gets delivered twice — once inside
  the story, once again under the label — which is the tell to avoid, not a
  technique to borrow.
- evidence: the underlying reporting (Thune's standing in his conference) is
  solid; the scaffolding is what a brief item should skip.
- stance: the label itself performs authority ("here is why this matters")
  instead of the sentence earning that weight on its own.
- notice: a reader who deletes the "Why it matters" heading loses no
  information, only a signpost — proof the label is decoration.
- diction: conversational, second-reference asides ("still keep his job")
  that suit a newsletter voice but read loose next to a wire item.
- reader: assumes a reader who wants to be told which paragraph is the
  important one, rather than a reader trusted to find it in the prose.
- the move the axes miss: the label is a substitute for a strong second
  sentence. A brief item does the same job by making the second sentence
  itself carry the consequence, with no header required.
Calibration: "Thune is one of the few Republicans in Washington who's
managed to say 'no' to President Trump and still keep his job. But the
Senate leader could enter the next Congress with diminished power, leading a
conference whose loyalty increasingly lies with Trump."
