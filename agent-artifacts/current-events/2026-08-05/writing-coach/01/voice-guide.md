# Voice guide: current-events/2026-08-05

Register stays the house default: calm, precise, argued from first principles.
What changes for a brief item is compression. Nothing here is a build-up genre —
each item gets one, maybe two, paragraphs to land a finding and its stakes, so
the compression has to happen inside single sentences instead of across them.
The reader already has the headline; write for someone who wants the fact and
the consequence in the same breath, not a recap followed by an explanation.

Three moves actually change sentences here:

- **Fuse finding and consequence in the lead sentence's main clause.** Do not
  report the fact in sentence one and explain why it matters in sentence two.
  A brief item does not have a sentence to spare for that.
- **Keep attribution inside the sentence that carries the fact**, positioned so
  the fact opens the sentence and the source rides at the end or in a tight
  subordinate clause. Attribution parked in its own sentence reads as
  throat-clearing.
- **When a claim is a live, quoted assertion rather than a settled fact, quote
  the load-bearing word inside the reported sentence** instead of hedging with
  "reportedly" or "according to." Precision about whose words they are does
  the hedging's job without the hedge.

## Licenses

```text
form: the fused lead
move: AP and Reuters open an item on one sentence that names the actor, states
  the finding, and lands the stake in the same clause or an immediate
  participial phrase, rather than reporting the fact and then explaining it.
  Reuters: "The 7.1-magnitude quake that struck southern Japan this week has
  brought many car and semiconductor factories in the area to a halt, but
  hopes are high that disruptions will be less severe than a similar-sized
  temblor that rocked the region a decade ago." One sentence carries the
  event, the scale, and the comparison that tells the reader whether to worry.
bar: the opening sentence must do both jobs on its own — what happened and why
  it is this brief's business — or it has not earned the lead position. A
  second sentence that exists only to state the consequence the first sentence
  could have carried is a fail, not a second beat.

form: in-line attribution
move: AP and Reuters fold sourcing into the sentence that makes the claim,
  fact first: "killing at least 23 people and wounding over 70 others,
  officials said." When the source must stay anonymous, the reason rides in
  the same breath: "a person familiar with the status of negotiations who
  spoke on condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to comment
  publicly."
bar: the fact opens the sentence, not the source. A sentence that starts
  "Officials said..." or "According to a report..." before the news itself
  has the order backward and should be rebuilt.

form: the quoted load-bearing word
move: The Economist's brief coverage reports a live or contested claim by
  quoting the specific word inside an otherwise plain reported sentence — "a
  draft agreement... had been 'circulated'" — rather than writing "reportedly"
  or wrapping the whole claim in quotation marks. The quotation marks sit only
  on the word doing the claiming.
bar: use this only when the article is reporting someone's own characterization
  of an unsettled situation and the exact word matters (a party's claim about
  a deal's status, a stated justification). It is not a device for adding
  color to a settled fact — a confirmed number or an established outcome gets
  no quotation marks at all.
```

## Recently used, do not reuse

Per the commission: no colon subtitles, no comma-triad dek, no
semicolon-reversal dek, no suspended-question dek, and no scaffolding subheads
("Background," "What's next"). These are commission-level bans, not craft
extracted from this research, and they hold regardless of what any exemplar
below does.

## Associated Press, "AP News in Brief"
Source: https://ca.news.yahoo.com/ap-news-brief-12-09-041018689.html
Craft:
- cadence: each item opens with one dense sentence carrying actor, action, and
  source, then each following sentence adds exactly one new fact — nothing
  restates the lead.
- argument: the item makes no argument; the sequence of facts is the judgment.
  The most consequential status goes first, the complicating detail second.
- evidence: every non-obvious claim is attributed by name or role, and every
  anonymous source's reason for anonymity is stated in the same sentence, not
  waved at.
- stance: studiously neutral — no adjective the sourcing doesn't earn.
- notice: exact counts, always with a unit conversion for a global reader
  ("6,000 and 7,000 gallons (22,700 and 26,500 liters)").
- diction: plain nouns and active, specific verbs (detonated, collapsed,
  opened fire) — never a generic verb standing in for an exact one.
- reader: assumes the reader wants the news fast; no scene-setting before the
  fact.
- the move the axes missed: an anonymous source's justification for anonymity
  is never separated from the sourcing itself — the two travel as one clause,
  which is what lets the sentence stay fast and still be checkable.

## Reuters, "Japan quake halts auto and chip plants, testing supply chain resilience"
Source: https://ca.news.yahoo.com/death-toll-japan-earthquake-rises-012750089.html
Craft:
- cadence: the lead sentence carries the event, its scale, and a historical
  comparison in one breath; later paragraphs shorten as they move
  company-by-company through the evidence.
- argument: builds a comparison (this quake's disruption against 2016's) not
  by asserting it but by walking the same set of companies through both
  events and letting the reader do the subtraction.
- evidence: named executives with exact titles, quoted for technical specifics
  ("earthquake resistance measures introduced at the site since 2016 had
  helped limit damage") rather than for sentiment or color.
- stance: reportorial throughout; the piece never states the takeaway, it
  assembles the companies' individual statuses until the takeaway is obvious.
- notice: every number gets the comparison that makes it legible — deaths
  against the 2016 toll, distance in km with miles alongside.
- diction: verbs are graded by severity and never flattened to one word —
  suspended, partially suspended, halting some production, idled, resuming —
  each company gets the verb that matches its actual status.
- reader: writes for someone tracking the business and supply-chain
  consequence, not a bystander wanting the human story.
- the move the axes missed: differing severities across companies are shown
  by choosing a different verb for each one, not by grading them explicitly
  ("worse," "less affected") — the verb does the comparative work the
  adjective would otherwise have to do.

## The Economist, "The World in Brief" / Morning Briefing
Source: https://shows.acast.com/theeconomistmorningbriefing
Craft:
- cadence: one packed sentence per item, often built as a causal clause —
  "Oil prices fell after Qatar said..." — so the cause sits inside the same
  sentence as the effect.
- argument: the causal frame itself is the analysis; there is no separate
  sentence of commentary.
- evidence: a claim still in dispute keeps its source's own word inside
  quotation marks mid-sentence ("had been 'circulated,'" "'staggering'
  second-quarter growth") so the reader knows exactly whose characterization
  it is.
- stance: dry and unemotional; no adjective beyond what a number or a quoted
  word supplies.
- notice: currency and percentage precision stated plainly (up 92% year on
  year, to around $8.15bn) with no rounding beyond what the source gave.
- diction: financial-register terms used without translation, on the
  assumption the reader already carries them, but every figure is complete on
  its own.
- reader: a globally literate reader who wants the causal chain, not
  background.
- the move the axes missed: quotation marks are reserved for the one word or
  phrase actually in dispute, so their presence itself signals "this is a
  claim, not yet a settled fact" without the sentence ever using a hedge word.

## Self-test
A writer following only the house default would still write clean, sourced,
declarative sentences — but nothing in the default forces a single sentence to
carry both a finding and its stake, and nothing stops attribution from
trailing in its own weak clause. This guide's one real addition: compress the
fact-and-consequence fusion down to the clause level, because a brief item
never gets a second sentence to recover ground the first one left flat.
