# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-27 (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a three-minute read for people who build machine learning systems and have read the front page of a newspaper that morning. It speaks the way a well-informed colleague would across a desk: plain declaratives, the specific noun, the date, the name, the figure. Nothing in the exemplars below reaches for importance. The NPR dog-show report states who won, how many dogs entered, and what happens next, and trusts the facts to be of interest. Items of two or three sentences leave no room for anything else.

Technology items can take the register of Simon Willison's post: say what the thing is, quote or name the exact mechanism in the practitioners' own terms, and let a short reaction show where the writer stands. A reaction is only worth a sentence where there is one. His "I'm thoroughly tickled by this one" is the whole of his opinion and it is enough. Where nothing in the material earns a reaction, the item can end on its last fact. News items can end the same way, and should not all end alike.

The Word of the Day can be written in the manner of the Merriam-Webster entries: a dated, checkable story told in order, with the uncertainty stated where the record has a gap. The shenanigans entry says what etymologists have guessed and what can only be said for certain, which is how a documented origin and an undocumented one both get told honestly. Where the word has a coiner or a first use, name it with its date and let the story carry the pleasure. A light touch is welcome, as in "fool us once", when the word's own meaning supplies the joke.

Hold the length to what each item needs. The sentence about who did it or claims it goes in plainly, with the attribution attached to the claim, and the sentence about what follows goes in only where something does follow.

## Willison, "Accidental prompt injection against RAG applications"

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Jun/6/accidental-prompt-injection/

> "RAG is commonly implemented using semantic search powered by embeddings—I wrote extensive about those last year (including this section on using them with RAG).
> This trick works really well, but comes with one key weakness: a regular keyword-based search can return 0 results, but because embeddings search orders by similarity score it will ALWAYS return results, really scraping the bottom of the barrel if it has to."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Jun/6/accidental-prompt-injection/ (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-27
He names the mechanism in the terms the reader already uses (embeddings, similarity score, keyword search) and states the weakness as a comparison between two systems that can be checked. The capitals on ALWAYS and the barrel phrase are one person talking.

> "Systems built on LLMs consistently produce the weirdest and most hilarious bugs. I'm thoroughly tickled by this one."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Jun/6/accidental-prompt-injection/ (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-27
The post closes on a short personal reaction after the explanation is complete. He says he is tickled and does not tell the reader what the episode means.

## Merriam-Webster, Word of the Day "bogart" (May 10, 2024)

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2024-05-10

> "Bogart also had a unique method of smoking cigarettes in these pictures—letting the butt dangle from his mouth without removing it until it was almost entirely consumed. It is believed that this habit inspired the current meaning of bogart, which was once limited to the phrase "Don't bogart that joint," as popularized by a song on the soundtrack to the film Easy Rider. Today, bogart can be applied to hogging almost anything."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2024-05-10 (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-27
The origin is told as a chain of dated, nameable things: an actor, a habit, a phrase, a song, a film. "It is believed" marks the one link that is inference, and the last sentence gives the word's present use in six plain words.

## Merriam-Webster, Word of the Day "shenanigans" (April 1, 2023)

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2023-04-01

> "Fool us once, shame on you; fool us twice, shame on us. Either way, we call it shenanigans, employing a word whose history is as tricky and mischievous as its meaning."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2023-04-01 (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-27
The opening joke is made out of the word's own sense, and it is over in two sentences.

> "Etymologists have some theories about its origins, but no one has been able to prove any of them. All we can say for certain is that the earliest known uses of the word in print appeared in the mid-1800s."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2023-04-01 (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-27
It says what is unproven and then gives the one dated fact. The writer admits the gap in a plain sentence and does not apologize for it.

Checked: https://text.npr.org/nx-s1-6054881 (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-27
