# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-06 (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a three-minute read for engineers and mathematicians who build machine learning systems. They read the news the way they read a changelog: they want the event, the party responsible, and the consequence, and they notice an unsupported adjective. Write for people who will repeat an item to a colleague an hour later, so each item has to survive being repeated. Keep to one fact per sentence and let the item's three sentences be the whole report.

The Associated Press story on the Paraguayan bank collapse shows the news register. The lede carries the actor, the verb, the day and the cause in one sentence, and later sentences hand each number to whoever owns it: "the central bank says", "reported ABC Color", "according to central bank exchange-rate data". Name the issuer, the agency or the court that holds the record and use a plain verb for what it did. Where the record is silent or a party disputes it, say so in the sentence that carries the claim. The reader can check a named source, and cannot check a mood.

Numbers come with their unit and the thing that scales them, as in the AP's $36,000 per customer covering an estimated 97% of clients. Terms the audience would not already hold get a short appositive where they first appear, as in "capital, the cushion banks must keep to absorb losses". Terms this audience holds (gradient descent, GPUs, signal-to-noise) get no gloss. John Timmer in Ars Technica builds a technical explanation out of ordinary sentences in the order a reader needs them, and Liz Hoffman at Semafor puts a dollar figure and a comparison where another writer would put an adjective. When the reporting supports a verdict, give it in the same flat tone as the facts. Hoffman's ranking of the CVS unwind against other deals is a position she then supports with specifics, and the sentence does not hedge.

Humor, if the material has any, belongs to the story's own nouns. Merriam-Webster's "wraith" entry calls the word's origin "misty" while reporting exactly what the etymologists have, and that is the model for the Word of the Day: say where the record stops and then stop. For the word card, the story is a documented sequence of who used the word, when, and in what text. Where the dictionary says the origin is uncertain, the uncertainty is part of the story. The card gives its part of speech, pronunciation and one-sentence definition as the series prompt requires, then a few sentences, and it does not praise the word.

At this length there is no room for a closing sentence that comments on the item. An item can end on the date something takes effect, the vote still to come, the figure still unknown, or the thing a reader could do. Where the material offers none of these, end on the last fact. Keep each item to its own subject, and leave out any sentence that would read the same under another item. The quoted passages below are in the guide for their sound only. Their subjects (Paraguay, CVS, a coronagraph pipeline, the words candidate and wraith) are not material for this edition.

## Merriam-Webster, "Word of the Day: candidate"

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/candidate-2026-11-03

> "When a man running for public office in ancient Rome greeted voters in the Forum, the center of judicial and public business, he wore a toga that had been whitened with chalk. As a result, the Latin word for someone seeking office came to be candidātus, meaning literally "clothed in white." Candidātus, in turn, comes from the adjective candidus, meaning "white." Candidātus was adopted into English as candidate, and since the 17th century that word has had an uncontested seat in the language."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/candidate-2026-11-03, retrieved 2026-11-06
The passage tells the origin as a short chain of events: a person in a place doing a dated, physical thing, then the Latin word and its root, then the English one. The Forum is glossed in a clause and the Latin gloss is given once. The writer is visible in the one dry sentence at the end, which says what the word has done since the 17th century without praising it.

> "A candidate is someone who aspires to or is nominated or qualified for an office, membership, or award. Candidate may also refer to someone likely or suited to undergo or be chosen for something specified (as in "a candidate for surgery"), or to a student in the process of meeting final requirements for a degree (as in "a PhD candidate")."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/candidate-2026-11-03, retrieved 2026-11-06
This is a definition that lists senses in order of how common they are and pins the rare ones with a short example in parentheses. It uses "or" to separate senses rather than a string of qualifiers.

## Merriam-Webster, "Word of the Day: wraith"

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/wraith-2026-10-31

> "If you see your own double, you're in trouble, at least if you believe old superstitions. The belief that a ghostly twin's appearance portends death is one common to many cultures. In German folklore, such an apparition is called a Doppelgänger (literally, "double goer"); in Scottish lore, it is a wraith. The exact origin of the word wraith is misty, however, and etymologists can only trace it back to the early 16th century—in particular to a 1513 translation of Virgil's Aeneid by Gavin Douglas (the Scotsman used wraith to name apparitions of both the dead and the living)."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/wraith-2026-10-31, retrieved 2026-11-06
The opening rhymes and the writer plainly enjoys it. The same paragraph then states how far the record goes: a century, a named translator and a named text. The uncertainty is reported as a fact about the sources, and the parenthesis adds what Douglas did with the word.

## The Associated Press, "Paraguay's president is impeached minutes after resigning following a bank collapse"

Source: https://www.npr.org/2026/11/05/nx-s1-6030842/paraguay-president-impeached-bank-collapse (AP story as carried by NPR, 5 November 2026; the apnews.com copy returned HTTP 403)

> "Paraguayan President Santiago Peña announced his resignation Thursday after the collapse of a major digital bank tied to his government triggered widespread protests, froze millions of people's savings and cost him the support of his own party.
>
> The crisis at Ueno Bank turned Peña's long-ruling Colorado Party against him, a stunning reversal for the former International Monetary Fund economist who had enjoyed the backing of the country's most powerful politicians and was welcomed in Washington just weeks ago as a key ally of the Trump administration."

Checked: https://www.npr.org/2026/11/05/nx-s1-6030842/paraguay-president-impeached-bank-collapse, retrieved 2026-11-06
The first sentence holds the actor, the act, the day, the cause and three consequences, and every clause is a thing that happened. The second paragraph supplies the reversal by contrast with a dated fact, the Washington welcome, so the reader gets the size of the change without being told it is big.

> "Once the books were corrected, Ueno's capital, the cushion banks must keep to absorb losses, fell to less than half the legal minimum. The central bank announced the lender's seizure Tuesday, beginning a process to transfer deposits and recoverable assets to other banks before liquidating what remained."

Checked: https://www.npr.org/2026/11/05/nx-s1-6030842/paraguay-president-impeached-bank-collapse, retrieved 2026-11-06
The technical term is defined inside the sentence that uses it, between commas, and the figure is given against the legal threshold that makes it mean something. The second sentence names the announcer and the day and lists the steps in the order they happen.

> "Under a law rushed through Congress on Wednesday, a state guarantee fund must repay stranded depositors within five business days, starting Thursday with small accounts of up to about $1,600 held by customers with no debts to Ueno. The fund insures up to about $36,000 per customer, which the central bank says will cover the full balances of an estimated 97% of clients."

Checked: https://www.npr.org/2026/11/05/nx-s1-6030842/paraguay-president-impeached-bank-collapse, retrieved 2026-11-06
The sentences give the deadline, the first tranche, the cap and the share of customers covered, and the 97% is attributed to the central bank as an estimate. The writer keeps the claim and its owner together and uses "about" where the source rounds.

## Liz Hoffman, "CVS joins the M&A unwind club" (Semafor)

Source: https://www.semafor.com/article/11/05/2026/cvs-joins-the-ma-unwind-club

> "CVS unwinding its 2018 purchase of Aetna is not quite the biggest, fastest corporate M&A about-face of all time, but it's close. The breakup, which essentially calls time on the $69 billion merger that aimed to create a healthcare giant across insurance and pharmacies, joins the top tier of corporate M&A whoopsies."

Checked: https://www.semafor.com/article/11/05/2026/cvs-joins-the-ma-unwind-club, retrieved 2026-11-06
The opening commits to a ranking in the first sentence and attaches the year and the $69 billion to the event. The reporter's own voice shows in "whoopsies", a small joke that sits inside a sentence that still reports the size of the deal and what it was meant to do.

> "It was a bigger mistake than GE's acquisition of Baker Hughes, which was less expensive and unwound faster. It was also less of a fiasco than both AOL's and AT&T's Time Warner deals. Taking into account the cost of the acquisition, and how long it took management to acknowledge its mistake, we'd put the CVS unwind on par with the mergers of Kraft and Heinz or Daimler and Chrysler."

Checked: https://www.semafor.com/article/11/05/2026/cvs-joins-the-ma-unwind-club, retrieved 2026-11-06
The judgment is made by comparison to named cases, and the two criteria used (cost and time to admit the mistake) are stated before the ranking. "We'd put" marks it as the outlet's own call and separates it from the reported facts.

## John Timmer, "Getting software to help block out a star so we can see exoplanets" (Ars Technica)

Source: https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/11/getting-software-to-help-block-out-a-star-so-we-can-see-exoplanets/

> "While telescopes can be fitted with devices called coronagraphs that block out much of a star's light, there are limits to their effectiveness, caused by noise from subtle variations in the telescope's configuration and pointing between observations."

Checked: https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/11/getting-software-to-help-block-out-a-star-so-we-can-see-exoplanets/, retrieved 2026-11-06
One sentence names the device, says what it does, and states the specific cause of its limit. The term is introduced by "devices called" at the point of use and the sentence moves on.

> "If you use a telescope to image a single point of light, it will not appear that way in the actual image. Instead, it will be slightly blurry and spread out, due to imperfections in the telescope hardware and the limits of our ability to keep it pointed accurately through the time it takes to image something. The mathematical description of how a telescope distorts a point of light is called its point spread function."

Checked: https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/11/getting-software-to-help-block-out-a-star-so-we-can-see-exoplanets/, retrieved 2026-11-06
The explanation starts from the thing the reader can picture and gives the two causes of the blur. It names the concept last, once the reader has the idea, and uses "we" and "you" only where it describes shared limits. Timmer assumes readers who know what a mathematical description is.
