# Voice guide: Daily Brief, 2026-09-30

## The voice

Write like a sharp editor briefing a well-read, technically literate person who has not followed these particular stories. Be brisk, exact, and lively through the reporting itself. Keep each card compact, but give it enough context to stand on its own. Prefer concrete actors, actions, dates, and quantities to commentary about importance.

The edition has six cards in this order: word of the day; three news items; two technology developments. Make the transitions between cards invisible. Do not add a general introduction or a wrap-up that competes with the six items.

## Build each card around its own job

- **Palimpsest:** State part of speech, pronunciation, and a precise present-day definition. Then connect the Greek roots to a documented historical example other than the familiar Archimedes story. Explain why the example illustrates the word's older physical sense. Keep etymology and historical detail sourced; avoid turning the card into a mini-essay.
- **Supreme Court order:** Lead with the September 29 order and its immediate legal effect. Establish the case and emergency posture for readers arriving cold. Distinguish permission to proceed during litigation from a final ruling on legality. Attribute government and advocacy-group counts to the groups that supplied them; do not present either as a court finding.
- **Consumer confidence:** Report the September index at 81.9 and the change from the prior reading. Identify the survey period and the relevant sub-index movement. Keep respondents' expectations distinct from observed spending, employment, or other economic measurements; do not turn a sentiment index into a forecast.
- **White House AI accord:** Name the six signatories and specify the commitments the public document actually contains. Describe its voluntary, non-binding status precisely. Attribute judgments about its force or adequacy to their speakers, and include material criticism without letting commentary blur the document's terms.
- **DeepSeek and Huawei:** Separate the September 30 announcement and its stated goal of Ascend programming tools from released code, tested capability, and deployed use. Name which organization made each claim. A roadmap or collaboration announcement is not evidence that the tools already work in production.
- **WSL containers:** Explain the September 29 general-availability change in terms a developer can use: what was preview-only before, what is now supported, platform or management integrations, and any stated limitations. Keep Microsoft's feature claims distinct from independent confirmation.

## Reporting and attribution

Use exact dates for publication and event dates, and label them separately when they differ. Put the originating record first for each consequential claim, then use independent reporting to test, contextualize, or challenge it. Each card needs at least one primary and one independent secondary source; the edition needs at least 12 distinct reliable sources overall. Close every card with its sources in the existing template furniture.

Link to the document, data, release, or order that owns the claim. Where practical, point into the relevant section or paragraph. Quote only words verified in the public primary document, with attribution and enough context to preserve their meaning. Never invent a quote or turn a source's characterization into fact. When sources disagree, say who reports what and what the record settles. If a point cannot be supported, qualify it plainly or remove it.

## Sentence and card craft

Open each news or technology card on the concrete change, ruling, or measurement; explain only the background needed to understand it; finish on the verified consequence or the important limit in the evidence. The word card may open on the word itself. Give each card one clear center of gravity instead of compressing every related detail into a list.

Use short, direct sentences and stable names for institutions, measures, and products. Define a specialized legal, economic, or developer term at first use. Use numbers at the precision the source supports, with a useful comparison when it helps scale them. Keep the prose readable aloud; periods usually beat semicolons, em dashes, and parenthetical asides.

Avoid stock scene-setting, generic importance claims, rhetorical questions, decorative metaphors, inflated verbs, repeated sentence openings, and tidy three-part rhythms. Do not address a hypothetical reader, narrate the newsroom, or add a moral at the end. Test especially the first and last sentence of every card: each must add a story-specific fact or inference. Headlines should name the actor and action early; deks should add a concrete detail rather than repeat the headline.
