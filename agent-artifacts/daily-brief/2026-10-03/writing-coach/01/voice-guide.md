# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-10-03

## How this piece should sound

The register is plain and exact. Each item is a few sentences, and each sentence
should carry a fact a reader can check, in the manner of Hamish Johnston's Nobel
report and Simon Willison's note, where there is no throat-clearing and no
sentence that only introduces the next one. The reader is fluent in mathematics
and computer science, so a technical term can stand once it is defined, as
Anatoly Liberman names enantiosemy only after he has shown what it means.

For the three news reports, the hard fact and the figure can lead, with the
qualifier behind them. Where a figure exists — a dollar amount of servers
diverted, a count of troops moved — give it, the way Johnston gives "a SEK 11m
prize ($1.2m)" with the conversion beside it so a reader can scale it. A claim
that is asserted rather than settled is attributed to whoever asserts it and in
what capacity, as Johnston writes "announced this morning by members of the
Royal Swedish Academy of Science" and names Olle Eriksson with his role before
quoting him. Where the primary filing and the independent reporting diverge, or
a number is still provisional, the item can say so plainly rather than pick one
for neatness.

For the two technology notes, say what is new, who announced it, and, where it
matters, what is still missing: Willison states the gap ("What I'd really like
to see are release notes for the models themselves") and shows how he knows.
Anything a reader could type, paste or match goes in as the literal string — a
feature name such as `pg_plan_advice` or `REPACK ... CONCURRENTLY`, a version
number — the way Willison quotes the identifier `claude-3-haiku-20240307`
instead of describing it. The release status is stated exactly; a beta is not
its general-availability release.

For the Word of the Day, the card can set the word's flavour before its meaning
and then land the exact definition, as Quinion puts the carpetbag-and-cigar
picture against "a shrewd, unprincipled person, especially a politician." The
origin can be built in short, exact steps — language, parts, meaning — the way
Liberman builds coleslaw from Dutch, giving a cognate in passing without
stopping the sentence. Where a derivation is disputed or a credit uncertain,
the card can say exactly how much is known rather than smooth it over, as
Quinion writes "The origin is unknown, though the Oxford English Dictionary
suggests"; one dated, specific episode can carry the rest of the card, as
Truman's 1952 misdefinition carries snollygoster, and a word with no such moment
needs none invented for it.

The six items are one day's selection by one editor, so they hold one voice, and
a sentence earns its place only where it has something to say. An opening or
closing line added for shape is the first thing to cut.

## Michael Quinion, "Snollygoster"

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-sno1.htm

> "This is another of that set of extroverted and fanciful words that originated in the fast-expanding United States of the nineteenth century. I see a snollygoster as a outsized individual with a carpetbag, flowered waistcoat, expansive demeanour and a large cigar. It actually refers to a shrewd, unprincipled person, especially a politician."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-sno1.htm, retrieved 2026-10-03

Quinion gives the picture before the meaning, then drops to a flat definition:
"a shrewd, unprincipled person, especially a politician." He marks the picture
as his own invention — "I see a snollygoster as" — so it colours the word
without being passed off as its history. The first person is where Quinion is
visible; the exactness of the final clause is where the lexicographer is.

> "These days it’s hardly heard. Its last burst of public notice came when President Truman used it in 1952, and defined it, either in ignorance or impishness, as “a man born out of wedlock”."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-sno1.htm, retrieved 2026-10-03

One dated episode carries the word's afterlife. "Either in ignorance or
impishness" is Quinion declining to decide which, and the restraint is his: he
reports the moment without ruling on Truman's motive. The sentence shows the
word still in use rather than asserting that it is colourful.

> "The origin is unknown, though the Oxford English Dictionary suggests it may be linked to snallygoster, which some suppose to derive from the German schnelle Geister, literally a fast-moving ghost, and which was a mythical monster of vast size — half reptile, half bird — supposedly found in Maryland, and which was invented to terrify ex-slaves out of voting."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-sno1.htm, retrieved 2026-10-03

Quinion states the uncertainty plainly — "The origin is unknown" — and then
lays out the one guess the OED offers, attributed to it and hedged again with
"some suppose." He does not round the doubt up into a story. A reader finishes
the sentence knowing exactly how much is settled.

## Anatoly Liberman, "Etymology gleanings for September 2013"

Source: https://blog.oup.com/2013/09/simpleton-capsize-kibosh-hobo-bragi-sanction-coleslaw/

> "This word is from Dutch. The Dutch compound is made up of two parts: one means “cabbage” (compare Engl. cole and German Kohl, the same meaning; both from Latin), the other means “salad.” Dutch sla is a conversational form of salad. The whole amounts to “cabbage salad.”"

Checked: https://blog.oup.com/2013/09/simpleton-capsize-kibosh-hobo-bragi-sanction-coleslaw/, retrieved 2026-10-03

The etymology is built in short declaratives, one step to a sentence: the
source language, the two parts, the gloss of each, the sum. Liberman slips the
cognates in without stopping the sentence — "compare Engl. cole and German
Kohl." Nothing is decorated and nothing is left approximate.

> "Sanction has always referred to law (that is, encouragement to do something) and a penalty for breaking it, though the first sense is of course primary (sanctify is a cognate of saint, from Latin sanctus). In the twentieth century, sanction “punitive measure” became so common that when today we read about sanctions, we think only about actions undertaken to enforce international rules. However, the verb in its positive meaning has survived. The coexistence of two opposite meanings in a word is called enantiosemy, and the examples are rather numerous."

Checked: https://blog.oup.com/2013/09/simpleton-capsize-kibosh-hobo-bragi-sanction-coleslaw/, retrieved 2026-10-03

Liberman explains why one word carries opposite senses, and only then names the
thing: "The coexistence of two opposite meanings in a word is called
enantiosemy." The term arrives after the reader already understands it, so it
teaches rather than labels. The scholar is visible in the parenthetical asides
and in the flat judgement that the approving sense "is of course primary."

## Simon Willison, "Anthropic release notes"

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Jun/18/anthropic-release-notes/

> "Anthropic have started publishing release notes! Currently available for their API and their apps (mobile and web)."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Jun/18/anthropic-release-notes/, retrieved 2026-10-03

The whole of the news sits in two short sentences: what happened, then exactly
where it applies. "Currently available for their API and their apps (mobile and
web)" is specific enough that a reader knows the scope and nothing is promised
beyond it. There is no preamble before the fact.

> "What I'd really like to see are release notes for the models themselves, though as far as I can tell there haven't been any updates to those since the Claude 3 models were first released (the Haiku model name in the API is still claude-3-haiku-20240307 and Anthropic say they'll change that identifier after any updates to the model)."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Jun/18/anthropic-release-notes/, retrieved 2026-10-03

Willison names the gap and then shows his evidence for it: the identifier
`claude-3-haiku-20240307` is quoted exactly, because it is a string a reader can
check, and it is what tells him nothing has shipped. He states his own wish
without hedging and then grounds it in the one observable fact. The parenthetical
carries the proof so the main clause can stay short.

## Hamish Johnston, "John Clarke, Michel Devoret and John Martinis win the 2025 Nobel Prize for Physics" (Physics World)

Source: https://physicsworld.com/a/john-clarke-michel-devoret-and-john-martinis-win-the-2025-nobel-prize-for-physics/

> "John Clarke, Michel Devoret and John Martinis share the 2025 Nobel Prize for Physics “for the discovery of macroscopic quantum mechanical tunnelling and energy quantization in an electric circuit”."

Checked: https://physicsworld.com/a/john-clarke-michel-devoret-and-john-martinis-win-the-2025-nobel-prize-for-physics/, retrieved 2026-10-03

The first sentence states who won and, in the committee's own words, for what.
Johnston quotes the citation exactly rather than paraphrasing the physics, so
the precise claim is on the record and not his restatement of it. The names and
the reason come before anything else.

> "The award includes a SEK 11m prize ($1.2m), which is shared equally by the winners. The prize will be presented at a ceremony in Stockholm on 10 December."

Checked: https://physicsworld.com/a/john-clarke-michel-devoret-and-john-martinis-win-the-2025-nobel-prize-for-physics/, retrieved 2026-10-03

Two facts, two sentences, each with a number: the amount and the date. The
figure is given with the dollar conversion beside the krona, so a reader who
does not hold the one currency can still scale it. Nothing is called large; the
number does that.

> "The prize was announced this morning by members of the Royal Swedish Academy of Science. Olle Eriksson of Uppsala University and chair of the Nobel Committee for Physics commented, “There is no advanced technology today that does not rely on quantum mechanics.”"

Checked: https://physicsworld.com/a/john-clarke-michel-devoret-and-john-martinis-win-the-2025-nobel-prize-for-physics/, retrieved 2026-10-03

Every claim is pinned to a named source: the announcing body first, then Olle
Eriksson, named with his role, before the line he is quoted saying. A reader can
see who is speaking and in what capacity, which is what lets the quoted opinion
stand without the writer vouching for it.
