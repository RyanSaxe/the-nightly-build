# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-03 (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a morning read of about three minutes for people who build machine learning systems and hold degrees in computer science or mathematics. They do not need the field explained to them, and they do not need the news dressed up. The register is a wire reporter's: past tense for what happened, a named actor for every action, the number as the source gives it. The BBC fusion report and Simon Willison's computer-use post both open on what happened and who did it, and then stay on that. Neither says why the reader should care before saying what occurred.

Each news and technology item is two or three sentences, so there is room for the event, the actor, and one consequence, and the item is done when the material runs out. Where a figure appears, the BBC passage shows one set beside something a reader already holds (a peppercorn, a few kettles) and the same passage gives the plain megajoule figures when the audience can use them. For this audience the technical word usually is the plain word. Willison writes "bounding boxes", "model IDs" and "prompt injection" without gloss, and a brief item can do the same for terms this readership uses daily. A term from outside their field gets a clause of definition in the sentence that first needs it.

The person on the page in Willison's post is someone who tried the thing and says what he saw ("It worked exactly as advertised"). A brief cannot try anything, but it can attribute precisely and say plainly when a claim belongs to a company and has not been checked. Where the source is a filing, a paper, or a release page, the sentence can say what the document states in the document's own terms.

The Word of the Day is the one place a story is told, in three or four sentences. The two Merriam-Webster entries show the pattern the series asks for: a date, a name, a place, a documented line from source to usage, with the uncertainty stated where the record is thin (the wraith entry says etymologists can trace the word only to 1513). A word story can end where the record ends. Neither entry needs a lesson at the close, and the wraith entry's last move is a usage example only because that usage exists. A closing twist is available when the history has one and is not owed.

Humor, where it comes, sits in the facts themselves: an elephant count, a mustache. It is not added in the phrasing.

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Oct/22/computer-use/ (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-03
The opening names the company, the two releases and the date in one sentence, with the company's own term for the feature. A practitioner can tell at once what shipped.

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Oct/22/computer-use/ (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-03
He places the new capability against a specific existing product and states the concrete difference. The term "hosted virtual machine" is used without explanation because his readers know it.

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Oct/22/computer-use/ (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-03
This is a first-person fact that accounts for why he is in a position to say what he says. It is one plain sentence, with the version number given.
