# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-12

## How this piece should sound

This is a three-minute digest for engineers who build machine learning systems,
and it should read like a colleague who checked the record before telling you
what happened. The register is plain and declarative. Each news and technology
item opens on the event in its own nouns: who acted, what they did, the figure
or document that shows it. Tom Chivers's Mexico item and LWN's Firefox and gawk
items get the event into the first sentence and the detail into the second.
Where an item needs one fact of history to make sense, as LWN's LibreOffice item
does, it can take a single sentence with a year in it before the news. With two
or three sentences per item there is no room for a scene-setter or a sentence
that grades the news.

Attribution is the main tool for being fair in so little space. Chivers sets a
report's finding against the government's claim and names who holds each
("a new report found", "The government claims"), so a disputed point stays
disputed without the writer taking a side the record has not settled. LWN
handles a project dispute the same way: it names the other party and its
interest ("a major LibreOffice developer that has its own online offering") and
leaves the merits alone. Where a claim is only the claimant's, the item may say
so in those terms.

The technical items can use the practitioner's words without apology. LWN writes
"default regular-expression matcher" and names MinRX without stopping to
explain what a regular expression is. This audience needs a definition only
for a term specific to the development, and Chivers's ancient-life item shows
how to supply one in a clause ("organisms called Asgard archaea") without
slowing the sentence.

The Word of the Day is where a person on the page is most visible. Michael
Quinion and Ben Zimmer both tell a word's history as a sequence of dated,
attributed events (a coiner, a publication, a year) and let one observation
about the record carry the wit. Quinion's "which ironically became a textbook
for art students" and Zimmer's note that Kenney "went ahead and ruined a whole
lifetime's worth of reading and viewing" are dry because they are factual. If
the word's story has a turn like that in it, the item has room for one, in the
story's own nouns. If it does not, a clean dated account is enough.

Neither lexicographer pretends a dating is firmer than it is. Zimmer says where
the phrase "first appears" and credits the people who found the earlier uses.
Quinion says a word "remains rare". The word item may state the first recorded
use, the coiner and the source that documents them, and say plainly where the
record runs out.

## Michael Quinion, "Grawlix"

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/ww-gra1.html

> "The word was the creation of the cartoonist Mort Walker, by which he meant a
> comic-strip balloon containing symbols to indicate profanity. He first used it
> in 1964 in an article he wrote for the National Cartoonists Society in the US.
> He included it in his 1980 book The Lexicon of Comicana, a satire on
> cartoonists' comic devices, which ironically became a textbook for art
> students."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/ww-gra1.html (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-12
Three sentences carry coiner, meaning, first use with a year and venue, and a
later appearance with a year. The only comment is the relative clause at the
end, and it is a fact about what happened to the book. Quinion's dry
temperament shows in choosing that one fact to finish on.

> "It would be hard to define a grawlix in a way that adequately distinguishes
> it from the others — they are all symbols and squiggles of various sorts —
> though his illustration for it is of what he calls "obstensibly obliterated
> epithets", or scribbled-out words. Of the four, grawlix has gained the
> greatest currency, though it remains rare, and has largely taken over the
> territory of the other three."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/ww-gra1.html (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-12
Quinion admits the definition is fuzzy and then gives the coiner's own example,
quoted with its misspelling intact. He reports how far the word has spread and
qualifies it in the same sentence ("though it remains rare"). This is a
lexicographer who will not overstate a word's standing to make it sound more
interesting.

## Ben Zimmer, "Spoiler Alert! Revealing the Origins of the 'Spoiler'"

Source: https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wordroutes/spoiler-alert-revealing-the-origins-of-the-spoiler/

> "Novels and films have long had twist endings, but no one was demanding
> "spoiler alerts" about, say, the identity of the murderer in an Agatha
> Christie whodunit or the meaning of "Rosebud" in Citizen Kane. In 1960, Alfred
> Hitchcock wanted his audiences to remain spoiler-free when watching "Psycho,"
> pleading with moviegoers, "Please don't give away the ending, it's the only
> one we have." Still, this sort of plot revelation had not yet been dubbed a
> "spoiler.""

Checked: https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wordroutes/spoiler-alert-revealing-the-origins-of-the-spoiler/ (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-12
Zimmer separates the thing from the word for it: the practice existed before
the name did. He uses examples the reader already holds and a dated quotation,
then closes on the gap the rest of the history fills. The "say," is a small
conversational aside that tells you a person is talking.

> "That would first happen about a decade later in the pages of the National
> Lampoon, an edgy humor magazine that had recently been spun off from the
> Harvard Lampoon. Doug Kenney's feature "Spoilers" in the April 1971 issue went
> ahead and ruined a whole lifetime's worth of reading and viewing — including
> Citizen Kane, Psycho, and those Agatha Christie mysteries."

Checked: https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wordroutes/spoiler-alert-revealing-the-origins-of-the-spoiler/ (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-12
The coining is given with a named author, a publication, and a month and year,
and the publication gets a one-clause gloss. The humor comes from the record:
Kenney really did spoil the very examples Zimmer listed earlier. "Went ahead
and" is Zimmer's own voice, mildly amused, inside a sentence that is still a
citation.

> "Soon there was a new kind of forum for fans who wanted to converse: Usenet
> newsgroups, the earliest Internet discussion groups. The phrase "spoiler
> alert" first appears in a posting about the second Star Trek movie, The Wrath
> of Khan, in 1982."

Checked: https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wordroutes/spoiler-alert-revealing-the-origins-of-the-spoiler/ (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-12
A term this audience may half-know (Usenet) is defined in an appositive, and
the dating sentence states the first appearance with the exact context and
year. "First appears" claims what the evidence shows and no more.

## Tom Chivers, "Semafor Flagship: Growing complexity"

Source: https://www.semafor.com/newsletter/02/20/2026/semafor-flagship-growing-complexity

> "Disappearances in Mexico have shot up by 200% over the last decade, a new
> report found, casting doubt on the government's claim that murder rates have
> plummeted. The rise of disappearances captures "the lethal violence" gripping
> Mexico, one of the report's authors said, and suggests cartels may be
> disposing of bodies to hide traces of killings. The government claims murders
> have steadily dropped since President Claudia Sheinbaum pivoted from her
> predecessor's "hugs, not bullets" approach to quelling violence."

Checked: https://www.semafor.com/newsletter/02/20/2026/semafor-flagship-growing-complexity (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-12
The first sentence gives the figure, the period and who found it, and sets it
against the claim it contradicts. Every claim after that carries its source,
and the government's position is stated in its own terms. Chivers's judgment
shows in which two numbers he puts side by side, not in any adjective.

> "A mystery about how the first complex life arose may have been solved.
> Multicellular creatures — all animals, fungi, and plants — are hybrids. Some
> time around 2 billion years ago, some ancient bacteria entered into other
> single-celled organisms called Asgard archaea, becoming what are now
> mitochondria, which generate energy in our cells."

Checked: https://www.semafor.com/newsletter/02/20/2026/semafor-flagship-growing-complexity (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-12
The lead hedges exactly as much as the research allows ("may have been
solved"), and the next two sentences give the background needed to follow the
result. "Multicellular creatures ... are hybrids" is a science writer stating
the textbook background as one short claim a general reader can hold.

> "A familiar premise is turned into something meditative and philosophical —
> albeit with no groundhogs — in this seven-volume International
> Booker-shortlisted novel."

Checked: https://www.semafor.com/newsletter/02/20/2026/semafor-flagship-growing-complexity (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-12
This is the one joke in a ten-item digest, and it sits in the lightest slot.
It depends on the item's own nouns, since the premise is a Groundhog Day loop,
and the sentence still delivers the length and the prize shortlist.

## LWN.net, "Brief items" (Weekly Edition for February 26, 2026)

Source: https://lwn.net/Articles/1059501/bigpage

The brief items are unsigned house copy; the page editor for this section was
Daroc Alden. LWN's short items are the standard among kernel and free-software
developers for reporting a release without promotion.

> "Version 148 of Firefox has been released. The most notable change in this
> release is the addition of a "Block AI enhancements" option that allows
> turning off "new or current AI enhancements in Firefox, or pop-ups about
> them" with a single toggle."

Checked: https://lwn.net/Articles/1059501/bigpage (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-12
The release is stated in five words, and the second sentence picks the one
change that matters and quotes the vendor's wording for it. LWN's editors make
a judgment ("most notable") and then show the thing they judged.

> "Version 5.4.0 of GNU awk (gawk) has been released. This is a major release with a change in gawk's
> default regular-expression matcher: it now uses MinRX as the default
> regular-expression engine."

Checked: https://lwn.net/Articles/1059501/bigpage (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-12
The item names the component that changed in the vocabulary a gawk user would
use and does not explain what a matcher is. That is a decision about who is
reading: practitioners, addressed as practitioners.

> "This project was put into stasis back in 2022, a move marked by some tension
> with Collabora, a major LibreOffice developer that has its own online
> offering. Now, the Document Foundation has announced a new effort to breathe
> life into this project."

Checked: https://lwn.net/Articles/1059501/bigpage (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-12
One sentence of history with a year, one sentence of news with the organization
that made it. The dispute is mentioned with the other party named and its
interest stated, so the reader can see why there was tension without being told
who was right.
