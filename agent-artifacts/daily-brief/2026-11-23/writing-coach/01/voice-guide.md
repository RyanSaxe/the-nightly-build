# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-23

## How this piece should sound

This is a three-minute read for people who build machine learning systems and read widely outside it. It holds the register of a good colleague's forwarded link: plain, specific, and slightly amused when the material is. Nothing in it explains what the reader already knows. A term gets one clause of definition only where a reader outside that field would stall, and the field's own vocabulary stays as the practitioners use it.

For the five news and technology items, Simon Willison's two link posts below show the shape the material may call for at this length. Each opens by saying what the thing is and who made it, in the nouns a practitioner would use (system prompts, JSON-RPC over standard input and standard output, evals). Each puts a named person's words in the item where there is one, with the name and the role beside them. Willison also says where he stands relative to the source ("I got a preview of this last week") when that bears on how far to trust him. Where an item has a figure, a count, a date or a named product, that is the sentence to write, as in "There are 13 of these in the modelcontextprotocol/servers GitHub repository so far". Where a limit of the release is itself the finding, he states it flatly and moves on. A brief may find it has nothing to add beyond what happened, who says so, and what follows; then it stops.

Attribution is part of the sentence and is not appended to it. In the v0 post the CTO's quotation is introduced in the same breath as the leak. An unverified claim carries its source's name where the claim is made. A closing "has not commented" belongs only where the silence is the news.

For the Word of the Day, the two Merriam-Webster entries below show how word history reads when it is told as a story with people in it: a named editor, a named poet, a year, a title, and a thing somebody said. The Bowdler entry puts the surprise (the sister did the work) after the setup that makes it a surprise. The Pandemonium entry walks the word from Milton's invention through its parts to its later sense and takes one light jab ("as any sensible 17th-century writer would") that comes from the facts, not from a joke bolted on. The story is documented dates and documented speakers. Where the first use is not documented, say so in the sentence. The entry may allow itself one wry clause. It may not run past three or four sentences.

The brief's length decides one more thing the exemplars cannot. Six items in about 300 to 700 words means each item has room for the one sentence a reader would repeat to a colleague, and not for a second. Choose the fact that does that work and cut the rest.

## Merriam-Webster editors, "Bowdlerize" (Did You Know?)

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/bowdlerize

> "In 1807, a new edition of the works of William Shakespeare hit the scene in England. Titled The Family Shakespeare, the collection of 20 of the Bard's plays in four volumes was at first anonymously edited, and promised in its preface to "remove every thing that could give just offence to the religious or virtuous mind.""

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/bowdlerize, retrieved 2026-11-23
The opening sentence gives a year, a place and a title with no lead-in. The second adds the count (20 plays, four volumes) and quotes the preface directly, so the reader meets the thing in its own words. The writer is visible in the choice to quote the preface's own phrase and in "hit the scene".

> "Though the sanitized project later became a public sensation (and a source of literary derision) after its expanded, ten-volume second edition was published in 1818 and credited solely to physician Thomas Bowdler, the original expurgation was in fact the work of his older sister Henrietta Maria "Harriet" Bowdler, an accomplished editor and author."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/bowdlerize, retrieved 2026-11-23
One long sentence carries the correction, and every clause in it is a checkable fact (an edition, a year, an occupation, a full name). The parenthesis is the only editorial aside, and it is short.

> "Within a year of the younger Bowdler's death in 1825, bowdlerize had come to refer to cutting out the dirty bits of other books and texts—testimony not only to the impact of his eye for impropriety, but to those of his sister Harriet as well, though her efforts were obscured by history, if not technically bowdlerized."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/bowdlerize, retrieved 2026-11-23
The date of the word's arrival is given as an interval after a death. "Dirty bits" is a plain-spoken phrase in an otherwise formal sentence, and the last clause turns the headword on the subject of the story. This is the entry's one wry move, and it comes from the facts.

## Merriam-Webster editors, "Pandemonium" (Did You Know?)

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/pandemonium

> "When John Milton needed a name for the gathering place of all demons for Paradise Lost, he turned to the classics as any sensible 17th-century writer would. Pandæmonium, as the capital of Hell is known in the epic poem, combines the Greek prefix pan-, meaning "all," with the Late Latin daemonium, meaning "evil spirit.""

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/pandemonium, retrieved 2026-11-23
The first sentence puts a named person in a position with a need, and the second gives the parts of the word with their languages and glosses. The clause "as any sensible 17th-century writer would" is where the writer is visible. It is a dry remark about a habit of the period, and it does not interrupt the etymology.

> "Over time, Pandæmonium (or Pandemonium) came to designate all of hell and was used as well for earthbound dens of wickedness and sin. By the late-18th century, the word implied a place or state of confusion or uproar, and from there, it didn't take long for pandemonium to become associated with states of utter disorder and wildness."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/pandemonium, retrieved 2026-11-23
The change in meaning is told as steps with a date marker, from a proper noun to hell to any scene of uproar. "Earthbound dens of wickedness and sin" is a specific phrase where "later uses" would have done, and "it didn't take long" is the writer talking.
