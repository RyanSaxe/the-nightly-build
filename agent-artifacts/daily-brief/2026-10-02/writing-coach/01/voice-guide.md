# Voice guide: Daily Brief, 2026-10-02

## How this piece should sound

Two registers share the page, and the exemplars show both. The five news and technology items sit in the register of the NPR Up First newsletter and of Simon Willison's short notes: a declarative sentence says what happened, the next names who is saying so, and a plain clause says what is not yet known. Brittney Melton's Miami crash item ("It remains unclear if the people who were hurt or killed were on the plane or on the ground") and her Saxony-Anhalt item ("has claimed a landslide victory") show the status of the evidence living in the verb and in one short sentence, with no adverb announcing care. Willison does the same from the technology side: "My guess is that" marks his inference as an inference, and the Gemini executive's figure is credited to the executive. Where a claim comes from a company, a filing, a dataset or one named person, the sentence says which, and the sentence about firmness can take whatever form the item's evidence calls for. The reader here knows mathematics and computing, so a figure goes in as the figure, a term of art goes in unexplained when the readers hold it, and nothing is dressed to seem important. Willison's note on Armin Ronacher is a model of crediting a source with a reason: he says who Ronacher is in one sentence, then lets him speak.

The word card is allowed more play, and the models are Michael Quinion and the Merriam-Webster editors. Both stay short and exact about dates and languages ("recorded first about 1500", "adopted by English speakers in the early 1600s") while letting one telling detail do the entertaining: an Elizabethan visitor sadly noting that salads were drowning in alegar, a Napoleon who lists with delight and is not, as modern ears would have it, leaning over. The humor in both comes from the nouns of the story and from restraint. Neither uses an exclamation mark or tells the reader the word is fascinating. Quinion's asides in parentheses and the Merriam-Webster habit of putting a small argument about usage into the origin paragraph ("here's the rub: language is very often untidy") show a person with opinions who still reports the etymology first. A word card can take one such human touch, and the origin and the dated moment still carry the item.

Both registers use plain, concrete sentences, vary their length, and do not close by summarizing. Every item in the quoted pieces ends on its last fact or on a small turn in the material, and the writer here can end the same way. Sentences that report an evidence status can differ from one item to the next, as the commission asks, because in the quoted pieces they do: one item says a thing remains unclear, another says who claimed it, another says what was and was not a breakthrough, another says the writer's own guess is a guess. When a word like "reportedly" or "claimed" is the only marker, check whether the sentence could say what the source is instead.

Length is the other thing the genre decides. Each item is one tight paragraph, and the passages quoted below are rarely longer than three or four sentences before the next thing begins. The writer should find the development, its source and its caveat inside that paragraph, and leave out the background that readers with this audience's training already have.

## Michael Quinion, "Alegar and beeregar" (World Wide Words)

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-ale2.htm

> "Of the words, alegar was much the more common, even though the product was mainly brewed from sour beer rather than from ale (the difference is that ale was made without hops or with only a very small amount). Both products came into English during the late Middle Ages, their names being recorded first about 1500, and became well-known preserving agents, often as a mixture with salt and spices."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-ale2.htm (raw HTML, text compared by script), retrieved 2026-10-02
The dating is given as a plain figure ("about 1500") and the parenthesis is a real aside: it answers a question the reader would otherwise have, then the sentence goes on. The writer is visible in the choice to correct the product's name against what was actually brewed, a small act of precision stated without fuss.

> "But they were fiery products, much fiercer on the palate than the milder wine vinegar. An Italian visitor to England during the first Elizabeth's reign noted sadly that all salads were drowning in alegar, with nothing added to modify its raw ferocity."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-ale2.htm (raw HTML, text compared by script), retrieved 2026-10-02
The liveliness is carried by the witness and the verb "drowning", and the claim is attributed to a type of person at a stated time. "Noted sadly" is the one place where the author's amusement shows, and it is a single word in a sentence that otherwise reports.

## Michael Quinion, "List" (World Wide Words, Topical Words)

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/topicalwords/tw-lis1.htm

> "This sense, the one we know best nowadays, arrived only in the seventeenth century, having been borrowed from the French liste, which could variously mean a border, a band, or a strip of paper. It turns up first in Hamlet, in which young Fortinbras of Norway had “shark'd up a list of landless resolutes” to invade Denmark. So a list was just a lot of names written down on a strip of paper, and to be on a list meant that you had been tagged for some special status or enterprise (hence enlist for joining the army)."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/topicalwords/tw-lis1.htm (raw HTML, text compared by script), retrieved 2026-10-02
Here the origin moves in three steps: the borrowing, the first recorded use with the line quoted, and the sense that follows from it. The last sentence draws a consequence from the evidence and adds a related word in a parenthesis, so the reader leaves with one more thing than was promised.

> "When Ralph Waldo Emerson wrote in a poem in 1857: “Great Napoleon stops his horse, and lists with delight” he didn't intend to say that Napoleon was happily leaning over, though the image is almost irresistible now."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/topicalwords/tw-lis1.htm (raw HTML, text compared by script), retrieved 2026-10-02
A dated quotation is used to show a meaning that has been lost, and the joke is the reader's own misreading, which the sentence names without scolding. "Almost irresistible" is the writer admitting to the same pull, which is where the person shows.

## Merriam-Webster editors, Word of the Day: "loath" (2026-09-10)

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-09-10

> "Loath as some language sticklers may be to accept it—indeed, they may loathe the fact—loathe is an accepted, though less common, spelling variant of the adjective loath. The language sticklers know that loathe is most often used as a verb meaning "to hate," and their desire to keep the language tidy by letting loath alone mean "not willing or wanting to do something" is understandable."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-09-10 (raw HTML, text compared by script), retrieved 2026-10-02
The opening plays on the word itself and then reports the usage fact in the same sentence. The second sentence states the sticklers' position fairly and in their terms before the paragraph goes on.

> "But here's the rub: language is very often untidy. Both loath and loathe trace back to the Old English word lāth, meaning “loathsome, disgusting.” Each has had a number of variant spellings over the years, and for at least some portion of their history each word has taken on the spelling of the other."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-09-10 (raw HTML, text compared by script), retrieved 2026-10-02
The short opening sentence is a turn in the argument, and the origin follows at once with the Old English form and its gloss. The reader gets the language, the dated history and the claim that spellings swapped places, all in the plainest vocabulary.

## Merriam-Webster editors, Word of the Day: "tactile" (2026-09-25)

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-09-25

> "Tactile has many relatives in English, from the sometimes-synonymous tangible to familiar words like intact, tact, tangent, contingent, and even entire. All of these can be traced back to the Latin verb tangere, meaning “to touch.”"

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-09-25 (raw HTML, text compared by script), retrieved 2026-10-02
The list of relatives is long and surprising, and the sentence after it supplies the one root that explains them. A reader with a mathematician's sense of structure gets a family of words from one source.

> "Tactile was adopted by English speakers in the early 1600s (possibly by way of the French tactile) from the Latin adjective tactilis (“tangible”). In light of tactile having tangere for a touchstone, its dual senses of “perceptible by touch” and “of, relating to, or being the sense of touch” are perfectly sensible."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-09-25 (raw HTML, text compared by script), retrieved 2026-10-02
The date, the possible intermediate language and the Latin source are each stated with the degree of certainty the record gives ("possibly by way of"). The pun on touchstone and "sensible" is the editors' one small indulgence, and it sits where the meaning is being explained.

## Brittney Melton (NPR), "Up First briefing: Miami plane crash; Ukraine; Affirmative for Germany"

Source: https://www.npr.org/2026/09/07/g-s1-142210/up-first-newsletter-miami-plane-crash-ukraine-affirmative-for-germany

> "The National Transportation Safety Board is investigating after a cargo plane crashed at Miami International Airport yesterday, killing five people and injuring five others. The Amazon Prime plane was landing at the airport around 2 p.m. ET when it overran the runway and crashed into vehicles on the ground. It remains unclear if the people who were hurt or killed were on the plane or on the ground at the time of the collision."

Checked: https://www.npr.org/2026/09/07/g-s1-142210/up-first-newsletter-miami-plane-crash-ukraine-affirmative-for-germany (raw HTML, text compared by script), retrieved 2026-10-02
The agency, the event and the toll come first, then the circumstance, then the one thing not yet known, in three sentences with no adjectives. The status of the evidence is a single plain sentence naming exactly what is unresolved.

> "Trump administration envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner said their meetings with Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy in Kyiv yesterday were "substantive." The envoys held extensive conversations with Russian President Vladimir Putin in Moscow the day before. Witkoff and Kushner want peace talks to resume, but the visit did not yield a breakthrough."

Checked: https://www.npr.org/2026/09/07/g-s1-142210/up-first-newsletter-miami-plane-crash-ukraine-affirmative-for-germany (raw HTML, text compared by script), retrieved 2026-10-02
The word "substantive" is put in quotation marks and credited to the envoys, so the characterization is theirs and the outcome is stated separately in the last clause. What the participants wanted and what happened are kept in different sentences.

> "The far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) party has claimed a landslide victory in the German state of Saxony-Anhalt, narrowly missing an absolute majority to form the regional government. It is unclear whether AfD will govern the state yet, but this outcome is shaking Germany's political landscape."

Checked: https://www.npr.org/2026/09/07/g-s1-142210/up-first-newsletter-miami-plane-crash-ukraine-affirmative-for-germany (raw HTML, text compared by script), retrieved 2026-10-02
"Has claimed" puts the victory with the party, and the next sentence says what is not settled. The second clause of that sentence leans toward consequence and is the loosest wording in the passage, so it shows where the genre's pull toward emphasis lies.

## Simon Willison, "Sora 2" (note)

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2025/Sep/30/sora-2/

> "Having watched this morning's Sora 2 introduction video, the most notable feature (aside from audio generation - original Sora was silent, Google's Veo 3 supported audio in May 2025) looks to be what OpenAI are calling "cameos" - the ability to easily capture a video version of yourself or your friends and then use them as characters in generated videos."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2025/Sep/30/sora-2/ (raw HTML, text compared by script), retrieved 2026-10-02
The first sentence says what he actually looked at (the introduction video) and then what he thinks is most notable, so the reader knows the basis of the judgment. The vendor's own term is credited ("what OpenAI are calling"), and the parenthesis gives the comparison with a dated prior release.

> "My guess is that they are leaning into this based on the incredible success of ChatGPT image generation in March - possibly the most successful product launch of all time, signing up 100 million new users in just the first week after release."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2025/Sep/30/sora-2/ (raw HTML, text compared by script), retrieved 2026-10-02
He labels the inference as a guess before giving it, and the supporting figure carries a "possibly" with a number beside it. The person is visible in "My guess is", which is first-person without being an opinion column; a house brief that speaks as the paper would put the same marker in the third person.
