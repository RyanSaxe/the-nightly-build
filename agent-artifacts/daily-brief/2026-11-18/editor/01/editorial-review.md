# Editorial review: daily-brief/2026-11-18 (editor/01)

## Correct

Thesis, from the draft alone: the NSA's classified network went offline on
17 November, the latest of several US outages that week, and the officials
explaining those outages point to patching and old systems, not an attack.
Under it, five more items: Castiglione's sprezzatura, the Crimean Bridge road
span, the Nvidia board's refusal to reinstate Huang alongside the Q3 results,
the BIS remote-access rule, and the Nature Sustainability estimate for
hyperscale data centers.

The lead broke in two places, both in its most quoted line.

- "98% certain" is not Mullin's wording. The Reuters copy (Idaho Business
  Review, paragraph 3) reads: "I can tell you 98% certainty this isn't a
  cyberattack." The evidence record had normalized it, and the draft put the
  normalized form inside quotation marks.
- Its scope was overstated. Reuters ties Mullin's remarks to United, the FAA,
  Microsoft and JPMorgan Chase. The story never mentions the NSA, NSANet or
  Apple. "None of the week's outages" therefore stretched his estimate over the
  NSANet outage, and the old dek pinned that estimate directly to the NSA.
  Fix: the figure is gone from the dek and the body. Mullin keeps his
  explanation (new technology layered on old systems), which is what Reuters
  reports him saying about the outages he named. The claim that no outage has
  been attributed to a cyberattack now rests on AP's own sentence ("None of the
  outages has been attributed to a cyberattack or nefarious activity"), and AP
  includes NSANet.

The headline's "a week of US system failures" holds against AP, which places
the NSA episode "among a series of cyber-related disruptions ... in the last
week" (FAA on Friday 13 November, JPMorgan and United on Monday, Apple on
Tuesday). It now says "outages", the word both wire stories use and the word
the item uses.

Clayton's remark was made "about the spate of disruptions", not about NSANet
in particular. The item presents it as one of three explanations for the
week's outages, which matches AP. CBS's two sources supply the patching account
for NSANet itself, and the item does not overstate it.

The rest held when I reopened the sources:

- Castiglione: the 1854 Le Monnier scan has "per dir forse una nuova parola"
  (p. 35), Bibbiena's Roberto passage and the Count's reply (p. 36), and the
  April 1528 Aldine colophon (p. 349). Merriam-Webster gives the pronunciation
  and definition.
- BBC: the road span collapsed, the rail arch is still standing, the transport
  ministry reports some rail services resumed, and the article covers Peskov,
  the terrorism case, the land corridor and the limited ferry.
- Nvidia 8-K Ex. 99.1: $115.8 billion, up 103% year on year, and $130.0
  billion ±3% with no China Data Center compute revenue. CNBC: Jones as board
  chair on the call, Huang's "honored", Heidrick & Struggles.
- Rule PDF: 12,000,000 "aggregate TPP" (p. 7), 90 days (p. 4), the five
  countries and the US-headquartered exception (SUMMARY).
- Nature abstract: 403 centers, about 82 TWh in the central scenario, 1.8%,
  25.7 Mt, 54% fossil, 314 against 348 gCO2/kWh.

Country Group D:5: the rule's own preamble (PDF p. 11) names "Macau, the
People's Republic of China (PRC), or other Country Group D:5 headquartered
entities", and §744.23 gives "a PRC-headquartered cloud or data server
provider" as its example (PDF p. 28). That supports saying the controlled
companies include Chinese ones, so the item now says "headquartered in China or
elsewhere in Country Group D:5 or Macau", and the citation locator includes
p. 11. I did not list the rest of D:5's membership, because no source I opened
gives it.

I dropped one candidate fact. CNBC writes that the rule "would prevent Nvidia's
chips from being rented to Chinese companies entirely". The rule sets a
12,000,000 aggregate-TPP threshold, so "entirely" goes further than the
primary. The line stays out.

Every printed href opens on its source (CBS, the FNN copy of AP, the IBR copy of
Reuters, BBC, the three Wikisource pages, Merriam-Webster, the Federal
Register PDF and Nature by direct fetch; the CNBC and SEC pages through a
browser-style fetch, since both refuse plain curl). Source kinds are right:
the four wire and broadcast reports and the CNBC live blog are secondary, and
the 8-K, the rule, the paper and Castiglione's text are primary. I corrected
paragraph locators for CBS, AP and BBC to the paragraphs that carry each claim.

## Reads well

- The old dek joined two clauses with "and", and its second clause was a quoted
  figure from an official. Your notes flag that as a recent habit (the
  17 November dek ran a quoted admission). The new dek gives the causes the
  headline leaves out and the AI link that matters to this paper's readers. It
  carries no date clause and no quoted figure.
- The lead's item heading and the new dek both opened "Officials blame". The
  heading now carries the NSA's own account instead.
- "It caps a week of failures" went. "Caps" was decoration, and "failures" was
  a second word for "outages".
- Word item: "The book's Count" dangled for anyone arriving from a link. It now
  reads "In the book's dialogue the Count". The Roberto sentence had lost the
  point of the joke ("keeps dancing as his robe slips off"). The source says he
  lets the robe fall to show he isn't thinking about it. It is now two plain
  sentences: Bibbiena names Roberto, and the Count gives the ruling and the
  reason from the text. The item ends on that ruling, not on a quoted line or a
  dating dispute, which breaks the recent pattern.
- Nvidia now names the company in its first sentence and says Huang resigned,
  so the item makes sense without the 13 November brief.
- BIS: "license destinations" was regulator jargon, recast as "Shipments to ...
  also need one". The heading "Commerce licenses chips" read as if Commerce were
  granting licenses, and it now says Commerce requires one.
- Nature: "attributed to it" had no clear referent. The central-scenario
  qualifier is now stated, and the fossil share, CO2 and intensity sit in one
  sentence whose "but" marks a real contrast in the abstract.

The draft was already plain and close to the voice guide's register. No
sentence failed the placeholder test outright.

## The experience

beyond the word card suit a three-minute brief, and no figure would show
anything faster than the sentences do. The draft finished at 684 words and the
edited piece at 693, with BLOCK 0 and WARN 0.

The handoff claims two pieces of original work. The first survives and is
sharper after the edit: the lead separates what is established (the NSA's
statement, and AP's line that no outage has been attributed to an attack) from
three officials' explanations, each tied to its owner and scope. The second,
setting Nvidia's no-China outlook beside the rule, is adjacency only, because
the only sentence that linked the two (CNBC's) overstated the rule. Selection
and correct scoping are this brief's contribution, which is enough for the
format.
