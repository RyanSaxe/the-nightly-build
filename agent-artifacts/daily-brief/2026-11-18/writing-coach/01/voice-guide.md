# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-18 (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a three-minute read for engineers who build machine learning systems
and read widely outside that work. The register is a well-informed colleague
passing on what happened: plain declarative sentences, the exact names of the
people, institutions, products and documents involved, and no warm-up. Nobody
reading it needs to be told a story matters. The figures, the actor and the
consequence tell them, so the item can go straight to those.

A two- or three-sentence item still reads as prose and not as a ticker line.
Willison's Astral post shows how: each sentence is complete, carries a
different job (what changed, how large it is, what happens next), and the
second sentence picks up a noun from the first so the item reads as one
thought. Where the material gives a number that shows scale, as his download
counts do, it can sit in the middle sentence beside a comparison the reader
already holds. A dated next step, like his "As-of December 17th", is the kind
of "what follows" an item may close on when the source supplies one. Keep
attribution in the sentence ("Astral's post", a named official, a filing) and
not in a trailing hedge. Avoid the telegraphic habits of headline-ese:
dropped articles and verbs, strings of noun phrases, sentence fragments
standing in for claims.

The Word of the Day is the one item with room for a person and a scene. The
series fixes its furniture (part of speech, pronunciation, a one-sentence
definition), and Garg's entry shows how bare that furniture can be. The three
or four sentences after it are where Quinion's manner applies: name who coined
the word, where and when it was printed, and what they were referring to, in
that order of plain fact, then let one sentence carry the detail that makes
the story worth telling, as his jailed camel-thief princes do. Garg's opening
shows that a dry line about how rarely coinages survive can sit next to the
facts without becoming the point. Any humor belongs to the documented story,
in its own nouns, and the item ends on a fact and not on a moral about
language.

Across all six items, treat the reader as someone who will check. Say who
claims what, give the figure the source gives, and stop when the item's three
sentences have said what happened, who did it, and what follows.

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/3/python-build-standalone-astral/

> "Gregory Szorc's Python Standalone Builds have been quietly running an increasing portion of the Python ecosystem for a few years now, but really accelerated in importance when uv started using them for new Python installations managed by that tool. The releases (shipped via GitHub) have now been downloaded over 70 million times, 50 million of those since uv's initial release in March of this year."

A whole news item in two sentences: who built the thing, why it now matters,
and a figure split so the second number explains the first. The person is
visible in "quietly running" and "really accelerated", the judgments of
someone who uses these builds, stated as plainly as the counts.

> "As-of December 17th Gregory will be transferring the project to the Astral organization, while staying on as a maintainer and advisor. Here's Astral's post about this: A new home for python-build-standalone."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/3/python-build-standalone-astral/ (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-18
The close is a dated consequence and a pointer to the primary source, with no
summing up. He says what happens next and who said so, and stops.

## Michael Quinion, "Serendipity" (World Wide Words)

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-ser1.htm

> "Horace Walpole coined it in a letter he wrote to his long-time diplomat friend Horace Mann in 1754. He told him that he invented it in reference to the title of an old Persian fairy story The Three Princes of Serendip, whose heroes regularly discovered pleasant things that they weren’t searching for."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-ser1.htm (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-18
Coiner, document, recipient, year, and the source of the coinage, in two
ordinary sentences. The person shows in small chosen details, "long-time
diplomat friend", that make Walpole someone writing a letter and not a name
on a citation.

> "If you read the story you will find that the princes were well educated and intelligent men and their good fortune (which was a bit slap-dash: they got thrown in jail as suspected camel thieves at one point) was based on careful deduction and sagacity, not chance."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-ser1.htm (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-18
The joke is a fact from the source, dropped into a parenthesis, and the
sentence still finishes its correction of the usual reading. "A bit
slap-dash" is a conversational aside from someone who has read the story
himself.

## Anu Garg, "galumph" (A.Word.A.Day)

Source: https://wordsmith.org/words/galumph.html

> "Odds are higher that you’d win a lottery or be struck by lightning than that you’d coin a word that becomes a part of the language (but that’s not a reason to not coin a word)."

Checked: https://wordsmith.org/words/galumph.html (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-18
A wry framing set beside a plain comparison anyone can scale. The
parenthesis is the writer talking back to his own odds, and it costs one
clause.

> "MEANING:
> verb intr. : To move clumsily or heavily.
> ETYMOLOGY:
> Coined by Lewis Carroll in his 1871 novel Through the Looking-Glass
> A blend of gallop + triumph."

Checked: https://wordsmith.org/words/galumph.html (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-18
The reference furniture at its most compact: part of speech, a definition
that uses no harder word than the headword, and an etymology reduced to
coiner, work, year and mechanism. It shows how little the fixed fields need,
which leaves the story sentences free for the story.
