# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-10-04 (01)

## How this piece should sound

Six items, each one paragraph, each read on its own by someone who has seen nothing else from the paper. The exemplars below do this in three registers that the brief needs: the news paragraph (NPR's Up First newsletter and the Guardian's First Edition), the technology release item (Simon Willison on Kimi K3), and the word card (Merriam-Webster and Anu Garg). All of them open on the thing that happened or the thing the word is. None opens with a sentence about the topic's importance.

News and technology items. Start with the event in its own nouns and the actor who did it, as the Up First Fed item does: the Fed, the date, the rate range, then what that does to a borrower. The stakes come in the second or third sentence and are a consequence somebody could check (the cost of a car loan, a $30 billion estimate), not a judgment. Where a claim is unverified, the exemplars name the person and the outlet in the same sentence as the claim: Netanyahu told Fox News, two Israeli security sources told Reuters, Moonshot's self-reported benchmarks, the CBO asked the Pentagon and got no response. Copy that habit of putting the source inside the clause. When the record does not settle something, say what is missing in the Up First way ("there's no way to compare them with past models"), which tells the reader what would settle it. A vendor figure is a vendor figure: Willison writes "describing it as" and "self-reported" and then gives the independent numbers separately. A technology item can carry a price, a parameter count or a benchmark score as the figure itself, with the claimant named beside it.

Standing alone. Every term that the item depends on gets a clause the first time it appears, the way Willison writes "Chinese AI lab Moonshot AI" and Up First writes "the Congressional Budget Office (CBO)". The reader arriving from a link to item 4 has no item 3. The Guardian bullets show the limit of compression: a place, a body, a verb and a consequence fit in one sentence, and the sentence still needs no neighbour to make sense. A paragraph of three to five sentences is the unit in Up First and in Willison's opening. Past that, the exemplars are writing a different genre.

A person on the page. The briefs are not voiceless. Willison's "I guess they're rounding 2.8 trillion up to 3 trillion" is an aside in parentheses that costs a dozen words and shows someone reading the press release. Up First's reporters appear as named sources ("NPR's Greg Myre says"). Anu Garg lets one dry sentence into a card ("Where the lowest common denominator becomes the gold standard"). Such a touch fits where the material supplies it and not where the facts are grave. An item may have none.

The word card. Merriam-Webster's card order is part of speech, pronunciation, definition, then the history. The history paragraph works by leading with a concrete scene (a frontier town whose bank vaults held broken glass and nails under a layer of coins) and then stating what the word did next in English. Garg's two-line etymology gives the source languages and the earliest documented use as a year, with no flourish. A lively card gets its life from the specific instance, so choose the dated quotation, the person or the object over a general remark about language. The card is the item where a short joke is most welcome and where it must depend on the word's own history. Keep it to the definition, the origin and the one moment.

What to leave out. A closing sentence that says what the development means in general terms. A first sentence that names the topic before the event. "Experts say", "observers note", and any attribution without a name. A figure given as a magnitude when the source gave a number. Any term from the research stage that the reader of this item has not been given. The exemplars end when the information ends: the Fed item ends on the consequence for borrowers, and the Guardian bullets end at the quotation.

## Up First newsletter, Sep. 16, 2026 (NPR)

Source: https://www.npr.org/2026/09/16/g-s1-143569/up-first-newsletter-fed-reserve-interest-rates-iran-war-kennedy-center-contraception

> "The Federal Reserve is expected to raise interest rates today for the first time in more than three years. Investors anticipate that the central bank will raise its benchmark interest rate by a quarter percentage point to a range of 3.75% to 4%. The move would result in higher borrowing costs for consumers looking to buy cars, expand businesses or carry credit card balances."

Checked: https://www.npr.org/2026/09/16/g-s1-143569/up-first-newsletter-fed-reserve-interest-rates-iran-war-kennedy-center-contraception (raw HTML fetched with curl and compared word for word), retrieved 2026-10-04
The first sentence is the event with its date and what is new about it. The second gives the number and who expects it. The third says whom it costs, with three concrete loans. The paragraph has no sentence about why interest rates matter.

> "There are 21.4 million women in the U.S. who need subsidized birth control living in counties with limited access, according to a new analysis shared exclusively with NPR from Power to Decide and the Guttmacher Institute."

> "Because the methodology behind these maps is new, there's no way to compare them with past models to see whether access has improved or worsened over the years. But Megan Kavanaugh, principal data researcher at Guttmacher, says there's reason to believe the current picture is already worse than what these maps show, as the data that informed them came from 2020-2023."

Checked: https://www.npr.org/2026/09/16/g-s1-143569/up-first-newsletter-fed-reserve-interest-rates-iran-war-kennedy-center-contraception (raw HTML, both passages are continuous text in the same item; the sentences between them are omitted), retrieved 2026-10-04
The figure comes first, followed by the claimants and the fact that NPR got the analysis exclusively. Two sentences later the limit of the evidence is stated as a checkable fact: new methodology, so no comparison with earlier maps. The named researcher is quoted for a judgment and the data years she cites are given.

> "The Iran war has caused extensive damage to U.S. bases and created shortages in American weapons, according to reports from the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) and the Pentagon Inspector General. Both of these reports come from independent, nonpartisan agencies."

> "Both reports estimate the war has cost more than $30 billion, but these estimates don't include the cost of rebuilding damaged U.S. bases."

Checked: https://www.npr.org/2026/09/16/g-s1-143569/up-first-newsletter-fed-reserve-interest-rates-iran-war-kennedy-center-contraception (raw HTML; the sentences between the two passages are omitted), retrieved 2026-10-04
The claim is attributed in the first sentence to two named documents, and the second sentence says what kind of bodies they are. The last sentence gives the figure and the single thing it leaves out. The writer is visible in what was chosen to be said about the sources and in the plain statement of the exclusion.

## Friday briefing, Oct. 2, 2026 (The Guardian, First Edition)

Source: https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/02/friday-briefing-how-we-covered-the-flydubai-incident-as-it-unfolded

> UK politics | "Ministers are working up sweeping plans for welfare changes including scrapping a key disability benefit for under-25s and replacing it with a multibillion-pound package of support to get into work."
>
> Northern Ireland | "Talks aimed at resolving the Drumcree parade dispute have broken up without agreement, with Northern Ireland secretary Chris Bryant describing the situation as being between a “rock and a hard place”."
>
> Europe news | "A fast-growing protest by high-school students over long hours, teacher shortages and decaying facilities has escalated into “urban violence”, the French government has warned."

Checked: https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/02/friday-briefing-how-we-covered-the-flydubai-incident-as-it-unfolded (raw HTML, "Five big stories" list; the label before each pipe is the Guardian's own, the sentence after it is quoted), retrieved 2026-10-04
Each item is one sentence with a subject, an action and a consequence, and each stands without the others. The quoted phrase carries the attribution inside the clause ("the French government has warned"), so the loaded word "urban violence" is the government's and the paper is only reporting it. The source of each judgment is a named person or body.

> "Yesterday, as internationally coordinated investigations continued, the attacker’s motives remained unknown. Israeli prime minister Benjamin Netanyahu told Fox News it was “too early to say” if there was any Iranian involvement, while claiming the co-pilot had previously undergone radical Islamist indoctrination and was suicidal."

> "Other senior Israeli figures have been more vehement, with ambassador to the UN, Danny Danon, describing the incident as a “terror attack”. Two Israeli security sources later told Reuters that they had not yet found Iranian involvement."

Checked: https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/02/friday-briefing-how-we-covered-the-flydubai-incident-as-it-unfolded (raw HTML, "In depth" section; the two passages are consecutive paragraphs), retrieved 2026-10-04
The status of the evidence comes first ("motives remained unknown"). Then every claim has a named speaker and a named outlet, and the later reporting that undercuts the earlier claim is placed after it with its own sourcing. The writer separates what was said from what was found without using a word like "unconfirmed".

## Simon Willison, "Kimi K3, and what we can still learn from the pelican benchmark"

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/16/kimi-k3/

> "Chinese AI lab Moonshot AI announced Kimi K3 this morning, describing it as their “most capable model to date, with 2.8 trillion parameters”. It’s currently available via their website and API, but an open weight release is promised “by July 27, 2026”."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/16/kimi-k3/ (raw HTML, opening paragraph), retrieved 2026-10-04
The first sentence gives the actor, what it released, when, and the claim in the company's own words inside quotation marks. The second gives availability and puts the one promise that has not happened yet in quotation marks, so the reader sees what is promised and what exists. A reader who knows nothing about Moonshot can follow it. This piece is a longer blog post, so only the opening of the post is a model for a brief item.

> "Moonshot are calling this the first “open 3T-class model” (I guess they’re rounding 2.8 trillion up to 3 trillion), taking the crown from DeepSeek’s 1.6T v4 Pro. Their self-reported benchmarks have K3 mostly beating Claude Opus 4.8 max and GPT-5.5 high, while losing out to Claude Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/16/kimi-k3/ (raw HTML, second paragraph), retrieved 2026-10-04
"Are calling" and "self-reported" mark the claim as the vendor's twice in two sentences. The parenthetical shows the writer checking the arithmetic of the claim in a dozen words. The comparison gives the model names on both sides of the result, including the ones it loses to.

> "The new model is notable for the pricing: $3/million input tokens and $15/million output tokens, putting it at the same level as Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet series and making it the most expensive model released by a Chinese AI lab to date."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/16/kimi-k3/ (raw HTML, the paragraph on pricing), retrieved 2026-10-04
The prices are given as the figures a developer would pay, in the units the vendor uses. The comparison is to a product the reader already knows the cost of, which supplies the scale. The sentence says what is notable and why in one clause each.

## Merriam-Webster, "Word of the Day: bogus" (June 4, 2025)

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2025-06-04

> "Bogus is an informal word used to describe something that is not real or genuine, making it a synonym of such words as fake, false, and counterfeit."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2025-06-04 (raw HTML, "What it Means"), retrieved 2026-10-04
The definition is one sentence, includes its register ("informal") and gives three neighbours so the reader can place the word. The card lists part of speech and pronunciation (BOH-gus) above it in a fixed order.

> "In her 1840 novel A New Home—Who’ll Follow?, author Carolina Kirkland wrote about a scandal affecting the fictitious frontier town of Tinkerville, whose bank vaults were discovered to contain “a heavy charge of broken glass and tenpenny nails, covered above and below with half-dollars, principally ‘bogus.’ Alas! for Tinkerville, and alas, for poor Michigan!” Alas indeed."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2025-06-04 (raw HTML, "Did You Know?", first sentences), retrieved 2026-10-04
The history opens on a dated source and a scene with specific objects: vault, glass, nails, half-dollars. The two-word "Alas indeed" is the writer's own voice and takes no more space than the quotation's last line needed.

> "Bogus (an apparent U.S. coinage) was first used in the argot of wildcat banks (like the one in Tinkerville) as a noun referring to counterfeit money. It later branched out into adjective use meaning “counterfeit or forged.” Although the noun is now obsolete, the adjective is still used today with the same meaning,"

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2025-06-04 (raw HTML, "Did You Know?", the sentences after the Kirkland quotation; the quoted text stops at a comma in the source), retrieved 2026-10-04
The origin is stated as a fact with its hedge inside the parentheses ("an apparent U.S. coinage"). The sentence then traces the word's change from noun to adjective in order. The writer says which form died and which survived.

## Anu Garg, "idiocracy," A.Word.A.Day (Sep. 28, 2026)

Source: https://wordsmith.org/words/idiocracy.html

> "1. Government by idiots."

> "A blend of idiot and -ocracy (rule or government). Earliest documented use in this sense: 1967."

Checked: https://wordsmith.org/words/idiocracy.html (raw HTML, "MEANING" and "ETYMOLOGY"), retrieved 2026-10-04
The etymology line names the parts, glosses the suffix in parentheses, and gives the first documented year for this sense. The definition is five words. The cards of this series are shorter than any other in this guide.

> "In the 2006 film Idiocracy, two people are placed in suspended animation as part of a secret military experiment. Forgotten, they awaken in 2505 to find that society has grown spectacularly less intelligent."

> "Idiocracy: Where the lowest common denominator becomes the gold standard."

Checked: https://wordsmith.org/words/idiocracy.html (raw HTML, "NOTES"; the two passages are consecutive in the notes), retrieved 2026-10-04
The note gives the film's premise in two sentences with the year it is set and a short list of facts. The last line is a joke that depends on the nouns of the word's own subject, and it stands alone as the final line of the note. The tone of the card is dry and the facts stay literal.
