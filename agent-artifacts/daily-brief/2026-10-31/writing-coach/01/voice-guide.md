# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-10-31

## How this series should sound

The brief has two voices, and the passages below keep them apart.

**The news and technology items (five of the six).** Two or three sentences in the register of a wire desk that has already decided what matters. The lede carries the event with its actor and its verb, in the present perfect or simple past, as in the BBC and Guardian ledes below. The consequence arrives in the last sentence or clause, as a fact with a date or a number attached: "Patterson has 28 days to decide..." is the model, a thing that now happens next. A figure goes in with its unit and its owner ("137 other drugs", "a statement from the Department of Pharmaceuticals said"). When a claim belongs to one party, the attribution sits in the same sentence as the claim. The person is visible in what was selected and in how exactly it is worded. There is no aside, no verdict on the actors, and no sentence that says the event matters. The technology items can use the field's own vocabulary without gloss, since the reader builds ML systems for a living, and can give the number that makes a release or result unmistakable.

**The Word of the Day.** The one place the brief can show delight, and the delight comes from the facts. The Merriam-Webster entries tell the story in dated, named, physical steps: a man, a year, a place, what happened to him, what people did with his name. The Wordsmith entries show the compressed card shape: part of speech and pronunciation, a one-clause definition, then coiner, source languages with glosses, and the earliest documented use. Where the origin has a known inventor or a quotable moment, name the person and give the date and the reported words. Where a step is uncertain, say which step and who reports it. A short closing sentence on how the word is used now is available when the history has one; the entry does not need a moral.

Two directions come from the brief and not from the passages. Each item is read in seconds, so the first four words should identify the subject. The page carries about 300 to 700 words in all, so no item has room to restate the headline above it.

## BBC News, "Erin Patterson: Australian mushroom murderer loses appeal to overturn convictions" (Lana Lam)

Source: https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ck8dz8j5n993o

> "Australia's mushroom killer has failed to overturn her murder convictions on appeal, with the court also ruling that she never be released from prison."

Checked: https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ck8dz8j5n993o, retrieved 2026-10-31
One sentence holds the actor, the verb, the court's second ruling, and what that ruling means for the person. The subject is named by the thing she did, and the sentence ends on the larger consequence.

> "Erin Patterson, 52, was found guilty of murdering three relatives and attempting to kill another when she served them a toxic mushroom-laced meal in 2023. Her life sentence included a non-parole period of 33 years."

Checked: https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ck8dz8j5n993o, retrieved 2026-10-31
The background a stranger needs is two sentences: age, count, method, year, sentence. Every noun is a number or a specific thing. The writer has chosen which facts a reader arriving cold must hold to follow the ruling.

> "On Friday, the appeal judges dismissed all seven grounds.
>
> Patterson has 28 days to decide if she will seek to convince the High Court of Australia to hear another appeal over the case."

Checked: https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ck8dz8j5n993o, retrieved 2026-10-31
The piece ends on a dated next step and a named court. It states what is open and for how long, and offers no comment on it.

## The Guardian, "India slashes cost of cancer drugs after court rules mark-ups are 'extortion'" (Amrit Dhillon)

Source: https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2026/oct/23/india-cuts-cost-cancer-drugs

> "The Indian government has moved to reduce the price of cancer drugs after a retired bank clerk took her case to the supreme court."

Checked: https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2026/oct/23/india-cuts-cost-cancer-drugs, retrieved 2026-10-31
The cause comes after the government's act in the same sentence, and the plaintiff is identified by her former job. A reader gets who acted, what changed, and what set it off without a second sentence.

> "A 30% trade margin cap on the price of 42 drugs has been in place for some years but many others remained off this list. Now, prodded by the supreme court’s findings, the government has imposed the cap on 137 other drugs."

Checked: https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2026/oct/23/india-cuts-cost-cancer-drugs, retrieved 2026-10-31
The old state and the new state sit side by side with their counts (42, then 137), so the size of the change can be read off. The word "Now" marks the change of state and does no other work.

> "“The measure is expected to reduce medicine prices by up to 70% and help cancer patients save 2,500 crore rupees [£199m] annually, significantly reducing the amount they pay from their own pockets,” a statement from the Department of Pharmaceuticals said."

Checked: https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2026/oct/23/india-cuts-cost-cancer-drugs, retrieved 2026-10-31
A projection is put in the mouth of the body that made it, with the original currency and a conversion in brackets. The forecast stays attributed and the reader can see whose number it is.

## Merriam-Webster, "boycott" (Did you know?)

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/boycott

> "In the 1870s, Irish farmers faced an agricultural crisis that threatened to result in a repeat of the terrible famine and mass evictions of the 1840s. Anticipating financial ruin, they formed a Land League to campaign against the rent increases and evictions landlords were imposing as a result of the crisis."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/boycott, retrieved 2026-10-31
The entry starts in a decade and a situation, before the word appears at all. Each sentence adds one cause, and the reader knows why the people acted before learning what they did.

> "When retired British army captain Charles Boycott, acting as an agent for an absentee landlord, tried to evict tenant farmers for refusing to pay their rent, he was ostracized by the League and community. His laborers and servants quit, and the crops in his care began to rot."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/boycott, retrieved 2026-10-31
The man is given his rank, his job and his employer, then the plain physical consequences in short clauses. The crops rotting is the detail a reader keeps, and it is stated without comment.

> "Boycott’s fate was soon well known, and his name became a byword for that particular protest strategy, both as a verb and as a noun."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/boycott, retrieved 2026-10-31
The step from a person's name to a word is one sentence with the two grammatical uses named. The conclusion is the word's own career, and it needs no sentence after it.

## Anu Garg, A.Word.A.Day: "consilience" and "psychobabble"

Source: https://wordsmith.org/words/consilience.html and https://wordsmith.org/words/psychobabble.html

> "consilience PRONUNCIATION: (kuhn-SIL-ee-yuhns) MEANING: noun : The linking or agreement of different disciplines when forming a theory or coming to a conclusion. ETYMOLOGY: Coined by the philosopher William Whewell (1794-1866). From Latin con- (with) + salire (to leap). Earliest documented use: 1840. He also coined the words scientist and physicist."

Checked: https://wordsmith.org/words/consilience.html, retrieved 2026-10-31
The card gives pronunciation, part of speech, a one-sentence definition, coiner with dates, the parts of the word with glosses, and the earliest documented use. The closing sentence is a second fact about the same coiner. It is the most compressed form of the documented story.

> "Coined by journalist Richard Dean Rosen (b. 1949). From Greek psycho- (mind) + babble (drivel, blather). Earliest documented use: 1975."

Checked: https://wordsmith.org/words/psychobabble.html, retrieved 2026-10-31
The coiner is placed by occupation and birth year, and each source element is glossed in brackets by its own plain English word. A date attests the word, and the entry makes no claim past it.

> "“Psychobabble is ... a set of repetitive verbal formalities that kills off the very spontaneity, candor, and understanding it pretends to promote."

Checked: https://wordsmith.org/words/psychobabble.html, retrieved 2026-10-31
The coiner's own definition is quoted with an ellipsis where the source continues, and it is attributed to the book that contains it. Letting the inventor speak is how a coinage with a known author earns its place in the card.
