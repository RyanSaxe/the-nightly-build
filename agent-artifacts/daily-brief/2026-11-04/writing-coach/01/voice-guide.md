# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-04

## How this piece should sound

Write it as a wire desk writes on the morning after an election. Each item gives the fact, the source of the fact, and the one consequence, and then it stops. The reader builds production ML systems and reads quickly. Do not explain what a race call is or why the House matters. Do give them the numbers that let them do the arithmetic themselves: seats called, the 218 threshold, seats outstanding, and the time and desk behind the count.

On the lead, the passages from Hilary Fung at NPR and from the Democracy Now! headline desk show how to state an incomplete count plainly. They name who calls the races ("NPR follows the AP for race calls"). They give what is decided and what is still out with the same flatness. They write the conditional as a conditional ("If all that holds"), and that keeps it from reading as a projection. Where the count supports no call, the item can say so in those words and give the figures. It does not need to describe suspense. Each sentence in those passages carries a place, a candidate or a number, which is why they hold up. The other news and technology items can follow the same discipline.

The word card can be lighter. Michael Quinion is allowed a pun because his facts are exact: the date, the publication, the coiner's job, the coiner's own words. Where the word's documented story offers a coiner and a date, those are what make the card enjoyable. A joke is fine where it depends on those facts. With three or four sentences there is room for the story and perhaps one aside, and no more.

## Hilary Fung, NPR, "Election results: Here are the tight House races that haven't yet been called"

Source: https://www.npr.org/2024/11/07/g-s1-33301/2024-house-seats-undecided-control

> "With a call of a House race in Arizona from the Associated Press Wednesday night, Republicans have now won enough seats to control the lower chamber in the next Congress."

Checked: https://www.npr.org/2024/11/07/g-s1-33301/2024-house-seats-undecided-control (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-04
One sentence gives the triggering race, the desk that called it, the time, and the result. Nothing is claimed beyond what the call shows. The writer's judgment shows in what is left out: no adjective on the win.

> "Democrats needed a net gain of four seats to win the majority. So far, they have flipped five seats and are leading in one more GOP-held seat. But Republicans also flipped four seats of their own and are leading in one other. If all that holds, Democrats would have seen a net gain of one seat in this election, and Republicans would maintain an even-narrower majority in the next Congress."

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-04
Called seats ("flipped") and uncalled ones ("leading in") are kept apart by the verb. The arithmetic is done in front of the reader, and the outcome is labeled as conditional by "If all that holds".

## Democracy Now!, "Control of U.S. Senate Hangs on Three Undetermined Races"

Source: https://www.democracynow.org/2022/11/10/headlines/control_of_us_senate_hangs_on_three_undetermined_races

> "In Arizona, incumbent Democratic Senator Mark Kelly has a significant lead over Republican Blake Masters, with nearly a third of ballots yet to be counted."

Checked: https://www.democracynow.org/2022/11/10/headlines/control_of_us_senate_hangs_on_three_undetermined_races (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-04
Who leads, and how much of the count is still out, in a single sentence. The state opens the sentence, so a reader scanning several races finds each one fast.

> "In Nevada, Trump-supporting Republican Adam Laxalt is leading incumbent Democratic Senator Catherine Cortez Masto by fewer than 20,000 votes. Most of the 20% of the ballots yet to be counted are mail-in votes from urban areas likely to favor Cortez Masto."

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-04
The second sentence says why a lead may not last, and it does so with the kind of ballot and where the ballots come from, not with a prediction. (Elsewhere this desk adds labels such as "climate denier" that a neutral brief would not use. These two sentences do not.)

## Michael Quinion, World Wide Words, "Gobbledygook"

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-gob1.htm

> "This is a truly maverick word, not only because it is surprisingly modern and also one whose genesis we can pin down to the day, but also because a maverick coined it —Maury Maverick, a Texan lawyer who was at various times a Democratic Congressman and mayor of San Antonio."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-gob1.htm (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-04
The pun opens the entry, and it works because it is literally true: the coiner's surname is Maverick. Quinion is enjoying himself, and he still gets the man's profession and offices in before the sentence ends.

> "He used the word in the New York Times Magazine on 21 May 1944, while he was chairman of the US Smaller War Plants Committee in Congress, as part of a complaint against the obscure language used by his colleagues. His inspiration, he said, was the turkey, “always gobbledy gobbling and strutting with ludicrous pomposity”."

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-04
Publication, date, the post he held, and his motive are packed into one sentence. Then the coiner's own words do the comic work, attributed with "he said", so Quinion does not have to make the joke himself.
