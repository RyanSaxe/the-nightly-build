# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-14

## How this piece should sound

The brief is a three-minute read for people who build and run software and know their way around a news cycle. Each of the five news and technology items has two or three sentences, and the word card has a few more. At that length the sentences carry everything, so each one holds a fact, a figure or a named source.

The wire exemplars below open on what happened, with the verb and the number in the first sentence. Marcia Dunn's Swift report gives the date, the 22 years and the failed rescue inside two sentences, and Charlotte Graham-McLay's Nauru report puts the president's claim in the first sentence with his own words quoted. A claim goes to whoever made it. Graham-McLay writes "he said", quotes the president's phrase, and then says what he did not explain. Where one side's account is the only account, her last passage shows the report saying so. The Iran item, the Gemini 4 Hydrogen figures and anything else a party asserts can be handled the same way when the material has that shape.

Dunn states what is not known in the same flat register as what is known ("It was not immediately known how much of the 1 ½-ton spacecraft burned up"). Where the reporting stops, say it stops, in one plain sentence. Figures appear as figures with their units. Simon Willison's aws-emulator entry gives "29,684 commits in the first three weeks" and names the exact platforms where the tool does not yet run, which is what lets a technical reader judge the item in a line. The technology items can use the names practitioners use for the thing: the language, the runtime, the file format. The brief speaks as the paper and has no "I", so what carries over from Willison is the specific limitation and the named person, not the first-person hope.

The word card is where the pleasure is allowed. The Merriam-Webster entries tell an origin as a short chain of forms, languages and dates, and they let the reader hold a wrong guess for a sentence before correcting it ("could have something to do with a rooster"). A coinage with a documented inventor can be told the same way: who made the word, when it first appears, what the coiner's own sentence says. The mild joke in "the not-so-golden days" shows how much humor fits: one adjective, inside a sentence that is still reporting. Quote a word's source text exactly where the card quotes it.

Readers here have degrees in the field and no need of a term explained twice. Define a word the paper is introducing and leave common ones alone. Keep each item standing on its own, since a reader arrives at any of them from a link.

## Marcia Dunn, "NASA space telescope plunges to Earth after two decades studying cosmic explosions" (Associated Press)

Source: https://abcnews.com/Technology/wireStory/nasa-space-telescope-plunges-earth-after-decades-studying-137857196

> "One of NASA’s premier space telescopes plunged to Earth on Wednesday, ending its 22-year investigation of the universe’s biggest explosions. The Swift Observatory’s demise came less than three months after a long-shot rescue mission failed."

Checked: https://abcnews.com/Technology/wireStory/nasa-space-telescope-plunges-earth-after-decades-studying-137857196 (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-14
The first sentence carries the event, the day and the duration, and the second places it against the earlier failed rescue with a time gap. "Plunged" and "demise" are ordinary verbs of the story, used once each. The writer is visible in the choice of "long-shot", a judgment word that the next paragraphs back with the Katalyst Space details.

> "Swift rocketed into orbit in 2004 on what was expected to be a two-year study of gamma ray bursts and exploding stars. It observed thousands of them over a mission that lasted 11 times longer than planned, serving as a first responder for other telescopes that could probe deeper."

Checked: https://abcnews.com/Technology/wireStory/nasa-space-telescope-plunges-earth-after-decades-studying-137857196 (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-14
The planned two years and the actual 11-times-longer run sit in adjacent sentences, so the reader gets the scale from a comparison and no adjective. "First responder" is the one figure of speech, and it explains Swift's job to a reader who has not met it.

> "It was not immediately known how much of the 1 ½-ton spacecraft burned up in the atmosphere. NASA anticipated some of the denser pieces surviving."

Checked: https://abcnews.com/Technology/wireStory/nasa-space-telescope-plunges-earth-after-decades-studying-137857196 (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-14
The unknown is stated in a short sentence with the spacecraft's mass attached, and the next sentence says who expected what. There is no hedge word anywhere in the two sentences.

## Charlotte Graham-McLay, "Nauru's president has no political opponents after all lawmakers joined the government side" (Associated Press)

Source: https://abcnews.com/International/wireStory/naurus-president-political-opponents-after-lawmakers-joined-government-138102578

> "Nauru’s president, David Adeang, has said all opposition lawmakers in parliament have joined his government, a move he said reflected “unity, stability and shared purpose” in the Pacific Island country’s politics."

Checked: https://abcnews.com/International/wireStory/naurus-president-political-opponents-after-lawmakers-joined-government-138102578 (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-14
The claim is in the first sentence and belongs to a named person, and the characterization of the move is in his words inside quotation marks. The reporter's own assessment is held back for the paragraphs that follow.

> "In a Facebook post Tuesday, Adeang didn’t explain what had prompted the change beyond a “shared commitment” to put Nauru's interests first. He also didn’t suggest who would supply the scrutiny usually provided by opponents in a country with a single house of parliament."

Checked: https://abcnews.com/International/wireStory/naurus-president-political-opponents-after-lawmakers-joined-government-138102578 (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-14
She reports what the source left out, and says where the statement was made and on what day. The second sentence names the missing function, scrutiny, in a plain noun, so a reader sees what is absent.

> "Government Facebook posts announcing the change were the sole source of information about it; all news outlets in the country of 12,000 people are government-owned."

Checked: https://abcnews.com/International/wireStory/naurus-president-political-opponents-after-lawmakers-joined-government-138102578 (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-14
The limit of the reporting is stated as a fact about the sources, with the country's population beside it. The sentence says why the account is one-sided without commenting on it.

## Simon Willison, "aws-emulator" (link blog)

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/11/aws-emulator/

> "Finally, an official emulator from AWS to run tests against a local clone of their many different services without having to provision a paid API key to do so. It's written in Rust by a huge swarm of agents - 29,684 commits in the first three weeks of the project."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/11/aws-emulator/ (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-14
The opening says what the tool does in the terms a developer would use: run tests against a local clone, with no paid API key. The commit count and the time span are exact. "Finally" is the only sign of the writer's view and it is one word.

> "It currently requires Docker to run on macOS or Windows. I'm hoping they fix that in the future - it would be great if you could download a single binary and use that as part of a standard test suite in other languages and on other platforms."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/11/aws-emulator/ (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-14
The one limitation is named with its platforms, and the wished-for fix is described as a concrete artifact, a single binary used in test suites in other languages. The first person is visible here and nowhere else in the entry.

## Merriam-Webster editors, "Word of the Day: gauntlet" (12 November 2026)

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-11-12

> "Gauntlet originally referred to a double file of men facing each other and armed with clubs or other weapons with which to strike at an individual made to run between them. It later gained an expanded sense referring to a line, series, or assemblage of people or things that poses some sort of ordeal, as in “faced a gauntlet of opponents to get to the championship.”"

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-11-12 (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-14
The original sense is described physically, in one sentence, and the extended sense follows with a quoted example of use. The entry labels the second sense as "later", so the order of senses is also the order in time.

> "Gantelope was a bit funny-sounding to the English ear, though, and through a process called folk etymology gantelope morphed into the already-familiar gauntlet."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-11-12 (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-14
The mechanism of the change is named with its technical term, folk etymology, and the reason for it is given in an ordinary phrase, "a bit funny-sounding to the English ear". The lexicographer's amusement is in "though" and "already-familiar".

> "In the not-so-golden days, a gauntlet was a military punishment in which a prisoner was made to pass between two rows of men armed with (and using) clubs or other weapons."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-11-12 (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-14
The joke is the phrase "not-so-golden days" and the parenthetical "(and using)", each a few words inside a sentence that is still defining the punishment. The parenthetical corrects the reader's assumption that the clubs were only held.

## Merriam-Webster editors, "Word of the Day: cockamamie" (8 November 2026)

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-11-08

> "By the look and sound of it, cockamamie (also spelled cockamamy) could have something to do with a rooster and the outrageous sound it makes. But in fact, cockamamie is believed to be an altered form of the term decalcomania, which refers to the process, invented in the mid-19th century, of transferring pictures and designs from specially prepared paper to surfaces such as glass or porcelain."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-11-08 (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-14
The entry states the guess a reader would make, then gives the documented origin with its date and its definition of the process. "Is believed to be" marks where the etymology is a judgment and not a record.

> "Those familiar with today’s temporary tattoos will understand quickly that these were regarded by many as silly novelties, lending the word cockamamie the necessary leeway for application to anything ridiculous."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-11-08 (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-14
The closing sentence ties the old object to a modern one the reader has seen, and gives the reason the word's meaning widened. The pleasure is in "necessary leeway", a dry phrase that makes the semantic step sound like a legal one.
