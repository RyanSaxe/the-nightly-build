# Writing-coach brief

## Role record

- Article: Daily Brief, `2026-09-24`.
- Stage: writing-coach.
- Resolved production directive: capable model tier, low effort.
- Scope: give the writer usable editorial direction; do not draft article copy or edit the HTML.

## Audience

- Write for a widely read audience with strong mathematics and computer-science literacy, but no assumed knowledge of these six stories.
- Assume a scanning reader who wants the concrete development, the number or mechanism that makes it specific, and the limit on what the reporting establishes.
- Define only the legal, climate, aviation, and chip terms needed for the item at hand; do not turn any item into a background explainer.
- Make every item stand alone. Do not require the reader to follow another item or a longer article.
- Keep the register plain, exact, and compact. Use the source's technical nouns where precision depends on them.

## Angle

- Treat the brief as six separate judgments about what moved on September 24, not as one invented theme.
- Make each item answer, in order, what happened, what concrete detail establishes it, and what the sources do not establish.
- Let the primary source own the announcement, record, definition, or claim; use the secondary source for independent context, opposition, or competition.
- Use figures and mechanisms as the reason to read each item, not as decoration.
- Keep the word card independent of the news. A possible diplomatic echo may guide selection, but it must not become a claim about the day's events.
- Avoid overlap with the commissioned Feature and Technical articles. Keep the FAA item operational and the Huawei item reportorial; do not turn either into an AI-governance argument or a technical walkthrough.

## Item-by-item purpose and boundaries

### 1. Word of the Day: `concordat`

- Purpose: give the noun's meaning, part of speech, pronunciation, and supported French/Latin origin in a concise word card.
- Boundaries: use only the Merriam-Webster and Oxford Learner's Dictionaries pages supplied in the commission; omit a church-history lesson, invented usage history, and any claim that the word describes the day's diplomacy.
- Keep the definition and etymology distinct from the day's reporting. Do not use the card to preview or interpret another item.

### 2. News 1: California's Paramount–Warner Bros. settlement

- Purpose: report the California-led settlement, its pending court approval, the five-year production commitments, and the worker fund; explain why the settlement is not approval of the merger.
- Boundaries: separate Attorney General Bonta's position from the merger's legal status; preserve the concrete figures; do not call the merger approved, closed, or cleared beyond what the sources say; do not speculate about the deal's outcome.
- Keep the item about the settlement's legal and production terms, not a general history of either company or a prediction about consolidation.

### 3. News 2: Big Bend 1 border-wall construction

- Purpose: state that construction began on the 47-mile Big Bend 1 project, give the first-panel date, and place it within the five-project, roughly 500-mile plan; include the opposition with attribution.
- Boundaries: distinguish Big Bend 1 from the temporarily paused project inside Big Bend National Park; do not merge the projects, generalize from one project to the whole border policy, or state opposition without naming its source.
- Keep the item to the project, schedule, plan, and documented objections. Do not add unreported operational or environmental consequences.

### 4. News 3: NOAA's August and summer temperature records

- Purpose: report the contiguous United States' 132-year record, the 75.6°F August average, and the 88.6°F daytime maximum; include the September fire-potential implication only if the source supports it and only at brief length.
- Boundaries: identify the geographic scope and record period exactly; do not convert the record into an unsupported forecast, regionalize it beyond the source, or imply a causal explanation the sources do not provide.
- Keep the record, its measured figures, and its sourced near-term implication separate.

### 5. Technology 1: FAA deployment of SMART

- Purpose: explain SMART's use of 200 data streams and predictive analysis, its limited-mode start around Washington, D.C., and its role as a staff-reviewed scheduling aid.
- Boundaries: state clearly that SMART does not control aircraft; distinguish scheduling support from autonomous air-traffic control; do not repeat the Feature article's AI-governance argument; do not infer delay reductions or operational success beyond the sources.
- Attribute agency descriptions and independent reporting precisely. Keep the mechanism understandable without adding implementation details not in the evidence.

### 6. Technology 2: Huawei Atlas 960E SuperPoD

- Purpose: report Huawei's claimed 4,096-NPU scale and FP8/FP4 figures, then give the AP context about the product's place in China's chip competition.
- Boundaries: label performance and scale figures as Huawei claims; do not present them as independently benchmarked results; keep Huawei's announcement separate from AP's competitive context; do not equate the figures with a competing product without evidence.
- Explain only the mechanism or capability needed to understand the announcement. Do not expand the item into a hardware deep dive or a general semiconductor forecast.

## Ordering and item shape

- Keep the exact template order: Word of the Day, three News items in commission order, then two Technology items in commission order.
- Do not rearrange the six items to manufacture a thematic arc or to lead with the most dramatic story.
- Within each item, lead with the development; follow with the decisive figure, date, mechanism, or term; close with the immediate context or explicit verification boundary when the evidence requires one.
- Keep each item short enough for a daily brief while retaining the detail that distinguishes it from a headline. Avoid cross-references between items.
- Use the supplied word-card furniture for the first item and the brief-item structure for the remaining five. Keep labels concrete and consistent.
- End the sequence on the Huawei item's sourced verification boundary, not on a generic prediction about technology or geopolitics.

## Headline and dek guidance

- Make the headline concrete and date-specific, and make the day's movement visible in the first words.
- Use a present-tense subject and verb where the headline reports an event; choose the one development or pattern that the six items actually support.
- Avoid generic freshness labels, a colon-plus-subtitle construction, rhetorical questions, paired-adjective triads, and a headline that promises more synthesis than the brief provides.
- Make the dek one lean sentence describing what moved across the brief and the distinguishing mix of records, settlements, projects, and technology announcements.
- Do not restate the headline in the dek. Add the who, scope, or concrete detail the headline leaves out.
- Keep the dek factual and defensible from the six items and their sources; avoid a grand conclusion, forecast, or claim about a broader trend.
- For item headlines, state the development rather than a topic label. Keep company and agency names where they carry attribution or precision.

## Evidence discipline

- Use exactly the twelve supplied source slots: one primary and one secondary for each of the six items. Do not add a thirteenth source or substitute an unlisted source without an orchestrator decision.
- Open and read every source before drafting. Cite the document or page that owns each claim, with the primary source ahead of coverage wherever it made the announcement or record.
- Preserve this source pairing:
  - Word: Merriam-Webster definition page; Oxford Learner's Dictionaries definition page.
  - Settlement: California Attorney General release; Reuters litigation report.
  - Border wall: CBP SMART Wall map; Associated Press report.
  - Climate: NOAA/NCEI national climate report; Al Jazeera report.
  - FAA: FAA SMART announcement; Reuters report.
  - Huawei: Huawei Atlas 960E announcement; Associated Press report.
- Record exact locators or relevant page sections for figures, definitions, dates, and legal status. Do not cite a homepage when the source has a specific passage.
- Attribute claims by owner: dictionary, California Attorney General, CBP, NOAA/NCEI, FAA, Huawei, Reuters, AP, or Al Jazeera. Avoid anonymous or collective attribution.
- Mark Huawei's performance numbers as company claims and keep AP's account as context, not validation. Apply the same separation to agency descriptions and independent reporting.
- Check every number, date, place, scope, and status word against the opened source. Preserve units and precision; use “roughly” only where the source does.
- Cut any claim that cannot be supported by one of the twelve sources. Do not use general knowledge to fill a missing transition or implication.
- Keep citations attached to the item they support and number sources in first-citation order. Every item must be cited.

## Slop watchlist

- Cut generic ledes, generic closers, throat-clearing, and filler transitions.
- Cut inflated adjectives and claims of significance that the figures do not demonstrate.
- Replace vague attribution with the named agency, company, publication, or official.
- Remove unsupported forecasts, causal leaps, and broad conclusions drawn from one record or announcement.
- Avoid machine-shaped headline and dek patterns: “latest” framing, colon subtitles, rhetorical questions, paired triads, and negative-parallelism constructions.
- Do not make abstractions act, especially “the data,” “the technology,” “the moment,” or “the trend,” when the source or actor can be named.
- Keep metaphors, wordplay, and diplomatic or geopolitical framing out unless the supplied evidence makes them necessary for understanding.
- Test the opening and final line of every item for a claim that says nothing beyond the nouns already present; delete it rather than decorate it.
