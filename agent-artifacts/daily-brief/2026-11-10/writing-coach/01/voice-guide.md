# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-10 (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a three-minute read for people who build and study machine learning systems and have degrees in computer science and mathematics. Each item is two or three sentences, so each sentence carries a fact. The reader is treated as someone who would rather have the number, the name of the document or the exact term than a gloss on it. Scott Horsley's passages below show the news register: he states what happened in plain verbs, gives the figure with its source attached, and says why the figure matters in the same breath. Simon Willison shows the same for a technology item: he names the project and the person, says what changed, and gives his own view in one short sentence that is plainly his. Where an item turns on a technical term, use the term the practitioners use, the way Willison writes "XMLHttpRequest" and "pre-filter" without explaining them to this audience.

Attribution can be done in the sentence that carries the claim. Horsley writes "according to the Fed's preferred measure" in the middle of a figure, and Willison writes "Carson Gross is technically keeping that promise" with the person as the subject. A claim that is someone's own goes with that someone, and a claim the reporting established is stated flat. Where a fact has a consequence for the reader, Horsley's "slightly cheaper to borrow money to buy a car, expand a business or carry a balance on a credit card" shows a consequence carried down to specific cases. For this audience the equivalent is usually a specific system, a specific cost or a specific number, whichever the material has.

The word card is the one place with room for personality. Merriam-Webster's two entries show it in small: the writer notices what the word looks like it should mean (a rooster, a forehead), says what it does mean, and gives the documented route in order. The effrontery entry's "(it is)" is a short aside that admits the question was odd. A card can sound like a person who finds the word interesting, provided every step of the origin is documented and the story has an actual path of changes in it. Give the part of speech, pronunciation and definition plainly and let the story take the remaining sentences.

Across all six items, a few things the material may call for: a verb that says what the actor did, with the actor as subject; a figure at the point the sentence needs it, in the units the source uses; one flat verdict from the writer where the evidence supports one, as Willison writes "htmx is a very responsibly run project"; a plain statement of what nobody has established when that is the case. Keep the register level from the first item to the last, so the Technology items read like the News items and not like a different publication.

## Scott Horsley, "A divided Federal Reserve cuts interest rates for a 3rd straight time" (NPR)

Source: https://www.npr.org/2025/12/10/nx-s1-5633393/fed-expected-to-cut-rates-for-a-3rd-straight-time

> "The rate cut should make it slightly cheaper to borrow money to buy a car, expand a business or carry a balance on a credit card."

Checked: https://text.npr.org/nx-s1-5633393, retrieved 2026-11-10
The sentence takes an abstract policy move to three things a reader has done, and the "slightly" is a calibrated size for the effect. Horsley is visible as a reporter who knows what a listener does with an interest rate.

> "Inflation is still well above the Fed's target, which would ordinarily call for keeping interest rates elevated. But unemployment has also been creeping up, which typically points toward lower rates. Fed policymakers were divided on which of those problems is more urgent."

Checked: https://text.npr.org/nx-s1-5633393, retrieved 2026-11-10
Two competing facts are each given a consequence in a short clause, and the third sentence says where the disagreement sits. The sentences are short and each has one verb of its own. "Creeping up" is the one figurative phrase, and it describes how a number moved.

> "Policymakers had to rely on somewhat stale economic data from September, when annual inflation was clocked at 2.8%, according to the Fed's preferred measure, while unemployment stood at 4.4%. Both figures were slightly higher than in the previous month."

Checked: https://text.npr.org/nx-s1-5633393, retrieved 2026-11-10
The figures arrive with the month, the measure and the comparison in the sentence that gives them, and the next sentence reads them for direction. The person is visible in "somewhat stale", a small judgment about the data stated without fuss.

## Simon Willison, "The fetch()ening" (simonwillison.net link blog)

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/3/htmx-the-fetchening/

> "After several years of stable htmx 2.0 and a promise to never release a backwards-incompatible htmx 3 Carson Gross is technically keeping that promise... by skipping to htmx 4 instead!"

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/3/htmx-the-fetchening/, retrieved 2026-11-10
The opening sentence gives the history, the promise and the news, with the person as the subject. The dry joke is in "technically" and the ellipsis, and it is a joke about a version number, so it depends on the nouns.

> "The main reason is to replace XMLHttpRequest with fetch() - a change that will have enough knock-on compatibility effects to require a major version bump - so they're using that as an excuse to clean up various other accumulated design warts at the same time."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/3/htmx-the-fetchening/, retrieved 2026-11-10
One sentence gives the cause, the consequence in versioning terms and what the project did with the opportunity. The terms are the practitioners' own and are not explained. "Design warts" is a working programmer's phrase.

> "It's a really great piece of technical writing - I learned a bunch about htmx and picked up some good notes on API design in general from this."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/3/htmx-the-fetchening/, retrieved 2026-11-10
His verdict comes in first person and says what he got from the piece. It is a claim the writer can stand behind because it reports his own experience.

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/3/the-case-against-pgvector/, retrieved 2026-11-10
He points at the section that matters, says why in two words, and then lets the source speak. The person is visible in having read to the section and chosen it.

## Merriam-Webster, "Word of the Day: cockamamie" (November 8, 2026)

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-11-08

> "By the look and sound of it, cockamamie (also spelled cockamamy) could have something to do with a rooster and the outrageous sound it makes. But in fact, cockamamie is believed to be an altered form of the term decalcomania, which refers to the process, invented in the mid-19th century, of transferring pictures and designs from specially prepared paper to surfaces such as glass or porcelain."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-11-08, retrieved 2026-11-10
The entry starts with the guess a reader would make, then gives the documented origin in the next sentence, hedged once with "is believed to be". The date for the process is in the sentence that introduces it.

> "Starting in the 1930s, painted strips of paper with images capable of being transferred to the skin were called decals or—in children's slang—cockamamies. Those familiar with today's temporary tattoos will understand quickly that these were regarded by many as silly novelties, lending the word cockamamie the necessary leeway for application to anything ridiculous."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-11-08, retrieved 2026-11-10
The last step of the route is dated and tied to an object the reader knows. The closing clause says how the sense widened and stops there.

## Merriam-Webster, "Word of the Day: effrontery" (November 5, 2026)

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-11-05

> "A bald-faced liar (or barefaced liar, if you prefer) is one who has no shame about their dishonesty. Is it possible to be so shameless, so bald or bare of face as to be completely without a forehead? If this strikes you as an odd question (it is), consider the history of effrontery, a formal synonym of such informal words for shamelessness as nerve, cheek, and gall."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-11-05, retrieved 2026-11-10
The writer opens on a familiar phrase, asks a silly question and admits it is silly in a two-word parenthesis. The related words are named exactly, so the register of the target word is placed among its neighbours.

> "The word traces back to the Medieval Latin adjective effrons, formed by combining the Latin prefix ex-, meaning "out" or "without," with frons, the Latin word for "forehead.""

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/2026-11-05, retrieved 2026-11-10
The etymology is given with the language stage, the parts and their glosses, and nothing else. The forms are in the original languages with an English meaning beside each.
