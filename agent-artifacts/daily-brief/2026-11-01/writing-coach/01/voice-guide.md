# Voice guide: Daily Brief, 2026-11-01

The brief is a wire service for readers who saw the headline and want the number or the caveat it dropped. The whole brief runs 300 to 700 words across six items, so every sentence carries a fact. The reader is technical, but write each item so a non-specialist could follow it. Define a term once, in passing, and assume nothing else.

## News and technology items

Two or three sentences. The first states what happened, with the actor as the subject and a present-tense verb: "The FDA approves...", "Researchers at ETH Zurich report...". The second gives the figure, the range the source gives, or the detail that separates this event from similar ones. The third, if the item needs one, says what happens next and when. Cut it if the next step is only a guess.

Attribute anything unverified to whoever says it: "the company says", "NPR's analysis finds". Where the record can be opened, say what it says and name the document. Give the figure as the source gives it. If the source says 12 to 15 percent, write that. When a number means nothing alone, set it beside one the reader holds. Say plainly what nobody knows yet.

Each item ends on its last fact. Do not add a closing line that hands the point back to the reader. Each item stands alone, so never refer to another item or assume the reader has seen earlier coverage.

## Word of the Day

State the part of speech, the pronunciation, and a one-sentence definition. The definition is plain and exact, the kind a dictionary would print if the dictionary were good. Then three or four sentences of documented story: who coined or first printed the word, when, where, and in what sentence or context. Name the person, the year and the publication. If the origin is disputed or the first use is only the earliest one found, say so in one clause. The word may be fun to use. Let the facts supply that pleasure, and leave out words like "delightful" or "curious".

## Headline lines

Follow `spec/headlines.md`. Subject, verb and the surprise come first, in the present tense. Include a number only when the number is the surprise. No colon subtitle, and no question unless the item answers it. The headline names a specific thing, so "Judge blocks Texas ballot-drop rule" and not "Court ruling shakes election landscape".

## Avoid

- Openers that announce: "In a significant development", "It was announced today".
- Puffery: significant, landmark, pivotal, transformative, game-changing.
- "X, not Y" constructions and "not just X but Y". State what X is.
- Three-item lists chosen for rhythm. List what the source gives.
- Vague attribution: "experts say", "critics argue". Name who, or cut it.
- Verbs that credit the writer's guess to the source: hinted, signalled, suggested, implied.
- Trailing clauses that grade the news: "highlighting", "underscoring", "raising questions".
- Em-dashes, semicolons and parentheses. Use a period or a comma.
- The slop test applies to every sentence. If swapping its nouns leaves a sentence that still reads fine, delete it.
