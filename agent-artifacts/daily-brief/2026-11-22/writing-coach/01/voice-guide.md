# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-22 (01)

## How this piece should sound

The brief has two registers, and they should not borrow from each other. The
news and technology items are wire copy: two or three sentences, written by
someone who has read the primary record and is telling a colleague what
happened. The Word of the Day is a short story told by someone who enjoys the
word and has the dates to back the enjoyment. The reader in both cases is a
person with a computer science or mathematics degree who has three minutes. Treat
that reader as able to follow a precise sentence the first time. No item should
explain what a Senate vote or a context window is.

For the news and technology items, the AP lead in the Thune story puts the
actor, the verb, the body, and the stakes into one sentence, and the second
paragraph says what the first one left out, including what was expected and what
was not. The Willison passages show the same job in technology: the person's
name and credential come before the finding, the finding is stated at its actual
size ("three out of three" cases, "a major version bump"), and the next sentence
says what the result is good for. Name the person or body that did the thing, in
the sentence that reports it. Where a claim is one party's, the sentence says
whose. When the source reports a figure, give the figure and the weekday it
happened. Where the reporting does not say what follows, a short sentence saying
that is better than a guess at it.

A dry remark is available when the nouns of the story supply it. The AP
passage where Senator Johnson turns to a reporter and asks "Do you want it?" is
there because the reporter wrote down what happened in the room, not because the
reporter made a joke. If an item contains a detail like that, it can carry the
sentence. Do not add one for color.

For the Word of the Day, Forsyth and Zimmer both start from a small puzzle and
follow the evidence. Forsyth gives the word's plain first meaning and then lets
the later meaning arrive as a surprise from it. Zimmer reports who he wrote to,
what they remembered, and what they could not remember. His sentences carry names
and dates ("Howie Krakow", "1970 or 1971") and he lets a gap in the record stay a
gap. Tell this word's story in the order the facts make it most surprising,
using the actual names, years, and the coiner's own words where they exist. A
sentence from a dictionary entry can be quoted, but the telling around it should
be plain. The exemplars show a person on the page as someone who looked
something up and is willing to say so. They do not show a persona to copy. Take
neither Forsyth's puns nor Zimmer's first person into the brief.

The pronunciation, part of speech, and definition are fixed furniture. The three
or four sentences that follow them are where this guide applies, and the length
leaves room for one or two of the moves above and no more.

## Associated Press (Mary Clare Jalonick and Steven Sloan), "Senate Republicans vote to keep Thune as leader after election wipeout"

Source: https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/senate-republicans-vote-to-keep-thune-as-leader-after-election-wipeout

> "WASHINGTON (AP) — Senate Republicans reelected South Dakota Sen. John Thune as leader of the GOP conference after a tense day of discussions about steep losses in the midterm elections that cost them their majority."

Checked: https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/senate-republicans-vote-to-keep-thune-as-leader-after-election-wipeout (raw HTML, text read), retrieved 2026-11-22
The lead has one actor, one verb, and the office in the same sentence, and the reason it was newsworthy comes after "after" in the same clause. Nothing in it is an adjective about significance.

> "Thune was always expected to prevail, as he is popular with his GOP colleagues and was running unopposed in the secret ballot election. But Senate Republicans vented their frustrations in three separate meetings on Tuesday after an election wipeout that cost them at least seven Senate seats."

Checked: same page, retrieved 2026-11-22
The second paragraph gives the expected outcome and its reason, then turns on "But" to what was unexpected, with the weekday, the number of meetings, and a floor figure ("at least seven"). The writer commits to the number the record supports and no more.

> "Before the meeting, Johnson, the Wisconsin senator, had circulated a letter asking for the delay. He said the conference needs to do some "real soul-searching" but conceded that there's no one else on the ballot."

Checked: same page, retrieved 2026-11-22
The attribution names the person, states what he did before the vote, and uses a quoted phrase only where the phrase is the claim. "Conceded" is the one verb that carries a judgment, and the quotation that follows in the story supports it.

## Simon Willison, link posts on "Claude Code Can Debug Low-level Cryptography" and "The fetch()ening"

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/1/claude-code-cryptography/ and https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/3/htmx-the-fetchening/

> "Go cryptography author Filippo Valsorda reports on some very positive results applying Claude Code to the challenge of implementing novel cryptography algorithms. After Claude was able to resolve a "fairly complex low-level bug" in fresh code he tried it against two other examples and got positive results both time."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/1/claude-code-cryptography/ (raw HTML, text read), retrieved 2026-11-22
The person and the credential come first, then what he did, then the count of attempts. The one quoted phrase is the source's own description of the bug, which keeps the writer from characterizing it. This is Willison writing as a reader of someone else's primary post, which is the same position the brief item holds.

> "After several years of stable htmx 2.0 and a promise to never release a backwards-incompatible htmx 3 Carson Gross is technically keeping that promise... by skipping to htmx 4 instead!"

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/3/htmx-the-fetchening/ (raw HTML, text read), retrieved 2026-11-22
The sentence reports the version history and the joke at once, and the joke is the version numbers themselves. It uses the project's own vocabulary without explaining it, for a reader who knows what a major version means.

> "The main reason is to replace XMLHttpRequest with fetch() - a change that will have enough knock-on compatibility effects to require a major version bump - so they're using that as an excuse to clean up various other accumulated design warts at the same time."

Checked: same page, retrieved 2026-11-22
The sentence gives the cause, the consequence, and the maintainers' second reason in one run, naming the actual API. A technical reader can verify each part against the project's own post.

## Mark Forsyth, "Don't Feed the Troll Fishers"

Source: https://blog.inkyfool.com/2013/08/dont-feed-troll-fishers.html

> "The verb troller first appears in Medieval French. It meant going out hunting without having any specific animal in mind. Imagine, if you will, a medieval Frenchman wandering through a wood with a bow and arrow and shooting any animal he felt like."

Checked: https://blog.inkyfool.com/2013/08/dont-feed-troll-fishers.html (raw HTML, text read), retrieved 2026-11-22
The origin is a plain fact in two short sentences, and the third turns it into something the reader can picture. The reader of this brief needs the first two sentences. The "Imagine, if you will" opener is the one part to leave behind, since it is a lecture opener.

> "This is one of those classic cases where the word could have come from trolling, and could have come from trailing, and probably came from both. It's just so convenient that the words sound alike."

Checked: same page, retrieved 2026-11-22
He says outright that the evidence supports two sources and says which he thinks it was. The uncertainty is stated as a fact about the record.

> "But, of course, far fewer people have heard of troll fishing, or indeed of aimless Frenchmen, that have heard of the nasty Scandinavian creatures that hide under bridges. People soon forgot the original precise meaning of trailing a bait, and that is why, under the influence of the Scandy creature, we now use troll to mean anybody on the Internet who's nasty."

Checked: same page, retrieved 2026-11-22
The ending is the mechanism of the change, which is what a reader came for. The joke ("aimless Frenchmen") is built from the story's own nouns. The word's later meaning arrives as the consequence of the earlier ones.

## Ben Zimmer, "Three scenes in the life of 'meh'"

Source: https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=3806

> "The problem with meh, as with so many fleeting interjections, is that they are terribly underrepresented in the linguistic and lexicographical literature."

Checked: https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=3806 (raw HTML, text read), retrieved 2026-11-22
He names the evidence problem in one sentence, using the field's terms. That sets up why the next find matters.

> "One reason I'd hoped to find meh or a close variant in the pages of Mad is that I imagined it would be the missing link explaining why the writers on "The Simpsons" enjoyed using it so much. I suppose I expected to find a tidy explanation, like the story of how Dan Castellaneta, the voice of Homer Simpson, translated the "annoyed grunt" of the show's early scripts into D'oh! by creating a staccato version of the Do-o-o-o used by character actor James Finlayson in "Laurel & Hardy" comedies."

Checked: same page, retrieved 2026-11-22
He reports what he expected to find, what he found instead, and a comparison case with its full specifics (the actor, the show, the earlier comedy). The disappointment is stated plainly and drives the next sentence.

> ""I had originally heard the word from an advertising writer named Howie Krakow back in 1970 or 1971 who insisted it was the funniest word in the world," he told me. So let's thank Mr. Krakow for his unwitting role in the spread of the meh meme."

Checked: same page, retrieved 2026-11-22
The source's own words carry the key fact and the date range as given, "1970 or 1971", with no tightening. The writer's reaction is one short sentence after the quotation and adds a name and a credit.
