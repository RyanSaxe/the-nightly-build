# Voice guide: daily-brief (series and brief template)

## How this series should sound

The Daily Brief is six short items read in about three minutes by engineers who
build machine learning systems and read widely outside their field. The register
is a well-informed colleague handing over the morning's facts: plain, exact,
unhurried even at two or three sentences, with no warm-up and no sign-off. Each
item trusts that whoever reads it can hold a vote count, a version number or a
legal term once it has been named. Nobody is addressed, and nothing is sold.

The word card is the one place with room for a person to show. Quinion's farb
entry shows how: he has an opinion about the people who use the word (they are
"deadly serious"), and he gives the word's origin as evidence with a magazine
name, a month and a year, and the person each account credits. Where the record
holds two accounts, he gives both and says who holds each. A word's story in
this series may be told that way when the sources support it: named coiners,
dated first uses, and a disputed origin left disputed, with the evidence for
each side. The definition stays one sentence. The pleasure comes from the
particulars of the story, and it needs no exclamation to show it.

For the news items, Nuyen's Up First paragraphs show the order a short item can
carry. Her Supreme Court item gives the ruling, the vote, and the legal ground
in one plain clause about what the Posse Comitatus Act does, and then the
paragraph after it says how far the ruling reaches and how far it does not,
attributed to a named reporter. When the source states what follows, an item can
end there. When it does not, the item can end on the fact. Her GDP item opens on
the economy and not on the agency, and it explains in one sentence why the report
came late. The opening noun of an item is a choice to make for each item, and
the actor is one option among several.

For the technology items, Alden's LWN lead paragraph and Willison's release
notes show how this audience likes to be told about a change: what the thing
does, the cost with its measured figure and who measured it, and the limit of
the fix where the source names one. Alden calls the glibc tunables "a welcome
hardening measure" and in the same sentence says they "may not address the root
problem", and the closing paragraph says why. Willison states exactly what a
vulnerability exposed and what it did not. Where an item's claim is the
vendor's or a lab's own, attribute it the way Willison marks his own
uncertainty: tie the hedge to the specific claim and give the reason for it, so
the rest of the item can stand without qualification.

The brief is too short for background. Define a term in the clause where it
appears, the way Nuyen glosses Posse Comitatus and Alden glosses ASLR, and only
where somebody outside that beat would stop on it. A machine learning term needs
no gloss for this audience. A court procedure, a budget mechanism or a word's
grammatical history usually does.

## Michael Quinion, "Farb"

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-far1.htm

> "Re-enactors are deadly serious about getting the details of uniform and
> equipment correct. They are dismissive of the people they call farbs who come
> for the fun but who don’t make the effort to get things right, the sort of
> people who will mix up items of uniform or carry a mobile phone or wear
> sunglasses."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-far1.htm, retrieved 2026-11-19
The meaning arrives through the people who use the word, and the examples are
small, exact and slightly funny: a mobile phone, sunglasses. Quinion's own
amusement at the re-enactors shows in "deadly serious", and he does not
underline it.

> "The term dates from the 1960s; an explanation for its origin was given in
> July 1986 in a re-enactors’ magazine, the Camp Chase Gazette, in which an early
> group leader, George Gorman, was said to have formed it from the beginning of
> “Far be it from me to criticise inauthentic uniforms ...”."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-far1.htm, retrieved 2026-11-19
The origin story comes with its paper trail: the decade of use, the month and
year of the explanation, the publication and the person it credits. "Was said to
have formed" keeps the claim at the strength the source supports, so the
reader knows this is an account and not a settled fact.

> "But the memories of early re-enactors asserts the word instead derives from
> German farbe, colour, because inauthentic re-enactors were over-colourful
> compared with the dull blues, greys or browns of the real Civil War uniforms
> that were the principal concern of American re-enactors at the time the word
> was coined. Farb was created by Gerry Rolph, a German teacher who led one of
> the early bands. The adjective farby is also known and may indeed have been the
> original form."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-far1.htm, retrieved 2026-11-19
The competing account gets the same treatment as the first: a source, a named
coiner, and a reason the etymology would make sense ("dull blues, greys or
browns"). Quinion leaves the dispute open and adds one small observation of his
own about "farby", hedged with "may indeed". (The agreement slip in "memories
... asserts" is in the original.)

## Suzanne Nuyen, "Up First newsletter: Epstein; SCOTUS National Guard ruling; U.S. GDP" (NPR)

Source: https://www.npr.org/2025/12/24/g-s1-103629/up-first-newsletter-epstein-files-trump-supreme-court-national-guard-us-gdp

> "The Supreme Court has ruled that National Guard troops must stay out of
> Chicago — for now. The decision is one of several "emergency docket" cases in
> which the conservative majority court has ruled against Trump since he began
> his second term as president. The justices ruled 6-3, stating that the
> president failed to explain why the situation in Chicago warranted an
> exception to a law called the Posse Comitatus Act, which limits the military's
> ability to execute laws on U.S. soil."

Checked: https://www.npr.org/2025/12/24/g-s1-103629/up-first-newsletter-epstein-files-trump-supreme-court-national-guard-us-gdp, retrieved 2026-11-19
Three sentences hold the ruling, its place in a pattern, the vote and the legal
ground. The statute is explained in one relative clause, so somebody who has
never heard of it can follow without a separate paragraph of background.

> "Because the ruling came through an emergency decision, it does not set
> precedent, NPR's Kat Lonsdorf explains. The decision applies only to this
> specific case in Illinois, not to troop deployments elsewhere. But deployments
> in other cities are currently tied up in litigation in lower courts, and
> Lonsdorf says lower court judges tend to look to these emergency decisions for
> guidance."

Checked: https://www.npr.org/2025/12/24/g-s1-103629/up-first-newsletter-epstein-files-trump-supreme-court-national-guard-us-gdp, retrieved 2026-11-19
This is the "what follows" part, and it is bounded: what the ruling does not
do comes first, then what it may still influence. The judgment about lower
courts is attributed to a named reporter, not stated as the newsletter's own.

> "The U.S. economy grew faster than economists expected from July through
> September, according to a delayed report from the Commerce Department on the
> nation's gross domestic product (GDP). The agency usually releases this report
> in October, but it pushed it to this month due to the government shutdown."

Checked: https://www.npr.org/2025/12/24/g-s1-103629/up-first-newsletter-epstein-files-trump-supreme-court-national-guard-us-gdp, retrieved 2026-11-19
The item opens on what happened to the economy, with the agency as the source,
not the subject. The second sentence answers the one question a careful reader
would have about the timing, and then the paragraph stops.

## Daroc Alden, "Additional hardening for the glibc dynamic linker" (LWN.net)

Source: https://lwn.net/Articles/1105134/

> "If ASLR had been enabled, the bug would still be exploitable in theory, but
> much more difficult to exploit in practice. In response, the GNU C Library
> (glibc) is considering a new set of tunable settings to allow administrators to
> forbid programs that do not support ASLR. The new settings, while a welcome
> hardening measure, may not address the root problem."

Checked: https://lwn.net/Articles/1105134/, retrieved 2026-11-19
The end of the lead paragraph does in three sentences what a technology item
has to: the stakes stated precisely ("in theory" against "in practice"), who is
doing what about it, and a judgment that names its own limit. The writer's
view is in "welcome" and "may not", and both are earned later in the article.

> "Compiling to position-independent code does have an impact: such code is
> often slightly larger and slightly slower than position-dependent code. In
> 2020, Node.js contributor "woodfairy" measured the impact on Node.js in
> particular at a slowdown of 5-10%, depending on workload. That slowdown was
> judged acceptable on macOS, but not on Linux (except for Android, which forbids
> position-dependent code, forcing the choice)."

Checked: https://lwn.net/Articles/1105134/, retrieved 2026-11-19
The cost is stated generally, then as a range from a named measurement with its
year, then as the decision people made with that figure in hand. The range is
given as the source gave it, with "depending on workload" kept.

> "The more difficult problem will come from executables built outside the
> distribution. Unless widespread adoption of these tunables motivates projects
> like Node.js to abandon position-dependent code, users may need to disable the
> tunables for those programs that the tunables are intended to address."

Checked: https://lwn.net/Articles/1105134/, retrieved 2026-11-19
The close is a consequence worked out from the facts above it. The irony that
the tunables may be disabled for the very programs they target is stated
flatly, and the writer leaves it to be noticed.

## Simon Willison, "Datasette 1.0a43"

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/15/datasette/

> "There are a couple of security fixes, one of which I deemed deserving of a
> GitHub advisory: private stored queries could be exposed, granting access to
> the title and SQL of the query (but not allowing that query to be executed)."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/15/datasette/, retrieved 2026-11-19
A security fix is described by exactly what leaked and what did not, so the
reader can size the problem without an adjective. The person shows in "I deemed
deserving", which owns the severity call as his.

> "With that in place, navigations within Datasette - still staunchly a
> multi-page application - feel more like an SPA. This works on Safari and Chrome
> at the moment and I believe supports for Firefox is due to land in Firefox 162
> on December 8th 2026 (seeing as they are no longer feature-flagged in Firefox
> Nightly.)"

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/15/datasette/, retrieved 2026-11-19
Availability is stated per browser, and the one uncertain claim carries its
hedge ("I believe") and its evidence in the same sentence. "Still staunchly"
is a small, dry piece of self-description from the project's author. (The
"supports" slip is in the original.)
