# Voice guide: daily brief, October 1

## Tim Radford, “Take big, wonderful and startling ideas and make them comprehensible”

Source: https://www.theguardian.com/science/blog/2011/apr/13/secrets-good-science-writing

> “So a science writer has at some point to become a translator; to convert science into journalism without, if possible, using journalese; to take big, wonderful and startling ideas and make them comprehensible with help from metaphor, analogy and imagery, but if possible without using the hand-me-down language of cliché. So that rules out the missing link, the Holy Grail, the magic bullet and Pandora's Box for a start.”

Checked: https://www.theguardian.com/science/blog/2011/apr/13/secrets-good-science-writing, retrieved 2026-10-01

Radford moves from the job (“translator”) to its practical test, then names familiar phrases as examples of what to avoid. The slight wit comes from the particular list, not from decorating the subject.

> “To be of any use, a science writer has to deliver something that does not necessarily induce apoplexy in his scientifically trained readership, but at the same time keeps the lay reader glued to the text. This has its own dangers: science isn't simple, but newspaper paragraphs and news bulletin scripts have to be vivid, clear and headlong – in a word, simple, with few asides for proviso or caveat.”

Checked: https://www.theguardian.com/science/blog/2011/apr/13/secrets-good-science-writing, retrieved 2026-10-01

He addresses two kinds of reader in one clean movement and admits the constraint without making the prose timid. The contrast is useful for cards read by technically fluent people who still need unfamiliar policy or release details explained.

## Gaia Vince, “Tell me a story!”

Source: https://www.theguardian.com/science/blog/2011/apr/19/secrets-good-science-writing

> “Make sure the path through your story is nice and clear, guiding your reader step by logical step around any obstacles without losing them or sight of the story you are telling. Use everyday words in unambiguous sentences, however tempted you might be to show off your incredible vocabulary.”

Checked: https://www.theguardian.com/science/blog/2011/apr/19/secrets-good-science-writing, retrieved 2026-10-01

Vince pairs a physical, easy-to-follow description of sequence with a specific warning against vocabulary used for display. Her voice is direct and companionable, without assuming the reader is uninformed.

> “Extensive and thorough as your information-gathering may be, don't for one minute think that your reader wants to be dropped in a quagmire of your new knowledge. The research is for you; the story is for the reader.”

Checked: https://www.theguardian.com/science/blog/2011/apr/19/secrets-good-science-writing, retrieved 2026-10-01

The short second sentence makes the distinction memorable because it is concrete and earned by the longer one before it. The playful image has a job: it describes the reader’s experience of excess detail.

## Chrissie Giles, “What's the point of this story? Handy tips for science writers”

Source: https://www.theguardian.com/science/2011/may/09/handy-tips-science-writers

> “It's hard to know what to include in a science story, but harder to know what to leave out. Think about your readers. What would they want to know about this? What do they know already? What questions would they ask, given the chance?”

Checked: https://www.theguardian.com/science/2011/may/09/handy-tips-science-writers, retrieved 2026-10-01

Giles turns selection into a practical reader test, then uses questions to make that test immediate. Her conversational voice stays brisk because each question advances the same decision.

> “But there are only so many points you can make in a short article, so it's always better to make one or two points clearly than to attack your reader with a barrage of semi-developed ideas. Be economical with your language: every word has to be doing something. Don't be afraid to delete any redundant adjectives or padding.”

Checked: https://www.theguardian.com/science/2011/may/09/handy-tips-science-writers, retrieved 2026-10-01

She names the limit of short form, the reader cost of ignoring it, and the remedy in plain language. The directness has personality without forcing jokes or a grand ending.

## How this piece should sound

Let each card sound like a compact report from a well-informed guide: plainspoken, alert to what is new, and comfortable with the vocabulary of economics, law, defense policy, and software when that vocabulary earns its place. Assume mathematical and computer-science fluency, but introduce the specific context needed to understand the GDP estimate, tariff dispute, command proposal, or release change.

The word card has room for one lively turn: give *quiddity* its precise definition and pronunciation, then make its scholastic history legible through one telling detail. In the five news and technology cards, put the consequential development early and spend the remaining space on the evidence, mechanism, or timeline that makes it distinct. Like Radford, translate specialized material without flattening it; like Vince and Giles, choose details for the reader and let each sentence move the explanation forward.

Vary the cards’ pace. A crisp sentence can follow one that carries a qualification or a number, but do not make every item close by listing what remains unknown or unimplemented. Mark proposal, legal posture, reported fact, and operational change precisely where they arise; vary the ending according to what the card has established. Keep humor tied to a word’s history or a concrete detail, and leave it out when it would soften a consequential dispute.
