# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-02

## How this piece should sound

The brief has two registers, and the passages below cover each. Both are written for readers who know the field and want the fact. Neither explains itself or the reader's reaction.

**News items.** Nina Totenberg's opening and the BBC's Chandler's Ford story both put the development, who did it and the place or date in the first sentence, in plain present or past tense. Totenberg's second sentence then says what is at issue, which is the job of an item's second sentence here. The BBC's fourth and fifth paragraphs show attribution in the sentence itself: "Hampshire Police said it believes", "He maintains". A claim nobody has confirmed carries its owner's name, and a confirmed fact carries none. Totenberg's "That caveat is where things went awry" is the one place she lets a short plain judgment stand, and it stands because the sentence before it set up the caveat. An item with a sourced consequence can end on it. An item without one can end where the facts end. Figures arrive as the source gives them: "three boys aged 16 and two aged 17", "22:30 GMT on Saturday".

**Word card.** Merriam-Webster's two pieces start from the word's own puzzle and tell the history in the first person plural of the language ("we took", "we elected to adopt"). The light touch is one joke built on the word's own history, in the suffrage ending and in the proximate parenthetical. It is never a general flourish about words being fun. Keep technical terms as the lexicographer writes them (Middle French, Medieval Latin, the original diacritics), and let a parenthesis hold a gloss or a joke but not both.

**From the brief.** With two to three sentences per item, a first sentence that could be cut is a lost third of the item. Where a sentence only says why something matters, test whether the date, name or figure it points at is already in the item.

## Nina Totenberg, "Supreme Court hears religious freedom case centered on universal pre-K program" (NPR, 1 Nov 2026)

Source: https://www.npr.org/2026/11/01/nx-s1-6000367/supreme-court-colorado-catholic-preschool-religious-freedom-lgbtq

> "On Tuesday, the Supreme Court hears arguments in a case that pits the Catholic Church against taxpayer funded school programs for all 4-year-olds in Colorado. At issue is whether religious schools may refuse to admit the children of gay and trans parents in violation of a state law that bars discrimination."

The first sentence names the actor, the day, the court and the stakes, and the second states the legal question in one clause. The present tense is for an event that has not happened yet. She is visible in choosing "pits" and "refuse to admit" where a more cautious writer would use a euphemism.

> "To accomplish that, the state did something most other states have not. It created a broad plan, including religious schools, as long as the schools abide by a state law that bars discrimination based on race, religion, sexual orientation or gender identity."

A five-word sentence marks the turn, and then the challengers are named with their counsel and one position stated as his. "Maintains" reports a view without endorsing it.

Source: https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c6n4ed1l2pn1o

What the police believe is attributed to the police, and what they have done is stated flatly. The closing sentence is the next step and nothing more. The writer takes no view of the cause.

## Merriam-Webster staff, "Word of the Day: Suffrage"

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/serendipity-2020-12-28 (the page served at this address is titled "Word of the Day: Suffrage", Dec 28, 2020)

> "Why would a 17th-century writer warn people that a chapel was only for "private or secret suffrages"? Because suffrage has been used since the 14th century to mean "prayer" (especially a prayer requesting divine help or intercession)."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/serendipity-2020-12-28, retrieved 2026-11-02 (raw HTML, compared word for word with whitespace ignored)
The origin story starts as a puzzle with a date, a century and a quoted phrase, and the answer comes in the next sentence. The unfamiliar sense of the word is glossed in a parenthesis. The question is one the piece answers at once.

> "We took the "prayer" sense from a Middle French suffrāgium offspring that emphasized the word's spiritual aspects, and we elected to adopt the "voting" senses directly from the original Latin."

Checked: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/serendipity-2020-12-28, retrieved 2026-11-02 (raw HTML, compared word for word with whitespace ignored)
The two borrowings are told in the first person plural of English speakers. "Elected to adopt" is a pun on the word's own meaning, and it is the only joke in the passage. The source languages and the diacritic are given exactly.
