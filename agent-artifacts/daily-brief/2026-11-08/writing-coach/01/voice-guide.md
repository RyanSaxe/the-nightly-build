# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-08 (01)

Two genres sit in this brief and they are exemplified separately. The AP and NPR passages show the news and technology items. The two World Wide Words entries show the word card. The exemplars cover other subjects. Their facts are no part of this brief.

## How this piece should sound

The brief is the voice of a wire reporter who has opened the record, followed by a short word history told by someone who finds the word odd. The registers differ and should. The reader has a degree in computer science or mathematics and does not need a magnitude word where a figure exists.

News and technology items. Each is two or three sentences, so each sentence carries a fact. The AP lede on the LSU scholarships puts the actor, the act, the day and the institutions in one sentence and leaves the sum for the next paragraph. Its second passage repeats "the White House said" after each figure, so a reader who stops partway still knows whose numbers they are. Attribute a figure in the clause where it appears, and name the document or the person that made a claim that cannot be checked. The NPR passage on the stolen toilet reports an odd detail flat: the insured value, that it was not recovered, and the defendant's own word in quotation marks. Where the record holds something odd or funny, the item may state it and leave it. What the headline dropped, the number or the caveat, belongs in the item in the source's own noun.

Word card. Quinion states the plain meaning, then the record, and says where the sources disagree. In the Hoosegow passages the first citation comes with its author, series, year and original spelling, and the etymology follows in steps, each a single sentence. In Gazump the disputed origin is reported as a disagreement ("some dictionaries suggest", "others are less sure") with the evidence for each side in the same sentence. Take the word's story in the order its own record supports, which may begin with a coiner, a first citation, a dispute or a misattribution. Use the lexicographer's vocabulary (first recorded, attested, from the Yiddish) and give foreign and older forms as the dictionaries print them. A dry aside may stand where the record supplies one, as the bed does in the last Gazump passage, because it is a fact first.

Across both: past tense for what happened, present tense for what a word means or what a filing says. Plain verbs, and one longer sentence only when a figure needs its source attached to it. People appear as the ones who acted, named with what they do (a judge, a defendant, a cartoonist). The Quinion passages use em-dashes in places and the house punctuation standard governs this piece, so a period goes where one would be.

> "BATON ROUGE, La. (AP) — Melania Trump on Saturday announced a multimillion dollar partnership between Louisiana State University and her "Fostering the Future" program to provide scholarships for LSU students who came through the foster care system."

One sentence carries who, what, the day, the two institutions and the purpose. The sum is stated as "multimillion dollar" here and given as a figure in the next paragraph, so the lede stays short and the number follows with its source.

> "Trump and LSU secured more than $7 million in private donations to create an endowment fund for the scholarships, the White House said. MMR Group Inc., an electrical contracting company based in Baton Rouge, committed $5 million, with additional LSU Foundation donors providing more than $2 million, the White House said."

The attribution is repeated after the second figure instead of once for both, so a reader who stops mid-paragraph still knows whose numbers they are. The donor is named with what it does and where it is.

> "The first lady started working on foster care issues after the Republican president's first term ended in 2021, and created "Fostering the Future" to provide resources and support, including scholarships. LSU is among more than 20 colleges and universities nationwide that participate in the program."

Checked: https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/melania-trump-and-lsu-announce-a-scholarship-program-for-students-who-were-in-foster-care, retrieved 2026-11-08
Background arrives as dated fact (what she started, when, and how many schools take part) and the scale is a count. Nothing in the paragraph judges the program.

## NPR, "UK court finds men who stole $6 million gold toilet guilty"

Source: https://www.npr.org/2025/03/20/g-s1-54909/golden-toilet-stolen-cattelan-6-million-convicted

> "The original heist took place on Sept. 14, 2019, when a group of thieves used sledgehammers and crowbars to remove the toilet from the floor of Oxfordshire's historic Blenheim Palace. It reportedly took them under five minutes to escape with the luxurious latrine."

Checked: https://text.npr.org/g-s1-54909, retrieved 2026-11-08
The date, the tools and the duration are all concrete, and "reportedly" marks the one claim that rests on someone's account. The last word of the second sentence is the only decoration, and it is a plain synonym.

> "The toilet, which was insured for a value of $6 million has yet to be recovered. Prosecutors say that it was dismantled and sold in parts. Jones was even questioned about having used the toilet the day before the heist. He described the experience as "splendid.""

Checked: https://text.npr.org/g-s1-54909, retrieved 2026-11-08
The amusing facts are reported in the order they bear on the case: value, status, who says what happened to it, then the defendant's own word, quoted. The writer leaves the joke to the quotation.

## Michael Quinion, "Hoosegow", World Wide Words

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-hoo1.htm

> "It’s a fine old American slang term for a jail, still widely known today. Most people would connect it with the nineteenth-century cowboys of the Wild West. It’s very likely that they knew the word, but it didn’t start to be written down until the early twentieth century. The first known example was penned by Harry Fisher, better known as Bud, in one of his early Mutt & Jeff cartoons, of 1908: “Mutt ... may be released from the hooze gow.”"

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-hoo1.htm, retrieved 2026-11-08
He states what the word is, names the popular assumption, and separates what is likely from what is on record. The first citation comes with its author, the series, the year and the original spelling.

> "The word is from Mexican Spanish juzgao , a jail, which came from juzgado for a tribunal or courtroom. It shifted to mean a jail because the two were often in the same building (and the path from the one to the other was often swift and certain)."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-hoo1.htm, retrieved 2026-11-08
The etymology is two steps in a plain sentence, with the reason for the shift of meaning given in a parenthesis that is a small, dry joke made of the facts. The foreign words are given in the form the dictionaries use.

> "Hoosegow is now the standard spelling, though in its early days it was written half a dozen different ways. We link it in our minds with cowboys largely because so much of their lingo was taken from Spanish and then mangled to fit English ideas of the way to say it."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-hoo1.htm, retrieved 2026-11-08
"We" appears once, for a shared mistaken association, and a person is audible in "mangled". He then resolves the cowboy impression the first paragraph raised.

## Michael Quinion, "Gazump", World Wide Words

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-gaz1.htm

> "It’s not easy to formulate a definition that matches the brevity of this useful little word. It means to unfairly acquire a property by making a better offer than one that has already been accepted by the seller."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-gaz1.htm, retrieved 2026-11-08
He admits the definition is longer than the word, gives it anyway in one sentence, and does not pad it. The opening is an observation about the word, so the reader is told why it is worth knowing.

> "Some dictionaries suggest gazump comes from the Yiddish gezumph , to overcharge or cheat. This is supported by the word’s first meaning in English back in the 1920s, to swindle, but others are less sure."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-gaz1.htm, retrieved 2026-11-08
A disputed origin is reported as a disagreement among named kinds of source (dictionaries, others), with the evidence on each side stated in the same sentence. No verdict is forced.

> "When the housing market is depressed, a stranger term appears, to gazunder , in which buyers arbitrarily reduce the offered price, usually near the date of exchange of contracts when there is little chance of the seller finding another purchaser. This appeared in the late 1980s, and is a rather curious blend of gazump and under . It has no connection with the colloquial use of gazunder for a chamber pot, so called because it usually “goes under” the bed."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-gaz1.htm, retrieved 2026-11-08
The tail of the entry is a later coinage dated to a decade, explained by its mechanism, and then ruled out as the same word as a homonym. The last sentence is funny because of the bed, and the writer adds nothing to it.
