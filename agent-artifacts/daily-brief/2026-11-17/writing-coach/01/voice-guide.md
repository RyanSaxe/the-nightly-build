# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-17 (01)

## How this piece should sound

The Daily Brief is six items in about three minutes, so every item is two to four sentences and each sentence carries a fact. The register is a good wire desk writing for readers who build production ML systems: plain, declarative, past tense for what happened, no throat-clearing. The AP Titan story below shows the order to look for. The first sentence names the actor, the action and the result, and the second gives the one concrete detail (a place, a size, a count) that makes the event this event and no other. Background the reader needs comes after that, in a clause, and only where the item cannot be understood without it.

When a figure is the news, give the figure with its unit and, where the reader could not scale it alone, the comparison that scales it. Willison's price passage gives the old price, the new price and the multiple, each in its own short sentence, and attributes nothing he cannot show. That is the model for the Nvidia results if they are released and for any technology item with a number in it: the company's figure, the period it covers, and the comparison the company's own release supports. Leave out adjectives that try to do the comparison's job.

A person can be visible in an item this short, but only through judgement and plainness, never through commentary. Willison's "This remains astonishing to me" is one short sentence after the facts that earn it. In a brief, the equivalent is choosing which detail goes in the second sentence, and stating plainly where nobody knows yet. AP quotes the Woods Hole engineer saying "I really don't know the answer to that question", and Quinion writes "Its ultimate source is unknown." Where the reporting stops, a brief item may say so in that many words and stop.

For the Word of the Day, Quinion is the register: a pronunciation, then the story told as a sequence of documented claims, each with who said it and when, including the claims that turned out wrong. His words are not candidates for tonight's word; take the method, not the material. The word's item has three or four sentences for its story, so the documented coinage or first use goes first and the unknown part, if any, is said once.

## Associated Press, "'Presumed human remains' found in wreckage of Titanic tourist sub, U.S. Coast Guard says"

Source: https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/presumed-human-remains-found-in-wreckage-of-titanic-tourist-sub-u-s-coast-guard-says (AP story as carried by PBS NewsHour, 28 June 2023)

> "The return of the Titan debris to port in St. John's, Newfoundland and Labrador on Wednesday is a key piece of the investigation into why the submersible imploded. Twisted chunks of the 22-foot submersible were unloaded at a Canadian Coast Guard pier."

Checked: https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/presumed-human-remains-found-in-wreckage-of-titanic-tourist-sub-u-s-coast-guard-says, retrieved 2026-11-17
The first sentence says why the event matters and the second gives a physical detail, the size of the craft and where it landed, in eleven words. The reporter's one choice of adjective, "Twisted", is something a person on the pier saw.

> "Debris from the Titan was located about 12,500 feet (3,810 meters) underwater and roughly 1,600 feet (488 meters) from the Titanic on the ocean floor, the Coast Guard said last week. The Coast Guard is leading the investigation into why the submersible imploded during its June 18 descent."

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-17
Figures carry the source's own hedges ("about", "roughly") and the attribution sits at the end of the sentence so the fact leads. The second sentence names who is responsible and dates the event, which is all the background the reader needs.

> "\"Certainly all the instruments on any deep sea vehicle, they record data. They pass up data. So the question is, is there any data available? And I really don't know the answer to that question,\" he said Monday."

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-17
The reporter lets an expert state the open question in his own words and does not speculate past it. This is how an unknown gets into a news item without the writer guessing.

## Simon Willison, "Things we learned about LLMs in 2024"

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/31/llms-in-2024/

> "In December 2023 (here's the Internet Archive for the OpenAI pricing page) OpenAI were charging $30/million input tokens for GPT-4, $10/mTok for the then-new GPT-4 Turbo and $1/mTok for GPT-3.5 Turbo.
>
> Today $30/mTok gets you OpenAI's most expensive model, o1. GPT-4o is $2.50 (12x cheaper than GPT-4) and GPT-4o mini is $0.15/mTok—200x cheaper than GPT-4, nearly 7x cheaper than GPT-3.5 and massively more capable than that model."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/31/llms-in-2024/ (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-17
He gives the before figures with their source, then the after figures, and computes the multiples himself so the reader does not have to. The practitioner's unit, "$/mTok", is kept as he uses it.

> "Training a GPT-4 beating model was a huge deal in 2023. In 2024 it's an achievement that isn't even particularly notable, though I personally still celebrate any time a new organization joins that list."

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-17
Two sentences place a development against the year before, which is the comparison a technology item needs. The person shows in one plain clause of his own reaction, after the claim and not instead of it.

> "This remains astonishing to me. I thought a model with the capabilities and output quality of GPT-4 needed a datacenter class server with one or more $40,000+ GPUs.
>
> These models take up enough of my 64GB of RAM that I don't run them often—they don't leave much room for anything else."

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-17
He states what he expected, with a price, and then the practical limit, so the surprise is measured and not just announced. The limitation arrives in the same register as the achievement.

## Michael Quinion, "Snarge" (World Wide Words)

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-sna1.htm

> "There are usually bits of mixed-up bird remains on the aircraft, such as blood, beaks, feathers, and flesh. This is snarge, a term widely known among US air-accident investigators. Analysing the remains can often tell its species."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-sna1.htm (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-17
He shows the thing before naming it, then names it and says who uses the word. The definition is concrete enough that the reader could point at it.

> "An article in the magazine Flying Safety in 2003 says that the term snarge was invented at the Lab. However, one of its researchers, Carla Dove, says they didn't but instead borrowed it from the experts who prepared bird specimens for the collections"

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-17
Each claim about the origin has a named source and a date, and the correction is reported as another sourced claim, not as the writer's opinion. This is the shape of a documented word story.

> "Its ultimate source is unknown. The only reference I can find to the word is in a 1925 British book, Soldier and Sailor Words and Phrases, by Edward Fraser and John Gibbons, in which they gloss it as \"any ugly or unpleasant person\"."

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-17
He says flatly what is not known, then gives the earliest evidence he found, with author, title and year. The "I can find" is the one place the person appears, and it tells the reader how far the search went.

## Michael Quinion, "Gallimaufry" (World Wide Words)

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-gal1.htm

> "It comes from the archaic French galimafrée, of unknown origin, which might have referred to a kind of sauce or stew. Support for this comes from its earliest sense in English of a ragout or mishmash of ingredients"

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-gal1.htm (raw HTML), retrieved 2026-11-17
A tentative etymology is marked as tentative ("might have") and then supported with evidence in the next sentence. The reader can see which part is established and which is inferred.

> "The figurative meaning, now the only one in use, is obviously derived from the cookery sense. It's as old as the literal one: \"So now,\" Edmund Spencer lamented in 1579, \"they have made our English tongue a gallimaufry, or hodgepodge of all other speeches\"."

Checked: same URL, retrieved 2026-11-17
He ends the story on a dated quotation that shows the word in use, so the history closes on evidence. One verb, "lamented", carries his reading of the source.
