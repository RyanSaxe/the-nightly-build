# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-28 (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a three-minute digest for technical readers who read widely, and each news and technology item has only two or three sentences. That length settles the register. Write like the wire. The Associated Press passages below put the actor, the verb and the result in the first sentence, then fit the context into a clause hung off the subject ("who was Dak Prescott's backup all last season", "signed by the Eagles about the same time..."). The reader gets the background without spending a sentence on it. In an item this short the second sentence can carry the attribution and the background, and the third can say what follows, when the record says something does. Give the exact count, date or vote the source gives, and put a number up front only where the number is the news.

Treat the reader as somebody with no time and a good memory. Nobody here explains why a story matters or tells the reader how to feel about it. Name who said what, and leave a claim attributed when the record only reaches as far as somebody's say-so.

The Word of the Day card is where some warmth comes in. Quinion and Zimmer show how. They are precise about documents: who wrote the word, in what letter or column, on what date, and what the dictionary says. Their pleasure in the material shows in small, checkable details, like Quinion's camel thieves or Zimmer setting "natural turf" beside "natural grass". It does not show in adjectives. If the sources turn up a correction to the popular story, or one telling detail from the coiner's own words, the card has room for it. Keep it to three or four sentences and let the documented story make the card lively. Serendipity and retronym appear here only to illustrate the writing. They are not suggestions for today's word.

## The Associated Press, "Eagles rally to stun Cowboys coming back from 21 points behind"

Source: https://text.npr.org/nx-s1-6055519

> "ARLINGTON, Texas — Joe Milton rolled to his left while motioning DeVonta Smith where to go near the end zone, then threw the kind of deep ball the Dallas Cowboys always knew he was capable of when he was playing for them."

Checked: https://text.npr.org/nx-s1-6055519, retrieved 2026-11-28
The first sentence is a single action told in order, with named people and a place. The writer's own view shows in one clause, "the kind of deep ball the Dallas Cowboys always knew he was capable of", which states a judgment about the rivalry as a fact about the teams.

> "Milton, who was Dak Prescott's backup all last season, threw three touchdowns and ran for another one in a 39-38 win Thursday in his third start for the Eagles, who signed him off the Dallas practice squad on Oct. 16."

Checked: https://text.npr.org/nx-s1-6055519, retrieved 2026-11-28
This one sentence holds the result, the score, the day and two pieces of background, and it reads easily because both pieces of background sit in clauses after the noun they describe. The main clause is subject, verb and figures.

> "Milton, signed by the Eagles about the same time they were determining that Jalen Hurts' season was over because of diagnosis of blood clots, completed 21 of 31 passes for a career-best 331 yards."

Checked: https://text.npr.org/nx-s1-6055519, retrieved 2026-11-28
The writer gives exact figures and no adjectives except "career-best", which is itself a fact. The clause about Hurts gives a cause the reader needs and does not spend a separate sentence on it.

## Michael Quinion, "Serendipity" (World Wide Words)

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-ser1.htm

> "Horace Walpole coined it in a letter he wrote to his long-time diplomat friend Horace Mann in 1754. He told him that he invented it in reference to the title of an old Persian fairy story The Three Princes of Serendip, whose heroes regularly discovered pleasant things that they weren't searching for."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-ser1.htm, retrieved 2026-11-28
The origin story comes in two plain sentences: who coined it, in what document, to whom, in what year, and what he said he meant. The coiner's own account is reported as his ("He told him"), so the reader knows where the claim comes from.

> "If you read the story you will find that the princes were well educated and intelligent men and their good fortune (which was a bit slap-dash: they got thrown in jail as suspected camel thieves at one point) was based on careful deduction and sagacity, not chance."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-ser1.htm, retrieved 2026-11-28
Quinion corrects the usual reading of the word from the source text. The joke in the parenthesis is a fact from the story, and "a bit slap-dash" is how a person talks, which is where Quinion himself shows.

> "The three princes came from a country the Persians called Sarandib but which we now know as Sri Lanka, or in earlier times Ceylon. The Persian is a corruption of the Sanskrit Sinhaladvipa, "the island where lions dwell", hence the name Sinhala or Sinhalese for the most commonly spoken Sri Lankan language."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-ser1.htm, retrieved 2026-11-28
He follows the word back one step at a time and names each language. The gloss in quotation marks gives the reader the image without any commentary on it.

## Ben Zimmer, "RIP Frank Mankiewicz, coiner of "retronym"" (Language Log)

Source: https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=15399

> "Mankiewicz was also a bit of wordsmith and coined a useful word now found in many dictionaries: retronym …"

Checked: https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=15399, retrieved 2026-11-28
Zimmer names the coiner and calls the word "useful", which is a small, plain judgment a reader can test. The colon leads straight to the word, and the OED definition follows in the source.

> "The first print appearance of retronym was in William Safire's July 27, 1980 "On Language" column in the New York Times Magazine:"

Checked: https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=15399, retrieved 2026-11-28
This is the lexicographer's habit: the first attestation, with the exact date, publication and column, so anyone can check it. He keeps who coined the word separate from where it first appeared in print.

> "I spoke with him a bit about retronyms afterwards, and the first one that he recalled noticing was "natural grass" at the beginning of baseball's Astroturf era. (Safire mentioned "natural turf," but "natural grass" is indeed the more common retronym.)"

Checked: https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=15399, retrieved 2026-11-28
Here Zimmer is visible as a person, because he reports the coiner's own memory and then quietly fixes the published record. The correction is one plain sentence in parentheses and is not made into a big moment.
