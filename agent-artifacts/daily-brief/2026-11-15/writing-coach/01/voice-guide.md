# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-15

## How this piece should sound

This is a three-minute read for engineers who build ML systems and read widely. The register is a well-informed colleague passing on what happened, in plain declarative sentences. Every item states the event, names who did or claims it, and gives the one figure or name that fixes its size. Readers here do not need to be told a thing is important. They need the specifics that let them judge it, as Simon Willison does when he lays out the old and new token prices side by side and lets the ratio do the work.

The Word of the Day is the one place a person can be audible. Michael Quinion tells a word's story in a run of dated, sourced facts (the inventor, the book, the year, then who picked the word up), and he lets one dry aside through when the source material supplies it. That aside comes from the record, as with Heinlein's own remark about his book, and not from a quip the writer adds. In three or four sentences there is room for at most one, and only if the documented story has one.

For the news and technology items, Jack Clark's format shows how to compress: who did it, how it works in one sentence, the headline number quoted from the source, and then a separate, labelled sentence for what follows. In two or three sentences that last part may be a single clause. When a claim is unverified, attribute it the way Clark puts the paper's own words in quotation marks, so the reader can see where the source ends and the brief begins. Use the field's exact terms (model names, precisions, benchmark names) without glossing them for this audience, and define only what a reader arriving from a link could not know.

Hold the item to what happened in the window. Willison's surrounding commentary and Clark's "Why this matters" paragraphs are longer than this genre allows. Take from them the habit of saying plainly what changed, and leave the speculation out unless a named source makes it.

## Michael Quinion, "Grok"

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-gro1.htm

Note for the orchestrator: this piece is chosen for its form. If the brief picks "grok" as its word, this guide would hand the writer Quinion's account, so a different word is safer.

> "We must look to Robert Anson Heinlein for the origins of this word, which he invented for his science-fantasy book Stranger in a Strange Land in 1961. In this, Valentine Michael Smith, a human being raised on Mars, returns to Earth with psi powers given him by the Martians and is transformed into a messiah."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-gro1.htm (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-15
The inventor, the book and the year all arrive in the first sentence, and the second gives only as much plot as the word needs. The compression of the plot into one clause ("is transformed into a messiah") is a reader's judgment, not a blurb.

> "The book became a cult classic despite its deeply flawed nature. Heinlein remarked self-deprecatingly about it that it was incredible what some people would do for money; it was originally published in a brutally edited form and became available as originally written only in 1990."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-gro1.htm (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-15
Quinion states his own opinion of the book in four words and moves on. The humour is Heinlein's, reported, and the sentence ends on a date, so the aside still carries a fact.

> "The term went into the language, at first among countercultural types in California and among SF fans (there used to be lapel buttons around with the message “I grok Spock”), but was later taken up by computer geeks and scientists, among whom it has largely remained."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-gro1.htm (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-15
The spread of the word is told as a sequence of communities, with one concrete artefact as evidence. The parenthesis is a true aside, and the lapel button is the detail of someone who remembers the period.

## Jack Clark, "Import AI 470: No rights for machines; automating environment generation with SPADE; and building better GPU kernels with Hawkeye"

Source: https://importai.substack.com/p/import-ai-470-no-rights-for-machines

> "Here’s a little analysis from METR which looks at where AI may be accelerating different types of science and technology. The study looks at three different areas: cyber, math, and AI research, and finds that AI has contributed a lot to cyber, a little bit to math, and it’s hard to say for AI."

Checked: https://importai.substack.com/p/import-ai-470-no-rights-for-machines (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-15
Who did it and the whole finding fit in two sentences, in conversational words ("a lot", "a little bit", "hard to say") that still rank the three results exactly. Clark's voice is in "a little analysis", which sizes the study honestly.

> "Researchers with Harvard, Stanford, Together AI, and Caltech have built Hawkeye, software to make it easier for agents to learn how to write well-optimized kernels for specific types of GPU hardware."

Checked: https://importai.substack.com/p/import-ai-470-no-rights-for-machines (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-15
One sentence holds the institutions, the name of the thing and what it is for. Nothing is said about its importance until the result has been given.

> "For games, “at 30B-A3B, SPADE reaches a suite average of 58.3: +8.1 over base and +5.3 over the strongest fixed-environment baseline”. For tools, they see the same significant boost: “the same recipe applied to tool-use environment design improves every backbone”."

Checked: https://importai.substack.com/p/import-ai-470-no-rights-for-machines (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-15
The number is quoted in the authors' own words, so the claim stays theirs. Both comparisons are given, against the base model and against the strongest baseline, which is the one a practitioner would ask for.

## Simon Willison, "Things we learned about LLMs in 2024"

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/31/llms-in-2024/

> "In December 2023 (here’s the Internet Archive for the OpenAI pricing page) OpenAI were charging $30/million input tokens for GPT-4, $10/mTok for the then-new GPT-4 Turbo and $1/mTok for GPT-3.5 Turbo. Today $30/mTok gets you OpenAI’s most expensive model, o1. GPT-4o is $2.50 (12x cheaper than GPT-4) and GPT-4o mini is $0.15/mTok—200x cheaper than GPT-4, nearly 7x cheaper than GPT-3.5 and massively more capable"

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/31/llms-in-2024/ (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-15
The claim is carried entirely by prices with a dated source, and he computes the ratios a reader would otherwise do in their head. The parenthetical link to the archived pricing page is a writer who expects to be checked.

> "Training a GPT-4 beating model was a huge deal in 2023. In 2024 it’s an achievement that isn’t even particularly notable, though I personally still celebrate any time a new organization joins that list."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/31/llms-in-2024/ (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-15
Two short sentences state the change by comparing two years. The first-person clause is his and belongs to a personal blog. The sentences before it are what a brief can use.

> "Today every serious provider has a 100,000+ token model, and Google’s Gemini series accepts up to 2 million."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/31/llms-in-2024/ (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-15
A state of the field in one sentence, with the general figure and the extreme case named. It uses the practitioner's unit, tokens, without explanation.
