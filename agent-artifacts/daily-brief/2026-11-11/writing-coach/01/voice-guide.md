# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-11

## How this piece should sound

This is a three-minute read for engineers who build machine-learning systems and read widely beyond them. The register is that of a well-informed colleague passing on the morning's news: plain declarative sentences, the actor named, the figure given, and no throat-clearing before the fact. Nobody reading it needs to be told an event matters. They need to know what happened, who did it or says so, and what it sets in motion, and in two or three sentences there is room for nothing else.

The Word of the Day is where the brief can relax a little. Quinion's "Serendipity" entry shows how: the dated quotation, the coiner and the letter, and then one sentence where the writer is plainly enjoying a detail (the princes jailed as camel thieves) before getting back to the history. The story of the word can carry that kind of small pleasure where the documented record supplies one. It should not be invented where the record is thin, and Quinion's "Murder" entry shows the honest version of that: he says what the earliest evidence is, who disputes the origin, and stops at "no more than a guess."

For the news items, Kottke's USPS item shows the shape: the institution, the verb, the specific thing, then the one detail that tells you what kind of story it is. The opening of each item can be the actor or the event itself; the commission notes recent briefs leaning on day-of-week openings, and Kottke's items open on the thing. For the two technology items, Willison shows how a practitioner reports a release: the version names, sizes and prices exactly as the vendor gives them, a judgment stated in the first person only where he has used the thing, and a qualification only where it is the news ("On paper... In practice"). A caveat belongs in an item when it changes what the release means, not as the last line of every item.

## Michael Quinion, "Serendipity" (World Wide Words)

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-ser1.htm

> "Horace Walpole coined it in a letter he wrote to his long-time diplomat friend Horace Mann in 1754. He told him that he invented it in reference to the title of an old Persian fairy story The Three Princes of Serendip, whose heroes regularly discovered pleasant things that they weren't searching for."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-ser1.htm (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-11
Coiner, document, recipient and year in one sentence, then the reason for the coinage in the next. The lexicographer is visible in how much documentary detail he thinks a reader wants and how little else.

> "If you read the story you will find that the princes were well educated and intelligent men and their good fortune (which was a bit slap-dash: they got thrown in jail as suspected camel thieves at one point) was based on careful deduction and sagacity, not chance."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-ser1.htm (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-11
The parenthesis is a real aside and a funny one, and the sentence still corrects the common reading of the word. The person here has read the story and enjoys it.

## Michael Quinion, "Murder" (World Wide Words)

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/topicalwords/tw-mur1.htm

> "Some caution is needed, since this phrase is hard to date — so much so that the Oxford English Dictionary put out a request for help two years ago. Their earliest example then was from Punch magazine in 1986: "I could murder a plate of ham and eggs", but the editors were rightly sure it was older."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/topicalwords/tw-mur1.htm (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-11
The uncertainty is stated as a fact about the record, with the source, the date and the quotation that make it checkable. He names the OED instead of "lexicographers".

> "Subscribers have suggested it might come from the old practice of describing an empty glass as dead. But really that's no more than a guess. All very puzzling."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/topicalwords/tw-mur1.htm (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-11
He reports a theory, says who offered it, and declines to endorse it in plain words. The last fragment is a person shrugging, and it is honest because the evidence ends there.

## Simon Willison, "Things we learned about LLMs in 2024"

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/31/llms-in-2024/

> "18 organizations now have models on the Chatbot Arena Leaderboard that rank higher than the original GPT-4 from March 2023 (GPT-4-0314 on the board)—70 models in total. The earliest of those was Google's Gemini 1.5 Pro, released in February."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/31/llms-in-2024/ (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-11
A count, the exact model identifier a practitioner would search for, and a dated first instance. The claim is the number, not an adjective about progress.

> "On paper, a 64GB Mac should be a great machine for running models due to the way the CPU and GPU can share the same memory. In practice, many models are released as model weights and libraries that reward NVIDIA's CUDA over other platforms."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/31/llms-in-2024/ (raw HTML via curl), retrieved 2026-11-11
The qualification is technical and checkable: shared memory against the CUDA-first release practice. The writer is a user with a specific machine, and that is where his judgment comes from.
