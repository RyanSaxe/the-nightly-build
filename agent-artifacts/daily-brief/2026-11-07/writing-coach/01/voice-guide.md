# Voice guide: daily-brief/2026-11-07

## How this piece should sound

This is a three-minute read of six short items for engineers and mathematicians who build machine learning systems. The register is a well-informed colleague telling you what happened since yesterday: plain, exact, a little dry, never breathless. Each item has two or three sentences, so every sentence carries a fact, a name or a consequence, and there is no room for a sentence that only sets up the next one.

For the news and technology items, Geoff Brumfiel's shearwater piece shows the order that suits a short report: the finding in one plain sentence, then the source by name and institution, then the figure that tells you how big it was, kept to the size the source gives. Where a figure is small or large, he says so with a comparison the reader already holds (three miles on a 600-mile journey) and lets that stand. An item here may need only the first and last of those moves. Simon Willison's pricing passage shows how to handle technology numbers for this audience: name the model or release exactly, give the unit the field uses (per million tokens, parameter count, context length) and put the old number beside the new one when the change is the news. Readers here do not need a model or a benchmark explained, and they notice when one is described vaguely.

Attribution belongs inside the sentence that makes the claim, the way Brumfiel writes "the authors say" mid-sentence. Where a claim is only the company's, the verb can carry that ("OpenAI says", "the filing reports") and the item can then move on to what follows from it; it does not need a closing caveat. Vary how items open: Brumfiel starts with the finding, not with a dated full name and title, and the news items may start with what happened, a place, or a number, whichever the event makes most concrete.

For the Word of the Day, Michael Quinion's entry shows the card's register: a dictionary-maker's precision with a light touch of personal judgment ("not the easiest cluster of consonants to say"). He states where a word comes from and who used it for what, with the documented source named, and allows himself one observation a reader can check. The word's story has three or four sentences to work with; told in his manner it is a short history of real people and dates, not a celebration of the word. Humour is welcome where the material supplies it, as Brumfiel's "flap lag" does, and absent where it does not.

## Geoff Brumfiel, "Even birds get jet lagged, study shows"

Source: https://text.npr.org/nx-s1-6019228

> "A study published in the journal Science found that a type of seabird known as the Manx shearwater appears to become slightly disoriented during long flights across the Atlantic Ocean. That disorientation, the authors say, is due to the bird's internal clock falling out of sync with the sun's position in the sky."

Checked: https://text.npr.org/nx-s1-6019228, retrieved 2026-11-07
The finding, the journal and the mechanism arrive in two sentences, and "appears to" and "the authors say" keep the claim at exactly the strength the study supports. Brumfiel is visible in the choice to attribute the explanation mid-sentence rather than tacking a caveat on at the end.

> "That's exactly what Lewin and his colleagues saw. As the birds rushed back to their partners and eggs, they veered ever-so-slightly off course. It wasn't much: A seven-degree error, the maximum deviation observed, only took a bird out of its way by about three miles on a 600-mile journey."

Checked: https://text.npr.org/nx-s1-6019228, retrieved 2026-11-07
He tells you the effect is small before giving the number, then gives the number with its scale beside it so nobody has to judge it alone. "It wasn't much" is a reporter's own plain verdict, and it keeps him from inflating the result.

> "Which raised an interesting question: Do Manx shearwaters feel jet lag (or perhaps more aptly, flap lag)?"

Checked: https://text.npr.org/nx-s1-6019228, retrieved 2026-11-07
The joke is made from the story's own nouns and takes four words in parentheses. The person on the page is someone enjoying the subject without stopping the report to do it.

## Simon Willison, "Things we learned about LLMs in 2024"

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/31/llms-in-2024/

> "In December 2023 (here’s the Internet Archive for the OpenAI pricing page) OpenAI were charging $30/million input tokens for GPT-4, $10/mTok for the then-new GPT-4 Turbo and $1/mTok for GPT-3.5 Turbo.
>
> Today $30/mTok gets you OpenAI’s most expensive model, o1. GPT-4o is $2.50 (12x cheaper than GPT-4) and GPT-4o mini is $0.15/mTok—200x cheaper than GPT-4, nearly 7x cheaper than GPT-3.5 and massively more capable than that model."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/31/llms-in-2024/, retrieved 2026-11-07
Every model is named exactly and every price uses the unit practitioners use, and he links the archived page that owns the old figure. The comparison does the arguing; he adds only "massively more capable", a judgment from someone who uses these models daily.

> "Training a GPT-4 beating model was a huge deal in 2023. In 2024 it’s an achievement that isn’t even particularly notable, though I personally still celebrate any time a new organization joins that list."

Checked: https://simonwillison.net/2024/Dec/31/llms-in-2024/, retrieved 2026-11-07
Two short sentences place a capability against last year's baseline, which is the context a technology item usually needs. The personal clause shows a working practitioner, though a brief item would keep the placement and leave out the first person.

## Michael Quinion, "Sphragistics"

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-sph1.htm

> "The original idea behind this word in English was very much tied up with diplomacy, since there was a real practical need to confirm that the seals on what purported to be official documents from foreign countries were genuine. So specialists studied the history of seals. They still do, but these days more because the study of old seals can reveal a great deal about the history of a period."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-sph1.htm, retrieved 2026-11-07
He explains why the word was needed by naming the practical problem people had, so the history is about people doing something. "So specialists studied the history of seals. They still do" uses short sentences to move a word across centuries without a date-by-date list.

> "Sphragistics is almost the only word in English today that contains the /sfr/ combination of sounds, not the easiest cluster of consonants to say. It is from Greek sphragis, a seal (sphragistes is the Greek term for an Egyptian priest who kept and used the temple seal)."

Checked: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-sph1.htm, retrieved 2026-11-07
A checkable observation, a dry aside, then the etymology stated flatly with a precise gloss. Quinion is present in "not the easiest cluster of consonants to say", which is an opinion a reader can test by saying the word.
