## Item 1. Word of the Day: mumpsimus

Event date: not applicable. Primary record opens in Erasmus, letter to Henry Bullock, dated by its editor Rochester, "(22?) August 1516".

Card facts:
- Part of speech: noun. Pronunciation (Merriam-Webster): ˈməmpsəməs, plural -es.
- Definition (Merriam-Webster): "a bigoted adherent to exposed but customary error"; sense 2, "a custom or tenet adhered to by a mumpsimus".
- Story: Erasmus told of a priest who would not give up the "mumpsimus" he had said for twenty years after someone told him the word to read was "sumpsimus". He told it to mock the critics of his corrected New Testament. Richard Pace's *De Fructu* (1517) made the priest English and the interval thirty years. On 24 December 1545 Henry VIII told Parliament that "Some be to styff in their old Mumpsimus, other be to busy and curious, in their newe Sumpsimus", in a speech the chronicler Edward Hall says he reported "worde for worde, as nere as I was able".

### Contradictions (word)

- Wikipedia (read, discarded) says Erasmus "may have coined" the word. One search snippet said Erasmus "did not use the word mumpsimus in this account", but I did not open the page behind it. Allen's text settles the point: the word is in Erasmus's Latin, lines 73–74.
- The interval is twenty years in Erasmus and thirty in Pace (per Allen's note). Stories that give thirty years are following Pace.
- The first OED edition, as Wikipedia reports it, credits Pace's *De Fructu* as the origin. Allen, writing in 1910, puts Erasmus's letter first. I did not open the OED or Pace's text.

### Limits (word)

- I did not open Pace's *De Fructu* (1517). What Pace added comes from Allen's note.
- I did not establish Henry Bullock's title, so a card should not give him one. The Correspondence of Erasmus describes him, but I did not open it.
- The word did not start as a coinage by a known inventor. It began as a priest's misreading that Erasmus recorded. It meets the series test as "a word with a story" with documents behind it, not as a coined word.
- The Latin prayer containing "sumpsimus" ("Quod ore sumpsimus") comes only from Wikipedia. Erasmus's letter does not name the prayer.
