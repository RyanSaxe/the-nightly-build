# Evidence record: parenting-research/infant-iron (01)

The evidence supports a well-established physiological story (fetal iron stores run
down around four to six months, breast milk is iron-poor, formula is fortified) and
a well-established association (iron-deficiency anemia in infancy correlates with
worse long-term cognitive and behavioral outcomes). It does **not** support a clean
causal chain from "give more iron" to "better brain outcomes" in infants who are not
already iron deficient. The single best-documented RCT line in this file — the
Chilean iron-fortified-formula trial and its 10- and 16-year follow-ups — found that
supplementing infants who were already iron-replete at 6 months was associated with
*lower* developmental scores at 10 years, with large effect sizes in the highest
hemoglobin subgroup. Guidance bodies diverge sharply on what follows from this:
AAP's own screening recommendation changed in 2026, and USPSTF has held since 2015
that the evidence is insufficient to judge screening's net benefit at all. Coverage
is thin in one place the commission cares about: I could not obtain a clean, current,
first-year-specific US national prevalence figure narrower than "1–2 years old"
(NHANES-derived), so any single-digit-months prevalence claim should be qualified or
cut. AAP's newest clinical report (July 2026) was accessible only through secondary
summaries after repeated primary-access attempts were paywalled (see Sources); the
figures it reports are corroborated across two independent secondary write-ups plus
AAP's own news arm, so I treat them as reliable but flag the access gap explicitly.

3. **Andersson O, Hellström-Westas L, Andersson D, Domellöf M. "Effect of delayed
   versus early umbilical cord clamping on neonatal outcomes and iron status at 4
   months: a randomised controlled trial." BMJ. 2011;343:d7157.**
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22089242/ (resolves).
   *Primary*: the trial's own report, in the journal of record, its own numbers.
   Establishes: 400 full-term infants from a low-risk Swedish pregnancy cohort,

4. **Lozoff B, Jimenez E, Wolf AW. "Long-term developmental outcome of infants with

6. **Lozoff B, Castillo M, Clark KM, Smith JB. "Iron-fortified vs low-iron infant
   formula: developmental outcome at 10 years." Arch Pediatr Adolesc Med.
   2012;166(3):208–215.**
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22064877/ (resolves).
   *Primary*: this is the trial team's own 10-year follow-up report of their RCT,
   restating the original design in its methods.
   This is the article the "harm in iron-replete infants" claim should be pinned to.
   Establishes: 835 healthy, full-term infants enrolled in urban Santiago, Chile,
   1991–1994(1996), randomized at 6 months to iron-fortified formula (mean 12.7
   mg/L) or low-iron formula (mean 2.3 mg/L) from 6 to 12 months; 473 (56.6%) were
   reassessed at 10 years on IQ, spatial memory, arithmetic achievement,
   visual-motor integration, visual perception, and motor functioning. Across the
   whole sample, the iron-fortified group scored lower on every 10-year outcome
   (significant for spatial memory and visual-motor integration; suggestive for the
   rest), 1.4–4.6 points lower, effect sizes 0.13–0.21. The subgroup effect is much
   larger: among children with **high hemoglobin at 6 months (>12.8 g/dL)** —
   i.e., already iron-replete — those randomized to iron-fortified formula scored
   10.7–19.3 points lower at 10 years, effect sizes 0.85–1.36 (large). Conversely,
   children with **low hemoglobin at 6 months (<10.5 g/dL)** who got iron-fortified
   formula scored 2.6–4.5 points *higher*, effect sizes 0.22–0.36 (small but
   favorable). This is a within-trial, hemoglobin-stratified subgroup finding, not a
   separate randomized comparison — treat the subgroup effect sizes as suggestive of
   a dose-by-baseline-status interaction, not as independently randomized proof.
   Locator: PubMed structured abstract, Results field (reproduced with exact
   point-differences and effect-size ranges).

7. **Lozoff B, Castillo M, Clark KM, Smith JB, Sturza J. "Iron supplementation in
   infancy contributes to more adaptive behavior at 10 years of age." J Nutr.
   2014;144(6):838–845.**
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24717366/ (resolves).
   *Primary*: same trial team's own further follow-up analysis, social-emotional
   domain.
   Establishes a genuine complication to the "harm" story: this companion 10-year
   analysis (n=1,123 supplemented, n=534 no-added-iron at follow-up recruitment)
   looked at social-emotional/behavioral outcomes rather than cognitive/motor ones
   and found the iron-fortified group showed *more* adaptive behavior — greater
   cooperation, confidence, persistence, coordination, more smiling/positive affect

   the Chilean cohort's 10-year data cuts two ways depending on which outcome domain
   is scored: worse on cognitive/visual-motor measures (especially in the
   iron-replete subgroup), better on social-emotional measures. This belongs in
   Contradictions.
   Locator: PubMed structured abstract, Results field.

## Contradictions

**1. Association evidence vs. trial evidence — the article's spine.**
Observational cohorts (source 4, Lozoff 1991 NEJM; and its own later extensions not
separately re-verified here) show a consistent association: infants with more severe
iron-deficiency anemia score lower on developmental tests years later, even after
statistical adjustment for socioeconomic confounders. But the best RCT evidence does
not show that supplementing iron in infants who are not iron deficient improves
outcomes, and the largest, longest-followed trial (sources 6–8, the Chilean cohort)
found the opposite in iron-replete infants: those randomized to iron-fortified
formula at 6 months, despite having *good* hemoglobin at baseline, scored lower on
cognitive/visual-motor measures at 10 and 16 years than unsupplemented iron-replete
peers — effect sizes as large as 0.85–1.36 in the high-hemoglobin subgroup at 10
years (source 6). Observational designs cannot separate "iron deficiency causes worse
outcomes" from "whatever caused the iron deficiency (poverty, poor overall nutrition,
lower-quality caregiving environment) also caused the worse outcomes." The Chilean
RCT is the closest thing to a controlled test of the reverse question — does adding
iron help — in infants who did not need it, and its answer is no benefit and a
plausible signal of harm in the already-replete.

**2. The same RCT cuts the other way on a different outcome domain.**
The Chilean cohort's own 10-year social-emotional follow-up (source 7) found the
iron-fortified group showed *more* adaptive behavior (cooperation, positive affect),
not less — effect sizes 0.14–0.36, P<0.05 throughout. This is a real complication,
not resolvable by picking a side: the same trial, same children, same follow-up
year, shows harm on cognitive/visual-motor measures and benefit on social-emotional
measures. Any claim that the Chilean trial shows unqualified "harm" is overstated;
the honest claim is that supplementing iron-replete infants produced a mixed,
domain-dependent pattern, with the cognitive/visual-motor harm signal being larger
in magnitude and the one that has replicated at 16 years (source 8).

**3. Screening guidance disagrees, including within AAP's own history.**
AAP 2010 (source 1) recommended universal hemoglobin screening at 12 months for all
infants. AAP's 2026 report (source 2) replaced that with feeding-stratified,
age-stratified screening — 9–12 months for breastfed infants, 15–18 months for
formula-fed infants after the switch to cow's milk — implicitly conceding the 2010
one-size-fits-all approach didn't track actual risk well. Meanwhile USPSTF has held
since 2015 (source 9, reaffirmed by the evidence review, source 10) that evidence is
*insufficient* to judge whether screening this age group does more good than harm at
all — a formal "I statement," the strongest form of "we don't know" in USPSTF's
scale. AAP recommends a specific screening protocol; USPSTF declines to recommend
for or against screening; the two bodies are not simply using different ages, they
disagree about whether screening is evidence-justified in the first place.

**4. Supplementation guidance disagrees too.**
AAP (sources 1–2) has recommended universal iron supplementation (1 mg/kg/day) for
exclusively breastfed infants starting at 4 months (or, per the 2026 update,
optionally delayed to 6 months when iron-rich complementary foods start) for over a
decade. The 2020–2025 Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee (source 16) concluded
that "routine iron supplementation of all breastfed infants may not be advisable" —
a direct tension with AAP's standing position, not just a difference in emphasis.
WHO (source 15) takes a third position: it favors *targeted* supplementation for
specific higher-risk infants (preterm, low birth weight) over either universal
supplementation or reliance on complementary foods alone, and explicitly states that
even iron-fortified complementary foods, introduced early, don't reliably prevent
IDA in high-risk populations.

**5. What the evidence establishes about reversibility is limited, and the BOND
review (source 12) states this as a field-level gap, not a lapse in any one study**:
biomarkers are reliable for detecting anemia itself but weaker for predicting or
explaining developmental outcomes, which complicates any claim that "fixing the
ferritin number" fixes the underlying developmental risk.

## Source assets

- **Source 6 table (Lozoff 2012, 10-year Chilean outcomes by baseline hemoglobin
  subgroup).** The whole-sample vs. high-Hb-subgroup vs. low-Hb-subgroup point
  differences and effect sizes (whole sample 1.4–4.6 pts/ES 0.13–0.21; high-Hb
  10.7–19.3 pts/ES 0.85–1.36; low-Hb 2.6–4.5 pts/ES 0.22–0.36) would carry the
  article's central contradiction better as a small annotated bar or dot chart than
  as prose — three bars per outcome domain, colored by subgroup, would let a reader
  see the sign flip (harm vs. benefit) at a glance depending on baseline iron
  status. Crop/build must keep all three subgroups (whole sample, high-Hb,
  low-Hb) together in one figure — showing only the high-Hb "harm" bars without the
  low-Hb "benefit" bars would misrepresent the finding as one-directional. Source:
  Lozoff et al. 2012, Results (as reproduced in the PubMed structured abstract,
  source 6 above).
- **Source 13 table (Gupta 2016 NHANES prevalence by age band).** The 1–2y vs. 3–5y
  split (13.5% vs. 3.7% iron deficiency) is a clean, citable age-gradient that could
  support a simple two-bar or small-multiple comparison if the piece wants a
  concrete US number; must retain both age bands and the CIs, not just the point
  estimate, since the CIs are wide enough (e.g., 9.8–17.2 for the 1–2y group) to
  matter to how confidently a number should be stated.
- **Source 3 (Andersson 2011).** None found beyond the two headline numbers already
  in the Numbers table — the trial does not have a public figure/chart asset beyond
  the paywalled BMJ figures, which were not independently viewed this session.
- **Sources 1, 2, 9, 15, 16 (guidance documents).** None found — these are text
  recommendations, not producing a natural chart; a simple comparison table of "who
  recommends what, by age" (already drafted in Contradictions #3–4) is the
  appropriate visual/furniture treatment, not a data chart.

  papers' restatements of its design and findings; I could not locate and confirm a

  Do not cite this paper's numbers directly; source 6 (its own team's 2012 10-year
