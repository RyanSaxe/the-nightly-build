# Voice guide: parenting-research/infant-iron (01)

## Directive

Register: calm authority, not reassurance and not alarm. The house baseline
(Hashimoto precision, Olah/Weng patience in building a concept) already gives
you plain sentences and structure that persuades. What this piece adds on top:
every load-bearing claim carries its own epistemic status, stated in the
sentence that makes it, not hedged once at the top and forgotten. The reader
is a parent who can hold a number and a study design at once and wants to
reason alongside you, not be steered.

Three moves will actually change sentences in this draft:

1. **Label the design where you use the result, not before it and not after
   it.** "Cohort study," "randomized trial," "sibling-comparison design" earn
   their place in the same sentence as the finding they produced, because the
   design is why the finding means what it means. A design named in a
   methods-note aside, disconnected from the claim it licenses, teaches
   nothing.
2. **Every number gets a twin: the relative figure needs its absolute pair,
   and the pair needs a reference the parent already carries** — a well-child
   visit interval, a jar of baby cereal, a number of feedings a day. Not a
   generic "put in context" gesture; the second number must be there, in the
   same breath as the first.
3. **Put the clinician-boundary language to work inside the argument, at the
   exact decision it bounds** (dosing, a diagnosed anemia, a preterm infant),
   not as a closing disclaimer paragraph. A boundary stated where the
   decision actually forks is information. The same sentence moved to the end
   is furniture.

None of this licenses a folksier voice, direct address, or first-person aside.
Two of the four exemplars below reach for those; this piece does not have that
license. Take the structural moves, leave the persona.

## Licenses

```text
form: design-carries-the-claim sentence
move: Jetelina's Tylenol piece and Carroll's mammogram piece both fold the
      study type into the sentence stating its result ("the link disappeared
      when comparing siblings," "that's a relative risk reduction, not an
      absolute one") instead of filing method in a separate clause. It
      deploys wherever a finding's strength depends on how it was produced —
      which is most of this piece's evidence.
bar:  A reader who skips every other sentence should still be able to say
      whether a given claim came from a randomized trial, a cohort, or a
      design that controls for a specific confound, and why that matters for
      how much weight it carries.
```

```text
form: paired-number grounding
move: Carroll's mammogram piece converts a relative figure into its absolute
      form in the same passage (a 28% relative reduction becomes roughly a
      quarter of one percentage point) and stops there — no extra rhetorical
      frame around the conversion. It deploys at the moment a percentage or a
      risk ratio would otherwise stand alone.
bar:  The absolute number and a felt-scale comparison must both be present
      within the same sentence or the one immediately following. A relative
      figure standing alone, anywhere in the piece, fails this license. This
      is not license to reuse the specific pairing shape already retired
      below — vary the sentence that carries the two numbers.
```

```text
form: default-then-exception structure
move: Oster's vitamins piece sets the default position first (most
      supplementation does nothing measurable) and then carves out the
      narrow, evidenced exceptions rather than building the piece as a list
      of maybes. It deploys when a piece is answering a yes/no household
      question that actually has a general answer with specific, named
      carve-outs.
bar:  The exception must be named with the population and the evidence that
      earns it (preterm, low-birth-weight, exclusively breastfed past six
      months without iron-rich foods), not gestured at as "some babies."
```

```text
form: front-loaded finding, then the case for it
move: Jetelina's Tylenol piece states the bottom line before walking the
      evidence that supports it, so an anxious reader gets the calibrated
      answer first and the reasoning is not a cliffhanger. It deploys once,
      at a passage where the piece is resolving the reader's actual worry
      (does correcting iron deficiency change how my child develops).
bar:  The front-loaded line must be exactly as hedged as the evidence
      supports — no more confident than the RCT evidence earns, no more
      alarmed than the observational evidence earns. If the line oversimplifies
      what follows, cut it and let the evidence lead instead.
```

```text
form: named-mechanism confound check
move: Jetelina's Tylenol piece doesn't just say "correlation isn't causation";
      it names the specific confound a design controls for (shared genetics,
      via the sibling comparison) so the reader sees why the association
      shrank. It deploys wherever this piece cites the cohort-vs-RCT split on
      iron and neurodevelopment.
bar:  Naming a design must include what it specifically rules out (which
      confound, which bias), not just that it is "better evidence."
```

## Recently used, do not reuse

The desk's last two pieces both leaned on one mold to pair a large relative
effect with its small absolute counterpart in a single tight clause, and both
opened by qualifying a single trial's population before stating its finding
("in the infants [X] actually tested"). Keep the underlying honesty about
effect size and population; do not reuse either sentence shape. If a sentence
comes out reading like "cuts X by three-quarters, and by four in a hundred,"
or opens on "in the population [trial] actually tested," it is a repeat and
needs a different construction, not a synonym swap.

## Emily Oster, "Vitamins Are (Mostly) Pointless"
Source: https://parentdata.org/vitamins-are-mostly-pointless/
Craft:
- cadence: short declaratives carrying the claim, longer sentences doing the
  explaining; the piece never accelerates into a list where a sentence would
  do.
- argument: inverts the expected default (skepticism, not endorsement) and
  then earns each exception on its own evidence, rather than surveying every
  vitamin evenhandedly.
- evidence: names the study type doing the work ("a large randomized trial")
  and states a null result plainly instead of softening it.
- stance: treats the reader as capable of holding "no evidence this helps"
  without hearing "don't bother" — the piece separates the two.
- notice: catches that self-selection, not the vitamin, drives many
  observational associations — people who take a supplement also do other
  health-conscious things.
- diction: precise gradation between "no convincing evidence" and "good
  evidence," never smoothed to one flavor of uncertainty.
- reader: fellow reasoner, not a student being corrected.
- the move the axes miss: builds the whole piece as a burden-of-proof
  reversal — state the null default, then let each exception justify its own
  admission — which is the shape this piece needs for the supplementation
  question specifically.

## Aaron Carroll, "Relative and absolute risk: Mammogram edition"
Source: https://theincidentaleconomist.com/wordpress/relative-and-absolute-risk-mammogram-edition/
Craft:
- cadence: opens loose, then visibly slows and shortens its sentences at the
  exact moment it isolates the relative/absolute distinction — pacing marks
  the important sentence.
- argument: does not dispute the study's results, isolates how a true number
  gets mispresented, which lets the piece stay generous to the source while
  still correcting the reader's take-away.
- evidence: converts the paper's own percentage into the absolute figure
  inline, in the same breath, arithmetic shown rather than asserted.
- stance: "to the author's credit" before the critique — corrects the
  framing, not the researcher.
- notice: flags that a relative-risk headline figure and an absolute-risk
  reality can differ by more than an order of magnitude, and that the gap is
  the whole reason the number was reported the flashier way.
- diction: plain arithmetic language, no rhetorical inflation around the
  correction itself.
- reader: invited into the calculation, not handed the verdict.
- the move the axes miss: the conversion is the entire craft unit — quote the
  relative number, do the division, state the absolute number, stop. No
  editorializing wrapper around the two figures.

## Katelyn Jetelina (Your Local Epidemiologist), "Tylenol and autism: what parents deserve to know"
Source: https://yourlocalepidemiologist.substack.com/p/tylenol-and-autism-what-parents-deserve
Craft:
- cadence: states the calibrated bottom line early, then spends the piece
  earning it rather than building to a reveal.
- argument: walks from claim origin, to the mixed observational evidence, to
  the design (sibling comparison) that isolates the confound, to what a
  parent does with that.
- evidence: names the specific confound a design rules out (shared genetics,
  via comparing siblings) rather than gesturing at "other factors."
- stance: treats parental worry as legitimate input, not noise to be
  managed down.
- notice: the generic disclaimer at the piece's foot is inert; the clinician
  guidance that actually lands is placed earlier, attached to the specific
  decision (fever in pregnancy) it bears on.
- diction: alternates technical terms (confounding, natural experiment) with
  plain restatements of what each does, so the term is never left to carry
  meaning alone.
- reader: a worried parent owed a straight answer, addressed directly.
- the move the axes miss: the clinician-relevant guidance is load-bearing
  argument, embedded where the decision forks, and the boilerplate disclaimer
  is kept separate and inert — proof that the useful version and the
  boilerplate version can coexist in one piece without contaminating each
  other. This article should follow the same split.

## Vinay Prasad, "In Defense of the Randomized Trial"
Source: https://www.sensible-med.com/p/in-defense-of-the-randomized-trial
Craft:
- cadence: builds by escalation — historical puzzle, then a list of
  problems, then the single mechanism that resolves them all.
- argument: makes one structural claim (randomization solves several
  distinct problems — confounding, selection bias, unmeasured time effects —
  at once) rather than a scattered list of why RCTs are "better."
- evidence: names the specific failure modes an observational design cannot
  rule out, then states that only randomization clears all of them
  simultaneously.
- stance: exasperated and combative — not usable here; the house register
  for this piece stays even-tempered where Prasad runs hot.
- notice: catches that intuitive, mechanism-based reasoning ("this makes
  sense") has a track record of being wrong on its own, independent of any
  specific trial.
- diction: shifts registers between technical precision and blunt dismissal;
  only the technical half transfers.
- reader: addressed as someone who should already agree, which this piece
  cannot do to a parent weighing a real decision.
- the move the axes miss (transferable part only): naming, in one place,
  everything a given study design cannot control for — not just "cohort
  studies show correlation" but the specific list of what stays uncontrolled
  — is the craft to take. Leave the temperature at the door.

## Self-test

A writer following only the house default would still separate fact from
estimate and cite its sources — that default already demands it. What it does
not supply on its own: putting the study-design label inside the claim
sentence every time, pairing every relative figure with its absolute twin and
a comparison in the same breath, resolving the household worry with a
front-loaded, precisely-hedged line before walking the evidence, and moving
the clinician-boundary language out of a closing disclaimer and into the
sentence where the decision actually forks. Those four habits are what should
make this piece read differently from a house-default explainer that happens
to be about iron.
