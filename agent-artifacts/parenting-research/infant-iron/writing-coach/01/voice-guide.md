# Voice guide: parenting-research/infant-iron

Register: a calm, exact explainer, not an alarm and not a hedge. The reader is
an evidence-literate parent who can hold a study design in mind once it is
named; write to that reader, never down to a lay stereotype of one.

Moves that will change sentences in this draft:

- Give association and trial their own sentences. Each evidentiary claim gets
  its own subject (the cohort study, the Chilean trial, the meta-analysis)
  and its own verb of confidence (found, showed no difference, was
  underpowered to detect). Never let one sentence's verb quietly lend its
  certainty to the next fact. When a paragraph moves from an observational
  finding to a trial finding, start a new sentence and change the subject,
  not just the verb.
- Attach every number to its twin. State a relative figure and its absolute
  counterpart with the denominator in the same breath, the way a reader can
  do the arithmetic without reaching for a calculator: not "cut risk by X%"
  alone, but how many babies out of how many, and how many saw no change. A
  confidence interval gets stated as a range of plausible truth, not
  dropped as a parenthetical the sentence doesn't use. If a trial lacked the
  power to detect a difference, say what size of effect it could and could
  not have ruled out, rather than reporting only "no significant difference"
  as if absence of evidence were evidence of absence.
- Name the exact source every time evidence enters the piece: the study, its
  population (term, healthy, breastfed; preterm; low-birth-weight), the
  nutrient, the dose, the age in months. "Research shows" and "studies find"
  are not allowed to carry a claim; a named source is.
- Split sentences that both report and interpret. A single sentence that
  states what a trial found and what it means for a household is doing two
  jobs; give the finding its sentence and the interpretation its own,
  usually its own paragraph slot, so the reader always knows which one they
  are reading.
- Write the "what this might change at home" material in a plainly different
  register from the evidence sentences: direct, imperative, second-person
  where the guidance is truly general (offer iron-rich first foods from six
  months), and explicit about the handoff where it is not (a preterm baby, a
  restrictive diet, suspected anemia is a clinician's question, stated as an
  instruction to ask one, not folded into a hedge inside an evidence
  sentence).
- Hold a firm stance without partisanship toward any guideline body. The
  piece can say a recommendation outran its evidence, or that a screening
  question remains genuinely open, in declarative sentences, without
  editorializing about the body that issued it.
- Prefer the concrete noun to the abstract one at every joint: the nutrient's
  name, not "this micronutrient"; the trial's population and year, not
  "research in this area." Short, single-purpose sentences carry the
  causal-versus-associational distinction better than a longer sentence
  trying to hold both.

Recently used, do not reuse: opening on a striking relative-vs-absolute
number by reflex; headlining the effect size; the "the trials built to catch
harm found none" shape; comma-triad deks.

## Melinda Wenner Moyer, "Why Almost Everything Dean Ornish Says about Nutrition Is Wrong"
Source: https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-almost-everything-dean-ornish-says-about-nutrition-is-wrong/
Craft:
- cadence: Long explanatory sentences that set up a claim, then a short
  declarative sentence that cuts it down. The short sentence always lands
  after the reader has the material to feel its force, not before.
- argument: Builds a methodological hierarchy explicitly before using it:
  she states that observational studies show association, not causation,
  and names the mechanism (confounding) before she uses that standard to
  discount a source. The standard is argued, not asserted.
- evidence: Attributes the harder methodological point to a named expert
  rather than asserting it in her own voice, then applies it herself in the
  sentences that follow, which lets her keep an even tone while still
  landing a hard verdict.
- stance: Respectfully adversarial. She grants the opposing side a genuine
  concession (the diet's practical merits) before contesting its causal
  claim, which earns the contest that follows.
- notice: She catches the sleight of hand in a single opening statistic and
  spends the rest of the piece unpacking why it doesn't carry the weight put
  on it, rather than listing new counter-facts.
- diction: Direct evaluative words used sparingly and precisely, "tenuous at
  best," "misleading," so that when they land they read as earned, not
  reflexive.
- reader: Treats the reader as someone who can follow a methodological
  argument step by step if each step is built before it is used; never
  simplifies the distinction itself, only the path to it.
- the move the axes miss: she closes by naming her own position relative to
  the dispute ("I have no horse in this race") instead of a summary line,
  which converts the whole piece's stance from opinion to disinterested
  analysis in one sentence.
Calibration: "Randomized controlled clinical trials, although certainly not
perfect, are better tools for chipping away at causality."

## Aaron E. Carroll, "Relative and absolute risk: Mammogram edition"
Source: https://theincidentaleconomist.com/wordpress/relative-and-absolute-risk-mammogram-edition/
Craft:
- cadence: States the impressive number first, lets it land, then pivots
  hard with a short corrective sentence before rebuilding the honest number
  underneath it. The pivot sentence is always short.
- argument: Converts a relative-risk claim into a number-needed-to-treat,
  then keeps repeating the same denominator until the reader cannot escape
  it. Repetition of one concrete figure is the entire argument.
- evidence: Does the arithmetic on the page rather than citing a conclusion.
  The reader watches 100/368 become 0.27%, so the honest number feels
  derived, not asserted.
- stance: Not debunking the finding, reframing what it is entitled to mean.
  He validates the reader's first reaction to the big number before
  complicating it, so the correction doesn't read as an accusation of
  gullibility.
- notice: Flags what coverage of a study leaves out, specifically which of
  two true numbers gets reported and which doesn't, and ties that omission
  directly to how a reader would decide differently with the other one.
- diction: Plain arithmetic language, bolded for the single word that
  carries the reversal ("no benefit"), no technical hedge words.
- reader: Assumes the reader can do the division once it is shown, and
  respects them enough to show it rather than summarize it.
- the move the axes miss: he never says the flashier number is wrong. He
  says what it is a true answer to a different question than the one the
  reader is actually asking, which is the honest way to hold two accurate
  numbers apart.
Calibration: "You'd need to invite 368 women to screening for one breast
cancer death to be prevented... of the 368 women you invite, 367 will see no
benefit."

## Hilda Bastian, "Statistical Significance and Its Part in Science Downfalls"
Source: https://absolutelymaybe.plos.org/2013/11/11/statistical-significance-and-its-part-in-science-downfalls/
Craft:
- cadence: Opens with an imagined ideal ("Imagine if there were a simple
  single statistical measure...") to state what the reader wishes were true
  before showing why it isn't. The wish and its correction are two
  paragraphs apart, giving the correction room to land.
- argument: Builds from a single misreading (p<0.05 as "95% certain") to the
  general principle by way of one worked case, not a list of rules.
- evidence: Treats a confidence interval as more informative than the
  p-value it's paired with and shows why with a concrete comparison of what
  each one tells you and what it doesn't, rather than asserting a
  preference.
- stance: Self-deprecating ("I'm not a statistician") without ceding
  authority, an admission that lowers the barrier to the explanation that
  follows rather than undercutting it.
- notice: Catches that "significant" gets read as "important" and separates
  the two explicitly, naming the failure mode before explaining the fix.
- diction: Colloquial connective phrases ("Therein lies the rub," "Except
  that you can't") mark the turns in the argument the way a spoken
  explanation would, without loosening the precision of the claims around
  them.
- reader: Addresses the reader's likely prior belief directly and by name,
  then dismantles it in the reader's own terms, not a statistician's.
- the move the axes miss: she keeps returning to what a lone number cannot
  tell you without prior knowledge of the field, so single-study certainty
  never gets a foothold even while she's explaining the tool correctly.
Calibration: "Significant doesn't mean it's important either. A sliver of an
effect can reach the less-than-5% threshold."
