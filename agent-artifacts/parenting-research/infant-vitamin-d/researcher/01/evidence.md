# Evidence record: parenting-research/infant-vitamin-d (01)

The evidence supports the commissioned angle: the case for giving a breastfed
infant 400 IU/day of vitamin D is strong and narrow, and the broader health
halo is not carried by the trials. The narrow case rests on physiology and
consensus, not large randomized trials of hard endpoints. Human milk from a
mother taking the standard 400 IU/day carries under 25 to 78 IU/L of vitamin D
(AAP 2008), far below an infant's requirement; unsupplemented breastfed infants
develop low 25(OH)D and can develop nutritional rickets; and 400 IU/day is the
dose shown to hold serum 25(OH)D above 50 nmol/L, the level the Institute of
Medicine judged adequate for bone health "for practically all individuals." The
recommendation's owners (AAP, IOM/NAM, and the 2016 global rickets consensus)
all set the target at 400 IU/day for deficiency and rickets prevention. Every
place the higher-dose or broader-benefit case has been tested in a randomized
trial, it fails: higher doses (800-1600 IU) buy no extra bone at age 3 (Gallo
follow-up), no bone strength or infection benefit through age 2 (VIDI), and no
neurodevelopmental benefit (VIDI/Tuovinen); infant vitamin D did not reduce
allergy (Rueter); and both the IOM (2011) and, three years earlier, the AAP
(2008, in cautious "potential"/"may eventually" language) judged non-skeletal
benefits unproven. The record's thinnest point is the strong endpoint itself:
because nutritional rickets is now rare, the RCT base for the deficiency/rickets
claim is small (the Cochrane review found only two trials reporting biochemical
rickets and rated bone-health evidence insufficient), so the narrow case leans
on physiology, historical experience, and 25(OH)D as a surrogate rather than on
trials counting rickets cases. A second live thread is definitional: what counts
as "deficient" is contested (50 vs 75 nmol/L), and the higher target is the
entire basis for the higher-dose argument.

Conversion used throughout, from AAP 2008: nmol/L divided by 2.496 = ng/mL
(so 50 nmol/L = 20 ng/mL; 75 nmol/L = 30 ng/mL; 30 nmol/L = 12 ng/mL).

## Contradictions

- **What counts as "deficient" is contested, and that gap is the whole
  higher-dose argument.** The IOM (2011) set bone adequacy at 20 ng/mL (50
  nmol/L) and warned that deficiency is over-diagnosed because labs use higher
  cut-points. The Gallo trial and higher-dose advocates aim at 75 nmol/L (30
  ng/mL); only 1600 IU/day reaches that bar in nearly all infants. So the
  higher-dose case is really a claim about the target, not about a demonstrated
  outcome. The AAP 2008 itself records that "consensus has not been reached with
  regard to the concentration of 25-OH-D to define vitamin D insufficiency for
  infants and children." This is the record's central tension and belongs in the
  article's spine.

- **The higher target does not translate into a hard-endpoint benefit.** The
  steelman for 1600 IU (Gallo: it alone reaches 75 nmol/L) is broken by the same
  trial line and by VIDI: extra 25(OH)D bought no extra bone mineral content at
  age 3 (Gallo follow-up), no bone strength or infection benefit through 24
  months (VIDI), and no neurodevelopment gain (Tuovinen). And 1600 IU exceeds
  the IOM infant UL of 1,000 IU (0-6 months). The higher number is achievable and
  inert on outcomes tested so far.

- **The respiratory endpoint splits by population and by question.** VIDI found
  no infection benefit from a higher dose in vitamin D-sufficient healthy term
  infants. Hibbs' D-Wheeze found sustained 400 IU (vs withholding once formula
  supplied 200 IU) cut recurrent wheeze in Black preterm infants, a
  deficiency-prone group. Read together they do not conflict: the D-Wheeze signal
  supports the deficiency-prevention frame (supplement the at-risk vs let them go
  short), on a soft endpoint, in a special population — not the general
  higher-dose/broad-benefit claim. The writer should not present D-Wheeze as
  evidence that vitamin D "prevents wheeze" for a healthy term breastfed infant.

- **The health halo shrank as the evidence grew.** AAP 2008 floated immune,
  diabetes, and cancer roles as "potential." The IOM in 2011, after reviewing
  1,000+ studies, judged those same non-skeletal outcomes inconclusive and
  unreliable. More evidence narrowed the claim rather than widening it.

- **An alternative dosing route exists but is not endorsed.** AAP 2008 records
  pilot data (Hollis/Wagner) that giving the mother high-dose vitamin D (up to
  6400 IU/day) can raise a breastfed infant's 25(OH)D about as much as giving the
  infant 300-400 IU directly. This is a real steelman for "dose the mother
  instead," but the AAP explicitly declines to recommend it universally pending
  larger safety validation, so direct infant supplementation remains the
  standing advice.

- **The strong endpoint is strong in physiology and consensus, weak in RCT
  count.** No contradiction to the recommendation, but a caveat the writer must
  not paper over: because rickets is now rare, the deficiency/rickets claim rests
  on milk vitamin D content, 25(OH)D as a surrogate, historical cod-liver-oil
  experience, and observational rickets series — not on large randomized trials
  counting rickets cases (Cochrane found only two trials reporting biochemical
  rickets).
