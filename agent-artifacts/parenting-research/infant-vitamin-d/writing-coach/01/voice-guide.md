# Voice guide: parenting-research/infant-vitamin-d (01)

## Directive

Register: a peer who has read the studies grading them for the reader, not a
reassurer delivering a verdict. Every claim about what supplementation does or
does not do carries its evidence type in the same breath — trial or
observational, and what the trial actually measured. The reader can already
read a study; do not translate methodology into lay hand-waving, and do not
withhold the number that would let the reader judge magnitude themselves.

Reader relationship: earned trust through visible grading, not through tone.
The piece does not tell the reader deficiency prevention is "strong" and
bone-density claims are "weak" — it shows the trial design and lets the
difference be visible before naming it.

Moves that change sentences here, beyond the house default:

- Tag every evidence-strength claim with its source type and endpoint inline,
  at the sentence that makes the claim — not in a separate methods aside.
  "An RCT measuring 25(OH)D status" and "an observational cohort tracking
  fracture rates years later" are different sentences doing different jobs;
  never let one borrow the other's confidence.
- When a risk or benefit is rare, give the denominator in the same sentence
  or the next. "Rare" unsupported by a rate is an adjective doing a number's
  job.
- Ground the mechanism before the recommendation. State why breastmilk is low
  in vitamin D and why a given trial's endpoint is a surrogate or a hard
  outcome before using either as evidence — the reasoning should make the
  conclusion feel derived, not asserted.
- Report a null or small effect by stating the actual figures against what
  the reader would expect, and stop. Let the gap between expectation and
  number do the work; do not add a verdict adjective on top of it.

## Licenses

```text
form: denominator framing for a rare outcome
move: Oster cites rickets case counts against total births in the same
      window ("166 cases" against "approximately 68 million births") to let
      the reader calibrate rarity themselves rather than being told a number
      is small.
bar:  any rate or frequency claim in this piece states its denominator in
      the same sentence or the next. A bare "rare," "common," or "small
      risk" without the number that earns it is cut.
```

```text
form: mechanism-before-recommendation ordering
move: Oster explains why breastmilk is low in vitamin D (evolutionary sun
      exposure, modern indoor life) before stating the supplementation
      recommendation, so the recommendation reads as derived from the
      mechanism rather than handed down.
bar:  used once, at the point the 400 IU recommendation is introduced, and
      only to explain a specific causal step the argument needs next — not
      as scene-setting or a general biology detour.
```

```text
form: inline evidence-type tag on the claim it supports
move: Carroll and Meyerowitz-Katz name what kind of study produced a number
      at the sentence that reports it — an RCT's measured outcome versus an
      observational association's endpoint — rather than sorting evidence
      quality into a separate methods paragraph the reader must hold onto.
bar:  every sentence asserting evidence strength (strong, weak, absent)
      names the study type and what it actually measured in that same
      sentence. A strength claim without its evidence type attached is cut.
```

```text
form: flat effect-size reporting on a null or small result
move: Carroll reports a trial's actual numbers (e.g., 1.05 infections in the
      high-dose group against 1.03 in the standard-dose group) without an
      adjective doing the interpreting, letting the closeness of the figures
      itself carry the finding.
bar:  used only for reporting a null or small effect the argument is
      building on; states the compared figures and stops — no "essentially
      no difference" or similar verdict word layered on top.
```

## Recently used, do not reuse

- Do not run infant-iron's spine: "supplement given before deficiency shows
  no measured benefit." This article's deficiency-prevention case is strong,
  not absent — the shape here is the opposite claim and needs its own
  structure.
- Do not run nirsevimab's relative-vs-absolute-risk move as the article's
  organizing spine. A comparison table separating well-supported from
  weakly-supported endpoints is fine; reusing the specific "big relative
  number, small absolute one" rhetorical turn as the piece's engine is not.
- Avoid the comma-triad dek mold and the semicolon-reversal dek
  (`spec/headlines.md` bans both house-wide; check they have not crept back
  in via a strong sentence reused as the dek).

## Emily Oster, "Do I Need to Give My Breastfed Baby Vitamin D Drops?"
Source: https://parentdata.org/do-i-need-to-give-my-breastfed-baby-vitamin-d-drops
Craft:
- cadence: short declaratives carrying the claim, followed by one longer
  sentence that supplies the mechanism or the number; she does not stack
  qualifiers inside a single sentence.
- argument: states the apparent contradiction (breastmilk is "complete," yet
  needs a supplement) and resolves it by mechanism rather than assertion —
  the resolution is the argument.
- evidence: leads with the rickets case-count-over-births ratio before
  characterizing the risk in words, so the reader's own arithmetic produces
  "rare," not her adjective.
- stance: matter-of-fact about uncertainty — states plainly that a step in
  the causal chain (drops to measured bone outcomes) lacks direct evidence,
  without softening it or over-flagging it as scandalous.
- notice: catches that "no downside" claims still need their own scrutiny —
  she treats supplement safety as a claim requiring support, not a default.
- diction: plain nouns for the biology (sunlight, skin, milk), no jargon
  substituted for the mechanism itself.
- reader: a parent who wants the reasoning, not the reassurance — the piece
  never says "don't worry," it shows why worry is or isn't warranted.
- the move the axes miss: she resolves practical noncompliance (missed
  drops) with the same evidence-based calm as the main claim, instead of
  switching into a softer "advice" register for the practical tail. The
  register does not change when the content turns practical.

## Aaron Carroll, "Seriously? Still with the Vitamin D?"
Source: https://theincidentaleconomist.com/wordpress/seriously-still-with-the-vitamin-d/
Craft:
- cadence: alternates a short skeptical aside with a data-bearing sentence
  immediately after — the skepticism is earned by the number that follows
  it, not left to float alone.
- argument: builds by walking a specific trial's arm-by-arm results, then
  measuring them against what the hypothesis being tested predicted, and
  finding the gap.
- evidence: gives the compared numbers from both trial arms in the same
  sentence, so the reader sees the closeness directly rather than being told
  a p-value crossed a threshold.
- stance: openly incredulous at claims outrunning their evidence, but the
  incredulity always attaches to a specific number he just showed, never to
  the subject in general.
- notice: catches when a guideline body's own threshold (e.g., the level it
  calls adequate) undercuts the claim being made about needing more.
- diction: colloquial admissions ("I have no idea who thinks this stuff up")
  used sparingly, bracketing rather than replacing the technical sentence
  beside it.
- reader: assumes the reader can hold a trial's arms and endpoints without
  translation, and spends no sentence re-explaining what an RCT is.
- the move the axes miss: he names the specific mechanism by which a
  correlation gets mistaken for a causal claim (people with a healthy trait
  also happen to have higher levels of the thing being sold) before
  dismantling the specific study, so the general failure mode and the
  specific instance both land.

## Gideon Meyerowitz-Katz, "There's No Such Thing As A Vitamin D Deficiency — Part 4"
Source: https://gidmk.medium.com/theres-no-such-thing-as-a-vitamin-d-deficiency-part-4-981078dfe547
Craft:
- cadence: short opening assertions followed by a qualifying clause that
  narrows the claim's scope immediately, before the reader can over-read it.
- argument: works by naming the exceptions to a broad null finding first
  (the specific groups where supplementation does have support), then
  explaining why the general population differs from those groups.
- evidence: leans on the source guideline body's own scope statement (who a
  recommendation covers and who it excludes) as the evidence for where a
  claim's strength ends.
- stance: frank about an evidence base's failure to support a popular
  belief, without treating the popular belief or its holders as foolish —
  the target is the claim, never the person making it.
- notice: catches that "high levels correlate with better health" and
  "raising levels with a pill improves health" are different claims resting
  on different evidence, and states the difference as the pivot of the
  piece rather than a caveat appended after the conclusion.
- diction: plain, sometimes wry, single memorable phrases used to mark a
  boundary ("everyone's favourite supplement") rather than to editorialize
  a is finding.
- reader: treated as capable of tracking a multi-part argument across a
  series without re-summary; each part assumes the last.
- the move the axes miss: he separates "this claim has support" from "this
  claim has support for you" by explicit population — age, latitude,
  baseline status — making the boundary of a recommendation as load-bearing
  as the recommendation itself.

## Self-test

A default house piece would already cite sources, keep reported fact and
synthesis distinct, and avoid unsupported claims of strength. It would not,
on its own, reliably attach evidence type to every strength claim in the same
sentence, supply a denominator every time it calls something rare, sequence
mechanism before recommendation at the 400 IU claim, or report a null result
in bare figures without a verdict word. Those four moves are what this guide
adds — they are the difference between an article that says the deficiency
case is strong and the halo case is weak, and one that shows the reader the
study design and the arithmetic that makes each claim what it is.
