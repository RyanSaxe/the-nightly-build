# Commission: parenting-research/starting-daycare

## The decision and why it is near now

The child is six months old this August, the age when many households return to
work and choose group daycare over home care. The decision is real and
evidence-bearing: group care changes a baby's infection exposure immediately and
may bear on later respiratory, allergic, cognitive, and behavioral outcomes, and
the evidence on each of those runs from fairly firm to genuinely contested. The
desk's job is to say what the research supports, how strongly, and what it cannot
establish, then land on what it might change for one family and where the
question becomes a clinician's.

The article should organize around what the evidence can actually carry:

- The near-certain, near-term effect: children in group care get more respiratory
  and gastrointestinal infections and more otitis media in the first year or two
  than children cared for at home. Give the reader the size of that effect from
  the studies that measured it, and the mechanism a parent can act on.
- The contested medium-term claims, steelmanned before they are weighed: whether
  early group care shifts later asthma, wheeze, and allergy, where the "more
  early wheeze, maybe less later asthma" reading comes from, and how much of it
  survives confounding and more recent evidence.
- The developmental evidence, which is where study design bites hardest: what the
  large longitudinal child-care studies found about cognitive and behavioral
  outcomes, how small the effects are, and how completely quality of care and
  family selection bound any causal reading.

Then the desk close: what a family might reasonably change (exposure timing,
what to ask a provider, when to expect the infection months), and the line where
an individual situation is a safety or medical matter for a pediatrician, for
instance an immunocompromised infant or a premature one.

## Template, sources, tags

- Template: `article`. 1200 to 3000 words. Min sources: 8.
- Sources must include the primary studies that own each figure: the daycare-
  infection cohorts, the daycare-and-asthma/allergy evidence and at least one
  study or review that complicates it, and the large longitudinal child-care
  study for the developmental claims. Read each to the cited passage; verify
  every rate, ratio, and denominator against the primary.
- Where a shape helps, a table or chart earns its place (for instance infection
  rates by care setting, or an effect that reverses over time). Furniture carries
  evidence, never decoration.
- Tags: child-development, infectious-disease, evidence (metadata only).

## Distinct value and habits not to inherit

This desk keeps opening on a counterintuitive reversal that relocates the danger
("The dangerous part of teething is the medicine cabinet"); do not open
starting-daycare on that mold. The recent deks lean on a three-clause,
comma-and-"and" list, which `spec/headlines.md` bans outright; the dek here is
one lean claim. Nirsevimab already covered RSV as a preventable single risk, so
where group care raises RSV exposure, note it and move on rather than re-arguing
that piece. Recent articles run short sections with a single table and a closing
safety note; keep the safety close the desk requires, but let this piece's
section shape follow its own argument, since it carries more contested evidence
than a teething explainer does.

## Boundaries

One article, this slug. Cover the daycare decision through the research, not
generic parenting advice and not a providers' how-to. The desk does not stand in
for individual medical care; say so where it applies. Stay within the word band.
