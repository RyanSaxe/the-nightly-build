# evidence: parenting-research/starting-daycare (01)

The record supports the commission's three-tier structure, but it forces two
corrections the writer must carry. The near-term infection effect is firm for
respiratory infections and otitis media and is measured with matching sign and
magnitude across a Quebec cohort (Côté 2010) and two Dutch cohort analyses (de
Hoog 2014, Enserink 2016): early group care raises these infections by roughly
40 to 60 percent during the first two to three years, then the excess narrows
and in several outcomes reverses to a protective rate by school age. The
correction is that the gastrointestinal half of the "near-certain" claim is far
smaller than the respiratory half. Côté found no significant GI effect at any
age, and the one cohort built to measure gastroenteritis found the first-year
increase was only 13 percent (IRR 1.13), not the two- to three-fold figure the
older review literature repeats. The asthma and allergy tier is genuinely
contested and, weighed across a meta-analysis of 32 studies, does not support a
durable protective effect: the well-known Tucson finding of less asthma with
early daycare (Ball 2000) is not reproduced at age 8 in the larger PIAMA cohort
(Caudri 2009, aOR 0.99) nor pooled for children 6 and older (Swartz 2019, aOR
0.98, not significant). What survives is a shift in the timing of wheeze, not a
change in asthma itself. The developmental tier is where design bites hardest:
the NICHD studies are observational, the effects are small (quality-of-care
effects on cognition of d = .08 to .16), parenting is the stronger predictor,
and family selection into care is never fully closed. No randomized evidence
exists for any of these questions in the general population. The record's
weakest points are the exact standardized coefficient for the hours-of-care
behavior effect, which I could not extract to a number, and the widely repeated
claim that a pediatric body advises high-risk infants to avoid group care in RSV
season, which I could not confirm in any accessible primary.

## Contradictions

- Respiratory-infection excess reverses, but healthcare use may not fully
  even out. Côté (respiratory IRR falls to 0.79, ear to 0.57 by ages 5-8) and
  Enserink (gastroenteritis protection persists to age 6) support a clean
  timing-shift reading. De Hoog complicates it: episode counts largely offset
  over six years (URTI+AOM IRR 1.08, not significant), yet GP consultations
  (IRR 1.15) and specialist referrals (HR 1.43) stayed raised, most clearly for
  6-12 month entrants. The reassuring "it all evens out" reading holds for
  infection counts more cleanly than for downstream medical use.
- The size of the gastrointestinal effect. The commission and the older review
  literature call daycare a near-certain cause of more GI infection, with review
  articles citing roughly three times the diarrheal-illness rate. The cohort
  built to measure it found a first-year increase of only 13 percent (IRR 1.13)
  and no net six-year excess, and Côté found no significant GI effect at all.
  The alarming reading rests on cross-sectional and clinic-based reviews; the
  prospective cohorts bound the effect far lower.
- Asthma protection: Tucson versus everyone since. Ball 2000 found daycare in
  the first six months protective against asthma (RR 0.4), the strongest single
  reassuring result. Its confidence interval reached 1.0, the cohort was 1,035
  children, and the finding is not reproduced: Caudri (n=3,963) found aOR 0.99
  at age 8, and Swartz's pooled estimate for ages 6-18 is 0.98 (not significant).
  What replicates is the wheeze timing-shift (Swartz: OR 1.80 under age 2, 0.43
  at 6-18), not a lasting change in asthma diagnosis. The hygiene-hypothesis
  mechanism Ball proposed is the contested part.
- Developmental effects: real and persistent, or small and confounded. The
  alarming reading (more center-care hours predict more externalizing, risk-
  taking, and impulsivity, and the effect persists to 15) and the reassuring
  reading (effects are d = .08 to .16, parenting matters more, and no randomized
  evidence rules out family selection) both draw on the same NICHD data. Neither
  side can invoke a randomized trial; the design cannot separate the care from
  the families who choose it.
- Steelmanning each side at the decision point. The reassuring case: the
  infection excess is time-limited and offsets, early care buys later respiratory
  and GI protection, and the developmental downsides are small and swamped by
  parenting. The alarming case: for a 6-12 month entrant the infection months are
  real and the medical-use excess may not fully reverse, the promised asthma
  protection does not survive the larger cohorts, and the behavior effects are
  small but persistent and were measured, not modeled away.

## Coverage check against the brief

- Near-term infection effect with sizes, age window, and later narrowing/reversal:
  answered (Côté for respiratory and otitis; Enserink and de Hoog for GI and
  healthcare use). Rates, ratios, and denominators verified against each primary.
- Contested asthma/wheeze/allergy, "more early wheeze, maybe less later asthma"
  origin plus a source that complicates it: answered (Ball for the origin; Caudri
  and Swartz complicate and narrow it; the asthma-protection claim does not
  survive).
- Developmental evidence, specific outcomes and magnitudes, quality and selection
  bounds: answered (Belsky for direction, Vandell for magnitude d=.08-.16 and the
  correlational caveat). Gap recorded: exact hours-to-behavior standardized
  coefficient not captured to a number.
- Design limits and pediatric-body guidance including higher-risk cases: answered
  in part. No randomized evidence exists for any tier; all cohorts are
  observational with family selection, provider quality, breastfeeding, and
  siblings as the bounding confounders (breastfeeding and siblings both appear
  as adjusted covariates in Côté and as effect modifiers in Caudri). The AAP
  bronchiolitis guideline supplies the higher-risk flag (KAS 1b) and two
  mitigations (hand hygiene, breastfeeding). Unresolved: no accessible primary
  confirms a pediatric body advising high-risk infants to avoid group care in RSV
  season; the writer should not attribute that specific recommendation without
  one.
