# Voice guide: parenting-research/starting-daycare (01)

## How this piece should sound

The reader is a parent who can read a hazard ratio and wants to know how much
weight the daycare evidence will bear, not to be calmed or alarmed. Hold the
register calm and exact. State uncertainty as a fact about the studies, the way
Nuzzo states what a P value can and cannot report, so that "we don't know yet"
reads as a measurement of the evidence rather than as either worry or comfort.

The article moves from a near-certain near-term effect to two bodies of
contested medium-term evidence, and the exemplars show moves each stretch can
use. Where the piece cites the infection effect, it can give the effect size
next to what bounds it in the same breath, the way Carroll follows the 29
percent figure with the dose, the food, and the study type: the caveat sharpens
the number instead of dissolving it. Where a developmental effect is real but
small, it can put the actual magnitude on a scale the reader can picture, the
way Nuzzo writes a rate as 7.67 down to 5.96 percent, so that a statistically
significant result is not read as a large one.

On the contested asthma, wheeze, and allergy question, the piece can say how it
is weighing the literature before it weighs it, as Carroll tells the reader why
he trusts a systematic review over a single striking study. Naming the appraisal
lets a quantitatively literate reader check the reasoning, not only the verdict.
Where a finding's meaning turns on confounding or on which families select into
group care, the piece can build the idea that a result depends on the prior
likelihood and the population, the way Gawande builds the same tracing read two
ways before the sentence that needs the concept.

The commission asks for a close that weighs a real decision. Where the evidence
lets the piece carry an argument to a verdict, it can do the arithmetic in plain
sentences the way Gawande sets a per-year benefit against an up-front risk over a
stated horizon, and land the judgment the numbers have already settled rather
than stopping at "there is a tradeoff." A verdict earned this way needs no
hedge. Where the situation turns medical, an immunocompromised or premature
infant, the piece marks the line and hands it to a clinician, and that handoff
is a statement about scope, not a disclaimer bolted on.

## Regina Nuzzo, "Scientific method: Statistical errors"

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/506150a

> "Consider Motyl's study about political extremists. Most scientists would look
> at his original P value of 0.01 and say that there was just a 1% chance of his
> result being a false alarm. But they would be wrong. The P value cannot say
> this: all it can do is summarize the data assuming a specific null hypothesis.
> It cannot work backwards and make statements about the underlying reality."

She states the reading most people would give the number, then says in one flat
sentence that it is wrong, and shows what the statistic actually reports. Nuzzo
is a statistician writing for people who are not, and she trusts the reader to
follow a correction that is stated rather than softened. The precision about what
the measure can and cannot do is where her training shows on the page.

> "That might have sounded impressive, but the effects were actually tiny:
> meeting online nudged the divorce rate from 7.67% down to 5.96%, and barely
> budged happiness from 5.48 to 5.64 on a 7-point scale."

A finding can be real and still small, and she shows it by setting the actual
numbers next to the impression they created. Giving the divorce rate as 7.67 to
5.96 percent, and happiness as two points on a stated seven-point scale, lets the
reader judge the size without being told what to conclude. The writer's judgment
is carried by the figures she chose to print.

## Atul Gawande, "Overkill"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2015/05/11/overkill-atul-gawande

> "The doctors didn't say, but carotid surgery in a patient like Bruce's father
> reduces stroke risk by about one percentage point per year. Therefore, it
> would take fifteen years before the benefit of the operation would exceed the
> fifteen-per-cent risk of the operation. And he had a life expectancy far
> shorter than that—very likely just two or three years. The potential benefits
> of the procedures were dwarfed by their risks."

Gawande does the arithmetic in front of the reader: a one-point-per-year benefit
against a fifteen-percent up-front risk, over a horizon shorter than the benefit
needs to arrive. He is a surgeon, and the verdict in the last sentence is one he
has earned by laying out the two numbers and the time they play out over. Nothing
is hedged, because the calculation has already settled it.

> "What's more, the value of any test depends on how likely you are to be having
> a significant problem in the first place. If you have crushing chest pain and
> shortness of breath, you start with a high likelihood of having a serious heart
> condition, and an electrocardiogram has significant value. A heart tracing that
> doesn't look quite right usually means trouble. But, if you have no signs or
> symptoms of heart trouble, an electrocardiogram adds no useful information; a
> heart tracing that doesn't look quite right is mostly noise."

He builds one idea, that a test's value depends on the prior likelihood of the
problem, and pays it off with the same tracing read twice, once in a patient with
symptoms and once in a patient without. The concept is set up before the sentence
that needs it, which is the patience of someone used to teaching. The reader can
check the claim against the worked example instead of taking it on trust.

## Aaron E. Carroll, "There's a reason I use systematic reviews and meta-analyses"

Source: https://theincidentaleconomist.com/wordpress/theres-a-reason-i-use-systematic-reviews-and-meta-analyses/

> "As I acknowledge in my Upshot piece, a 2013 meta-analysis found that people
> who eat a lot of red meat had a 29% relative increase in all-cause mortality.
> But even then, you gotta dig into the details. Most of this was driven by
> processed red meats (ie bacon, sausage or salami), and you had to eat at least
> 1-2 servings (with an "s") of red meat a DAY to be considered eating "a lot".
> And it's still epidemiological data."

Carroll gives the effect size, a 29 percent relative increase, and then in the
same breath everything that bounds it: which meats drove it, how much you had to
eat, and that the data are observational. The register is chatty ("you gotta dig
into the details"), and the caveats stay specific instead of settling into one
general hedge. What carries to a serious paper is that discipline, a concrete
bound attached to each number, rather than the tone.

> "The problem with using only epidemiologic data is that it's sometimes easy to
> find a study in the scientific literature that supports any belief. So if you
> want to be "proven" right, you can certainly cherry pick to prove your point."

Carroll names how he reads a contested literature before he reads it, telling the
reader that a single supportive study proves little when a study exists for
almost any belief. That is why he leans on systematic reviews, and he says so
plainly, so the reader can watch the appraisal rather than only receive its
result. Showing the method is part of why the judgment is trustworthy.
