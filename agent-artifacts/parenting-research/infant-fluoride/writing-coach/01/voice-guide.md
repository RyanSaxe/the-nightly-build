# Voice guide: parenting-research/infant-fluoride (01)

## How this piece should sound

The reader is a parent who can follow a study's design and wants to know exactly
what it did and didn't test — not a reader who needs the finding softened or the
uncertainty smoothed over. The register that fits is the one all three exemplars
below share: plain declarative sentences, numbers left as numbers, and a verdict
withheld until the evidence in front of the reader has actually earned it.

Bound every claim by what was tested, not by what a summary of it implies.
Goldacre's first move in "Anecdotes are great" is to say what the study showed
and, in the same breath, what kind of study it was — a small unblinded trial.
This piece moves between concentration levels, age ranges a trial did or didn't
include, and formulations, and the gap between what a trial covered and what a
guideline now recommends is frequently the real content of a paragraph, not a
footnote to it.

Where the evidence rests on a proxy rather than the outcome a household actually
cares about, hold the two apart the way Goldacre holds them in the Allhat and
HbA1c passages: say plainly that the proxy moved, and separately, what is and
isn't known about whether that movement tracks the outcome that matters. A
biological or laboratory measure moving in the expected direction is not the
same claim as decay prevented, and the piece can afford to spend a sentence on
that distinction each time it matters rather than once at the top.

Resist collapsing a contested figure into a single clean number. Yong's Santa
Clara passage holds "this particular figure is genuinely disputed" and "this
broader fact is not" in two adjacent sentences without either one leaking into
the other. Given how the commission frames this piece — evidence that differs by
age tested, by concentration, by whether a household's water is fluoridated —
that same move, applied locally each time a bound needs stating, will do more
work than a single caveat paragraph up top.

Earn a flat verdict sentence the way Gardner earns his "In summary" line: only
after the reviews or guideline bodies that support it have actually been walked
through by name. Cochrane, the AAP, the ADA, and the USPSTF are exactly the kind
of concrete noun Yong and Gardner reach for — a named instrument, a named trial,
a specific sample size — in place of "experts recommend" or "the research shows."
Use their names rather than a summary of their authority.

Where the water-fluoridation policy debate presses against the piece — live in
the news, not the subject, per the commission — Gardner's paragraph on the
"natural order" argument is worth noticing for its shape: he states the opposing
position in its strongest, most reasonable form, then answers only the empirical
question actually in front of him, leaving the political one alone. The piece
has the same move available wherever the policy noise threatens to answer a
question the trial evidence was never positioned to answer.

At 1200 to 3000 words carrying two separate household decisions, there is room
for one passage built the way Yong builds his false-positive paragraph — walking
a set of numbers through to a concrete consequence, worked out rather than
asserted — at whichever of the two decisions turns out to hinge hardest on a
number the reader can't otherwise picture. It does not have room for that move
twice.

## Ed Yong, "Why the Coronavirus Is So Confusing"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/health/archive/2020/04/pandemic-confusing-uncertainty/610819/

> "For now, doctors are routinely prescribing it without knowing if it works or, crucially, if it does more harm than good. Meanwhile, people with lupus and rheumatoid arthritis, who actually need hydroxychloroquine, can't get it. It is not the case that every new study contributes to our understanding of COVID-19. Sloppy ones are a net negative, adding to the already considerable uncertainty by offering the illusion of confidence where none exists."

Yong names the specific harm of bad evidence — a shortage for the patients who actually need the drug — before he states the general rule about sloppy studies. The general sentence lands because a concrete cost was paid first; nothing here is hedged, and nothing is inflated past what the paragraph has shown.

> "One could write a long article assessing the Santa Clara study alone, but that would defeat the point: that individual pieces of research are extremely unlikely to single-handedly upend what we know about COVID-19. […] The precise magnitude of the virus's fatality rate is a matter of academic debate. The reality of what it can do to hospitals is not."

The last two sentences divide the same subject into what remains genuinely contested and what does not, without either sentence borrowing certainty from the other. The division is made explicit rather than left for the reader to infer from tone.

> "One such test claims to correctly identify people with those antibodies 93.8 percent of the time. By contrast, it identifies phantom antibodies in 4.4 percent of people who don't have them. That false-positive rate sounds acceptably low. It's not. Let's assume 5 percent of the U.S. has been infected so far. Among 1,000 people, the test would correctly identify antibodies in 47 of the 50 people who had them. But it would also wrongly spot antibodies in 42 of the 950 people without them. […] your odds of actually having them would be little better than a coin toss."

Yong runs the arithmetic in the open, with a round population and named counts, rather than asserting the conclusion about the test's usefulness. The reader can check every step, which is what makes the final claim — "little better than a coin toss" — land as earned rather than asserted.

## Ben Goldacre, "Anecdotes are great – if they convey data accurately"

Source: https://www.theguardian.com/science/2011/jul/29/duchennes-muscular-dystrophy-surrogate-outcomes

> "Unfortunately, the study shows no such thing. The gene for making a muscle protein called dystrophin is damaged in patients with DMD. The Lancet paper shows a new treatment led to some restoration of dystrophin production in some children in a small unblinded study."

Goldacre corrects the media claim and states the study's actual limits — small, unblinded, in some children — in the same three sentences. The correction and the caveat about study design arrive together instead of the caveat trailing in afterward as a hedge.

> "But this story is also a reminder that we should always be cautious with 'surrogate' outcomes. The biological change measured was important, and good grounds for optimism, because it shows the treatment is doing what it should in the body. But things that work in theory do not always work in practice, and while a measurable biological indicator is a hint something is working, such outcomes can often be misleading."

He holds two claims at once without collapsing them: the finding is real and worth some optimism, and it is also a different kind of finding than the one that would settle the question. Neither claim is allowed to cancel the other out.

> "But patients on doxazosin turned out to have a higher risk of stroke, and cardiovascular problems, than patients on chlorthalidone – even though both lowered blood pressure – to such an extent that the trial was stopped early. Blood pressure, in this case, was not a reliable surrogate outcome for assessing the drug's benefits on real-world outcomes."

The example shows a proxy measure moving the expected way while the real-world outcome moved in the opposite direction, in a trial large enough to be stopped early on the strength of that reversal. The specificity of the drug names and the trial's own response (stopping early) is what makes the general point about surrogate outcomes credible rather than asserted.

## Bill Gardner, "What's wrong with the children of lesbian moms? Ummm, nothing?"

Source: https://theincidentaleconomist.com/wordpress/whats-wrong-with-the-children-of-lesbian-moms-ummm-nothing/

> "The adult children of the NLLFS families were assessed using the Achenbach, a gold-standard instrument for mental health that has been normed on large samples of Americans. They matched the adult children in the NLLFS sample with randomly sampled adults in the Achenbach normative sample who had the same age, sex, race or ethnic background, and educational level."

Gardner names the instrument and describes the matching procedure in plain terms instead of calling the study "rigorous" or "well-designed." A reader who has never heard of the Achenbach still learns exactly what was measured and how the comparison group was built.

> "In summary, 40 years of research indicates that being raised by lesbians is no different than being raised by a man and a woman."

The sentence is flat and unhedged, and it is placed after four independent reviews spanning sixteen years have each been quoted in turn. The confidence of the sentence is proportional to the evidence stacked in front of it, not to how strongly Gardner wants the reader to agree.

> "The core argument against lesbian parenting has been that parenting by a father and mother is part of the natural order, a view supported by thousands of years of tradition in many cultures. […] In my view, a preference for tradition is a reasonable heuristic, but that's all it is. You should also look for evidence of harm. […] So far as we can tell, there are none."

Gardner states the opposing position in its strongest and most reasonable form before he answers it, and the answer he gives — "so far as we can tell" — is bounded to what the evidence he just walked through can support, not stated as a closed question.
