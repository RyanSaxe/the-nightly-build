# Evidence record: parenting-research/infant-fever (01)

The evidence strongly supports the commission's spine. Authoritative pediatric
guidance (AAP 2011, reaffirmed 2022; NICE NG143) states plainly that fever is a
regulated physiologic defense, that its height does not reliably track illness
severity, and that the goal of an antipyretic is comfort, not a normal
thermometer reading. Three parental expectations fail against primary evidence:
that antipyretics meaningfully shorten the illness (a meta-analysis finds no
prolongation and if anything slightly faster fever clearance, so the belief that
they either shorten *or* lengthen illness is unsupported), that they prevent
febrile-seizure recurrence (a randomized trial finds identical recurrence with
and without antipyretic: 23.4% vs 23.5%), and that combining acetaminophen and
ibuprofen is a routine upgrade (it lowers temperature more, but with no shown
comfort benefit and a real dosing-error hazard). The young-infant emergency
threshold is firm and identical across guidelines: 38.0°C (100.4°F), measured
rectally. Where the evidence is genuinely thin: the two arms of the
febrile-seizure trial are badly unbalanced (197 antipyretic vs 34 placebo
children); the "faster fever clearance" duration finding leans on malaria trials
and is a few hours of thermometer time, not a shortened illness; and no guideline
draws the 3-to-6-month line with a single number, so the "softening" of the rule
across that band is a change in guideline category, not a bright line. The
ibuprofen six-month limit rests on labeling and renal-physiology caution, not a
trial of harm. One common belief turned out unsupported in the other direction:
antipyretics do **not** appear to prolong febrile illness, contrary to the
"let the fever burn" folk claim.

## Contradictions

- **Temperature evidence vs guideline advice on combining drugs.** The trials and
  the Cochrane review (Hay 2008; Wong 2013) show combined acetaminophen +
  ibuprofen lowers temperature more and for longer than one drug. The guidelines
  decline to endorse it: AAP 2011 finds "insufficient evidence to support or
  refute" routine combination use and stresses the dosing-error risk; NICE NG143
  instructs "do not give both agents simultaneously" and only to consider
  alternating "if the distress persists." The disagreement is not about the
  thermometer — it is that guidelines treat comfort, not temperature, as the end
  point, and neither trial showed a comfort benefit from combining. Record both
  halves: the temperature effect is real; the reason to withhold it is that the
  outcome that matters did not move and the error risk did.

- **Duration: guidance hedge vs meta-analytic result.** AAP 2011 says "limited
  data have revealed that fever actually helps the body recover more quickly from
  viral infections," which a reader could take to mean suppressing fever prolongs
  illness. Purssell & While (2013) found the opposite direction at the pooled
  level: fever cleared ~4 hours *faster* with antipyretics. Neither establishes a
  clinically meaningful change in illness length. The honest synthesis is that
  antipyretics neither meaningfully shorten nor lengthen the illness.

- **Age-band framing differs between guidelines (not a true conflict).** NICE
  bands by months (<3 months, 3–6 months) with temperature cutoffs 38°C then
  39°C; AAP 2021 bands the acute-evaluation window by days (8–21, 22–28, 29–60)
  with a single ≥38.0°C entry and stops at 60 days. For an infant aged 61–90 days
  NICE still flags 38°C as high-risk while the AAP acute algorithm has ended.
  They agree on the number (38.0°C) and on the direction (younger = harder rule);
  they draw the upper edge of "young infant" at different ages.

- **Recurrence rate: trial cohort vs guideline population figure.** Strengell's
  overall recurrence (~23%) is lower than AAP 2008's general-population figures
  (~50% if the first seizure was before 12 months, ~30% after). This is a cohort/
  case-mix difference (mean age 1.7 years in Strengell), not a contradiction of
  the finding that antipyretics do not change recurrence.
