# Commission: parenting-research/infant-fever

## Why now
The desk's child was born in February 2026 and is about six months old. Two
things converge at this age. The mother's transferred antibodies are fading, so
first real fevers arrive, and the baby crosses the age line at which ibuprofen
becomes an option alongside acetaminophen. Fever is the symptom that most often
sends a new household reaching for the medicine cabinet or the phone, and the
research on what to do is strong and widely misread. This is a decision the
evidence can actually change.

## The question the article answers
What does treating an infant's fever do, what does the evidence say it does not
do, and where does a fever stop being a home matter? The piece separates three
things a parent runs together:
- What antipyretics actually do: relieve discomfort. That is the supported
  benefit, and it is not nothing.
- What parents expect them to do and the evidence does not support: meaningfully
  shorten the illness, and prevent a febrile seizure or its recurrence. The
  febrile-seizure finding is firm and counterintuitive, and it is the piece's
  sharpest correction.
- The one rule that is not a judgment call: the young-infant temperature
  threshold that makes a fever an emergency regardless of how the baby looks,
  and how that rule softens but does not vanish across the three-to-six-month
  window the desk's child is now in.

The article's own contribution is to hold "treat the child, not the thermometer"
and the hard young-infant threshold in the same frame, and to time both to the
month a baby becomes ibuprofen-eligible, rather than restating either slogan.

## What the evidence must establish and bound
- Fever as a regulated defense, and how weakly its height predicts the severity
  of the illness in an otherwise well-appearing infant past the young-infant age.
- Antipyretics and illness duration: what the trials and reviews show, including
  the direction and the size of any effect, and how uncertain it is.
- Antipyretics and febrile seizures: the randomized evidence that they do not
  prevent recurrence, with the incidence and recurrence figures in absolute
  terms.
- Combined or alternating acetaminophen and ibuprofen: what the temperature
  evidence shows, the dosing-error risk, and what guidelines advise.
- The age thresholds: the strict rule for the youngest infants and its stated
  temperature, and how guidance treats the three-to-six-month band. Mark the
  warning signs that are a clinician or emergency matter at any age.
- Ibuprofen's lower age limit and why six months is the crossover.
Show how study design, normal variation, and feasibility bound each conclusion.

## Boundaries
- Research desk, not generic advice and not individual medical care. End on what
  the evidence might change at home and state plainly where a specific situation
  requires a pediatrician or emergency care. The young-infant threshold and the
  warning signs are safety matters, marked as such.
- Numbers in absolute terms with their denominators, not relative risk alone.

## Sources
Template floor is 8. Expect the AAP guidance on fever and antipyretic use in
children and the AAP 2021 clinical practice guideline for well-appearing febrile
infants; the NICE guideline on fever in under-5s; systematic reviews of
paracetamol and ibuprofen for childhood fever and of combined or alternating
use; the randomized febrile-seizure antipyretic evidence; the evidence on
antipyretics and illness duration; and the ibuprofen age-limit labeling. Read
the primary guidance and trials, not summaries of them.

## Habits to break (recent parenting desk)
Recent pieces open on "the guidance rests on trials that do not fit this baby /
the evidence settles only X" and close on the two-sentence "X is settled. Y is
not." form, with a holds-up block. This piece has its own spine and should not be
bent into either. The desk's absolute-versus-relative-risk move fits the
febrile-seizure numbers; use it once, plainly, without making it the article's
signature. Vary the closer from the recent settled/unsettled couplet.
