# Voice guide: parenting-research/infant-flu-vaccine

## How this piece should sound

This is an appraisal, not a brief for or against a shot, and the three writers
below all work the same way: state what a study measured before giving the
number it produced, and let the number carry the argument instead of an
adjective standing next to it. Before Branswell gives a single vaccine
efficacy percentage, she states what strain the trials behind it were run
against and what that means for every figure that follows. This article can
open each effect size the same way: the pivotal trial's efficacy figure for
six-to-35-month-olds needs its own sentence about which season, which
circulating strains, and which endpoint it measured, sitting before the
percentage rather than qualifying it afterward.

Two comparisons in the commission are exactly the kind Branswell shows how to
handle: a six-month-old's own vaccine efficacy against the protection that
reaches an unvaccinated infant through cocooning or through a mother's
vaccination in pregnancy. These are different quantities, measured in
different studies against different outcomes, the way Branswell's Pfizer,
Moderna, and J&J efficacy figures turned out to rest on differently designed
trials. Name what each figure actually measured before setting them side by
side, rather than letting three numbers imply they answer the same question.

Where the article needs the reader to hold an unfamiliar mechanism, take
Oster's move of building the thing before naming it: she walks through why a
bigger expected effect needs a smaller sample to detect, sentence by sentence,
and only calls it "statistical power" once the mechanism is already on the
page. The two-dose priming schedule can be built the same way. An infant
getting its first flu vaccine has no prior exposure to draw on, so what the
first dose does and what the second dose four weeks later adds are two
different things; work out that mechanism before the word "priming" carries
the weight of it.

Spiegelhalter and Masters track a single effect size across the evidence base
that supports it, watching a headline number change as weaker studies are
set aside, and they close on a plain sentence that states what can and cannot
be concluded rather than a softened one. The commission's own instruction,
that pediatric efficacy is real but "more modest and less precisely estimated"
than in older children, is a claim this piece can show the same way: what the
Cochrane synthesis finds next to what the individual pivotal trials found,
and why the range narrows or widens between them, rather than asserting
"modest and uncertain" as a pair of adjectives.

The commission also sets where the piece has to land: the pediatrician-facing
judgment calls on egg allergy, prematurity, chronic conditions, and the fact
that an infant under six months old has no direct protection available to it
at all. Branswell draws a flat line, in her side-effects section, between what
a finding shows and what a reader might assume it shows: common side effects
mean the immune system is responding; the CDC's safety monitoring is what
establishes whether the vaccine itself carries harm, and those are separate
questions with separate evidence. Close this piece on that same discipline,
stating plainly what the evidence settles and what it hands to a clinician,
rather than settling into reassurance or a pointer to go read further.

## Emily Oster, "Statistical Significance—and Why It Matters for Parenting"

Source: https://parentdata.org/statistical-significance-for-parenting/

> "Imagine the following (fake) study. Participants are randomly assigned to
> eat a package of either blue or green M&Ms, and then they flip a (fair) coin
> and you see if it is heads. Your analysis will compare the number of heads
> that people flip after eating blue versus green M&Ms and report whether this
> is 'statistically significant at the 5% level.'"

Oster doesn't define statistical significance and move on to the next idea.
She runs a study whose true answer she already knows, M&M color cannot affect
a coin flip, and lets the reader watch what "significant" means when the
underlying effect is genuinely zero. Nothing in the paragraph could be lifted
into a different topic: the color, the coin, and the package are what make the
logic visible.

> "The larger the effect you expect, the smaller the sample of people you need
> to detect it statistically. This is called 'statistical power,' and in
> well-designed experiments, researchers calculate this before they even
> start their study. They make an educated guess at the size of the impact,
> and they figure out how many people they need to detect that effect."

The claim arrives with its own mechanism attached, not asserted and left for
the reader to trust. She names the term of art, "statistical power," only
after showing what it does, so the label lands on something already built
rather than substituting for the explanation.

> "Specifically, they studied a dead salmon. They put this dead salmon into an
> fMRI machine, and they gave it a task. As they write: 'The salmon was shown
> a series of photographs depicting human individuals in social situations
> with a specified emotional valence, either socially inclusive or socially
> exclusive.' The study is careful. They provide a mirror to ensure the salmon
> can see the faces. ... I want to mention again that, at the time, the salmon
> was dead. (Brains can have electrical activity postmortem.) In addition,
> salmon are not well known for being responsive to the facial emotions of
> people, even while alive."

The humor here is entirely in the facts: the fMRI machine, the mirror, the
parenthetical about postmortem brain activity. Take out "dead salmon" and
nothing generic is left standing in its place; the specificity is doing the
work a punchline would do in a looser piece.

## Helen Branswell, "Comparing the Covid-19 vaccines developed by Pfizer, Moderna, and Johnson & Johnson"

Source: https://www.statnews.com/2021/02/02/comparing-the-covid-19-vaccines-developed-by-pfizer-moderna-and-johnson-johnson/

> "We should note off the top that the randomized controlled trials conducted
> to determine if and how well the mRNA vaccines worked were done before the
> emergence of what now seems like an endless stream of variants, the latest
> of which is the highly mutated Omicron. So the figures listed below are
> indications of how well these vaccines protected people from the original
> SARS-2 virus, called the Wuhan strain. It has since been shown that some of
> the mutations acquired as the virus evolves have eroded the vaccines'
> protective powers to a greater or lesser degree, depending on the variant."

Before giving a single efficacy number, Branswell states what the trials could
and could not measure: they ran against one strain, so every figure that
follows describes protection against that strain and nothing after it. The
caveat sits ahead of the numbers instead of trailing behind them as a hedge
once the numbers have already done their work on the reader.

> "But comparing the efficacy of those vaccines to the efficacy of Johnson &
> Johnson's is challenging because of differences in the designs of the Phase
> 3 clinical tests — essentially the trials were testing for different
> outcomes. Pfizer's and Moderna's trials both tested for any symptomatic
> Covid infection. Pfizer started counting cases from seven days after receipt
> of the second dose of vaccine, while Moderna waited until day 14 to start
> counting cases. ... Because of the difference in the trials, making direct
> comparisons is a bit like comparing apples and oranges."

Two efficacy percentages look comparable until she names the exact procedural
difference, when case-counting began, that makes them not comparable. The
idiom at the close earns its place because the sentences before it already
showed the reader why; it names a conclusion the passage built instead of
standing in for the argument.

> "To be clear: These side effects are a sign of an immune system kicking into
> gear. They do not signal that the vaccine is unsafe. To date there are no
> serious, long-term side effects associated with receipt of these vaccines,
> which will be closely monitored as their use expands. There is zero evidence
> to support claims that getting vaccinated against Covid will affect one's
> future fertility."

Four short declarative sentences separate one finding, side effects occur,
from a different claim entirely, that the vaccine is unsafe, and she draws
that line explicitly rather than trusting the reader to infer it. "Zero
evidence" is stated as a fact, not softened into "no evidence has yet been
found."

## David Spiegelhalter and Anthony Masters, "With Covid studies, the quality of the evidence matters"

Source: https://www.theguardian.com/theobserver/2021/nov/28/ivermectin-mask-wearing-quality-of-evidence-matters

> "In The Adventure of the Copper Beeches, Sherlock Holmes says: 'Data! Data!
> Data! I can't make bricks without clay.' Recent claims of massive benefits
> from wearing masks and using ivermectin against Covid-19 depended on mainly
> low-quality clay."

The Conan Doyle quotation sets up one idiom, data as clay, and the very next
sentence spends it: "low-quality clay" turns a critique of evidence quality
into a single concrete image instead of an abstract complaint. The image is
used once and dropped, which is why it reads as argument rather than
decoration.

> "Meta-analysis is a technique for pooling the results from many studies, but
> it cannot make silk purses out of sows' ears. A recent British Medical
> Journal review looked at six, fairly porcine, studies concerning
> mask-wearing and estimated an impressive 53% reduction in risk. But the
> single randomised controlled trial estimated the smallest effect: a
> reduction of about 18% (-23% to 46%) in Sars-CoV-2 infections."

Every effect size here carries its comparison and its number in the same
breath, 53% from the pooled studies against 18% from the one randomized
trial, with the trial's own interval given alongside it. The writers don't
assert that the pooled estimate is unreliable; they put two numbers that
disagree next to each other and let the disagreement make the case.

> "One meta-analysis originally found ivermectin reduced Covid-19 mortality by
> about 56%, a huge effect. But then a preprint server withdrew the largest
> study and there have been further concerns over major errors and fraud in
> some ivermectin analyses. After removing studies with a high risk of bias,
> the estimated effect dissipated: the estimated decreased mortality was about
> 10% but with a wide uncertainty interval (- 42% to 43%), so no firm
> conclusions can be drawn."

The passage tracks one number through two states, 56% and then 10%, and names
exactly what changed between them: which studies were removed, and why. It
ends on a plain verdict, stated once and not hedged further by a second
qualifying clause.
