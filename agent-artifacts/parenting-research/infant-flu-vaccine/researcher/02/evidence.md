# Evidence: parenting-research/infant-flu-vaccine (02)

This record carries forward the verified evidence of researcher/01 and makes one
targeted correction ordered by editor/01. The prior record and draft framed the
66%-to-(-7%) two-season efficacy swing in Hoberman 2003 as a change in antigenic
match between the circulating strain and the vaccine. Re-reading the owning
primary (Hoberman et al. 2003, JAMA) shows that framing is false: the paper
states the circulating strains were **well matched with the vaccine strains in
both seasons**, and it attributes the near-null second-season estimate to **low
influenza incidence** — the placebo attack rate fell from 15.9% (1999-2000) to
3.3% (2000-01), so the second-season estimate rests on only 4 placebo cases and
its wide, zero-crossing CI (-247% to 67%) is imprecision, not mismatch. The swing
therefore illustrates how uncertainly efficacy can be pinned down in the youngest
age band, not a demonstrated season-match difference. A general claim that flu
vaccine effectiveness "depends on antigenic match year to year" is true and
sourceable, but Hoberman does not own it; the proper owner is CDC's flu-vaccine
effectiveness page, added below. Everything else in researcher/01 stands: the LAIV
age floor, the applicable inactivated figure, the maternal-vaccination waning
curve, the dosing rule, and the safety signal all check out against their owning
primaries and are preserved verbatim.

## Contradictions

- **CORRECTION — the two-season swing is imprecision from low incidence, not
  season-match.** editor/01 found, and re-reading the owning primary confirms,
  that the prior record and draft wrongly attributed the 66%-to-(-7%) efficacy
  swing in Hoberman 2003 to a change in antigenic match. Hoberman states the
  opposite: "Circulating influenza strains were well matched with vaccine strains
  in the 2 respiratory seasons during which the study was conducted" — i.e. both
  seasons, including 2000-01, were well matched. The second-season near-null
  result is driven by low influenza incidence: the placebo attack rate fell from
  22/138 (15.9%) to 4/123 (3.3%), so the -7% estimate rests on only 4 placebo
  cases and its CI (-247% to 67%) is wide and crosses zero because of that small
  count. The authors write that "During the second year, influenza occurred
  infrequently and there was no clustering of cases." The swing therefore
  demonstrates how imprecisely efficacy can be estimated in the youngest age band
  in a low-incidence season, NOT a demonstrated antigenic-match difference. This
  supersedes the researcher/01 Hoberman note and the researcher/01 contradiction
  bullet "Direct efficacy in the youngest is unproven for a bad-match season,"
  whose "bad-match" framing is retracted. The valid residual point still stands:
  a six-month-old's own vaccination is estimated imprecisely in this age band and
  cannot be relied on for a given season in advance — because the season's
  incidence and the resulting estimate are not knowable ahead of time, not
  because of match.

- **The general "match matters" claim is sourceable, but only to CDC, and it
  cannot be pinned to Hoberman.** A statement that influenza vaccine
  effectiveness varies year to year with how well the vaccine matches circulating
  strains is genuinely true and owned by CDC's flu-vaccine-effectiveness page
  ("Flu vaccine effectiveness may vary based on how similar or different the
  vaccine virus is to circulating influenza viruses"). If the closer keeps a
  match-dependence line, it must cite that CDC page and stand on its own, kept
  clearly separate from the Hoberman swing (which was well matched both seasons
  and so is NOT an example of mismatch). If the writer does not want that
  separate general point, the cleaner course is to drop match from the piece
  entirely and rest the swing solely on the low-incidence/imprecision reading.
  Hoberman may not be used to support any match-dependence claim.

- **The strongest Cochrane number is out of scope for a six-month-old.** LAIV's RR
  0.22 is the headline efficacy figure, but LAIV is licensed only from age 2. The
  applicable product for this child is inactivated vaccine (RR 0.36 against
  laboratory-confirmed influenza), and Cochrane found very few randomized trials
  under age two, so the pooled inactivated figure is carried largely by older
  children. Using the LAIV number, or the pooled Cochrane efficacy, to describe a
  six-month-old would overstate the evidence.

- **Maternal protection fades before the child ages into direct eligibility.** The
  pooled maternal efficacy is 35% over six months but only 19% (95% CI -9 to 40) in
  the 4-to-6-month window, and the Mali trial found protection "no longer evident"
  by month six. So the two mechanisms the article leans on do not overlap cleanly:
  borrowed maternal protection is weakest just as the child becomes eligible for its
  own (imprecisely estimated) vaccine.

- **Site-to-site heterogeneity in the maternal trials.** Individual infant estimates
  range from 33% (Mali) to 48.8% (South Africa) to 63% (Bangladesh), all with wide,
  overlapping CIs; the Bangladesh point estimate rests on 22 total infant cases. The
  spread reflects differing seasons, circulating strains, and trial size, not a
  single stable effect. The pooled 35% is the most defensible figure. Note the pool
  itself is Nepal/Mali/South Africa; Bangladesh (Zaman) is a separate trial, not a
  constituent (editor/01 correction).

- **ILI versus laboratory-confirmed influenza.** Effects against influenza-like
  illness (a non-specific outcome that includes non-influenza respiratory disease)
  are markedly weaker than against laboratory-confirmed influenza (inactivated RR
  0.72 vs 0.36). Reporting the laboratory-confirmed figure as the vaccine's effect on
  "getting sick" would overstate what a family experiences.

- **A real, small safety signal exists but only in combination.** The commission's
  angle is broadly reassuring on safety; the honest bound is that co-administration
  of inactivated influenza vaccine with PCV13 or DTaP roughly triples the short-term
  febrile-seizure rate (IRR 3.50), even though inactivated vaccine alone shows no
  signal and the absolute excess is about 30 per 100,000.

No contradiction was found to the two-dose rule, the six-month eligibility, or the
LAIV age floor; these are consistent across every ACIP and AAP source read.
