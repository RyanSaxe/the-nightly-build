# evidence: parenting-research/teething (01)

The evidence strongly supports the commissioned framing on both questions, with
one honest boundary. On symptoms: multiple prospective cohorts and two
meta-analyses agree that primary tooth eruption is associated only with mild,
mostly local signs (drooling, gum irritation, biting, irritability, and at most
a small rise in body temperature that is not a true fever), and they specifically
do not support blaming high fever (>38.9 C / 102 F), serious illness, or (in the
strongest study) diarrhea on teething. The effect sizes are small: in the largest
cohort no teething symptom appeared in more than 35% of teething infants, and none
appeared more than 20% more often than in non-teething infants. On product safety:
the FDA's own primary letters establish the benzocaine methemoglobinemia record
(119 cases Feb 2009-Oct 2017, four deaths including a teething infant) and the
belladonna homeopathic-teething action (inconsistent, sometimes label-exceeding
belladonna alkaloids; a Class I requested recall of Hyland's products). Where the
record is thin: (1) the strongest contrary signal is a small fever meta-analysis
that found an association when fever was measured rectally (OR 2.82), so "teething
never nudges temperature" overstates the evidence — the defensible claim is "no
high fever, and no more than a small elevation"; (2) the widely cited belladonna
figures ("~99 FAERS cases," "10 infant deaths") come from FDA safety
communications that are now removed from live fda.gov, so I could not verify those
specific counts against a primary I opened. The benzocaine counts are fully
primary-verified.

```text
Establishes: Which infant symptoms are and are not statistically associated with

Paraphrase:  Prospective cohort of 125 consecutive well infants of Cleveland Clinic
             employees, followed from the 4-month visit to age 1. Parents recorded
             two tympanic temperatures and presence/absence of 18 symptoms daily
             plus every tooth eruption: 19,422 child-days, 475 eruptions. Symptoms
             clustered in an 8-day window (4 days before to 3 days after emergence).
             Associated with teething: increased biting, drooling, gum-rubbing,
             sucking, irritability, wakefulness, ear-rubbing, facial rash, decreased
             appetite for solids, and mild temperature elevation. NOT associated:

             decreased appetite for liquids, cough, non-facial rashes, fever over
             102 F, and vomiting. No symptom occurred in more than 35% of teething
             infants, and none occurred more than 20% more often in teething than
             non-teething infants. No teething child had a fever of 104 F and none
             had a life-threatening illness.
Locators:    Pediatrics 2000;105(4 Pt 1):747-752; structured abstract (Design,
             Outcome measures, Results, Conclusions).
Quote:       "Before caregivers attribute any infants' signs or symptoms of a
             potentially serious illness to teething, other possible causes must be
             ruled out."
```

```text
URL:         https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21824888/
Kind:        primary — Ramos-Jorge et al. own this prospective study. (Read
             complete structured abstract via PubMed eutils, PMID 21824888.)
Establishes: Independent prospective confirmation that eruption raises temperature
             only slightly (within normal range) and cannot explain fever; but it
             DID associate some systemic signs (diarrhea, rash) with teething,
             unlike Macknin — the key internal contradiction.
Paraphrase:  8-month longitudinal study, 47 non-institutionalized infants aged
             5-15 months, Diamantina, Brazil; daily tympanic and axillary
             temperatures, oral exams, and maternal interview on 13 symptoms.
             Teething associated with a rise in tympanic temperature on the day of
             eruption (P=.004), but the maximal readings were 36.8 C tympanic and
             36.6 C axillary — inside the normal range, not fever. Signs associated
             with teething: irritability, increased salivation, runny nose, loss of
             appetite, diarrhea, rash, sleep disturbance.
Locators:    Pediatrics 2011;128(3):471-476; abstract (Results, Conclusions).
Quote:       "Results of this study support the concept that the occurrence of
             severe signs and symptoms, such as fever, could not be attributed to
             teething."
```

```text
URL:         https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29104392/  (open full text: PMC5661046)
Kind:        primary — Nemezio et al. own this fever-specific meta-analysis.
             (Read complete structured abstract via PubMed eutils, PMID 29104392.)
Establishes: The strongest evidence that eruption CAN associate with fever, and
             the measurement-method caveat that qualifies it. This is the
             framing-breaking source the brief asked for.
Paraphrase:  Systematic review/meta-analysis on fever and primary tooth eruption;
             83 studies screened, 6 included, dichotomous data pooled
             (Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel, fixed effects). Overall: no association,
             OR 1.32 (95% CI 0.88-1.96). Subgroup where fever was measured
             rectally: association present, OR 2.82 (95% CI 1.55-5.14). Authors
             stress few suitable studies exist.
Locators:    Int J Clin Pediatr Dent 2017;10(3):293-298; abstract (Results,
             Conclusion).
Quote:       "In the overall meta-analysis, no association was found [OR = 1.32
             (0.88-1.96)] between fever and primary tooth eruption. However, in the
             subgroup analysis, when the method used to measure fever was the rectal
             temperature there was an association [OR = 2.82 (1.55-5.14)]."
```

## Contradictions

- **Does eruption raise temperature at all, and does that ever reach "fever"?**
  The sources do not fully agree. Macknin (2000) found "mild temperature
  elevation" associated with teething but fever over 102 F (38.9 C) not associated.
  Massignan (2016) pooled data as "a rise in temperature, but not fever."
  Ramos-Jorge (2011) found a statistically significant day-of-eruption tympanic
  rise, but the maximum readings (36.8 C) were within the normal range, not fever.
  Nemezio (2017) found no overall fever association (OR 1.32, CI crosses 1) but a
  significant association in the rectal-temperature subgroup (OR 2.82). Net: the
  claim "teething causes a small temperature bump but not a true/high fever" is
  well supported; the flat claim "teething never affects temperature" is not, and
  the strongest pro-fever signal hinges on measurement method and few studies.

- **Diarrhea and rash.** Commission and Macknin treat diarrhea/loose stool as NOT
  attributable to teething (Macknin: loose stool, increased stool number, and
  non-facial rash all non-significant). Ramos-Jorge (2011), by contrast, lists
  diarrhea and rash among signs associated with teething. AAP HealthyChildren.org
  sides with Macknin, calling diarrhea a false sign and warning that persistent
  diarrhea signals possible infection. The writer should follow the larger,
  better-powered cohort and the AAP framing but should not claim unanimity.

- **"Low-grade fever" wording inside the FDA itself.** The FDA consumer update
  relays AAP as listing "a low-grade fever" among occasional teething symptoms,
  while the current AAP HealthyChildren.org page calls fever a "false symptom."
  Both are AAP-attributed. The reconcilable reading: a slight rise within/near
  normal is expected; a measured fever (commonly >38 C) should not be blamed on
  teething. Do not present "low-grade fever is a teething symptom" as settled.

- **No contradiction on drug safety.** Every source (FDA letters, FDA consumer
  update, AAP) agrees benzocaine and belladonna homeopathic teething products are
  unsafe for infants and that firm chilled (not frozen) teethers and gum massage
  are the safe measures.
