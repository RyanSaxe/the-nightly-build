# Voice guide: evidence-based health writing for a rigorous lay reader

## Directive

Write to a reader who reasons about data for a living and is also holding the
baby. Keep the house register (calm, precise, argued from first principles) and
add one thing it does not supply: a firmness that visibly tracks the evidence
and the size of the harm. The default gives a single even tone. This subject
needs a range. A near-zero association, a low-stakes comfort measure, and a
documented poisoning risk must not land at the same temperature. The reader
should be able to feel which is which from the force of the sentence before they
reach the citation.

The reader holds the vocabulary. Use effect size, confounding, prospective
cohort, base rate, and absolute versus relative risk as working terms, not
lessons. Do not stop to define them and do not perform the reasoning at
textbook length. Spend the saved space on the specific numbers and the specific
decision. Every abstraction cashes out in what a household does at the crib
tonight.

Two moves will change the most sentences:

- Match force to warrant. Deflate a misattribution flatly. Report a low-risk
  measure plainly, with no manufactured urgency. Reserve the hard imperative for
  a harm that is documented, serious, and avoidable, and let its firmness be
  conspicuous against the calm around it.
- Name the confounder that makes this subject a magnet for false blame: a
  developmental window that overlaps a period of rising infection exposure, so a
  fever and a first tooth arrive in the same week for reasons that have nothing
  to do with each other. Name that mechanism instead of reaching for a slogan
  about correlation.

Give risk in natural frequencies with a denominator the reader already carries.
A relative risk never stands alone.

## Licenses

form: calibrated safety imperative (a flat prohibition or direct command)
move: Oster drops out of analytic calm into a plain prohibition at exactly the
  point where a harm is documented, serious, and avoidable, then returns to the
  measured register. Carroll reserves firm language for a danger that is "clear,
  large, and avoidable" and argues that spending that firmness on trivial risks
  spends it everywhere.
bar: the sentence transmits a specific documented action or finding by a named
  authority, with the harm mechanism named and the source cited. Strip the
  citation and it still reads as a report of what a regulator or a case series
  established, not the writer's counsel. Force scales to documented magnitude: a
  low or uncertain harm gets a flat report, never an imperative. One or two hard
  imperatives is the piece's whole budget; a third has miscalibrated the rest.

form: the null result stated as a finding, not a gap
move: Spiegelhalter turns extensive negative searching into a positive claim,
  arguing that a large effort finding nothing "can be considered evidence of an
  absence of an important effect," rather than retreating to a call for more
  study.
bar: the sentence names the design and scale that earn the null (prospective,
  the number of children, what was actually measured) and keeps evidence of
  absence distinct from absence of evidence. It never upgrades a null to proof,
  and never downgrades a well-powered null to a shrug or a "more research is
  needed."

form: the effect-size deflation (significant, and still not important)
move: Oster grants an association its statistical significance and then withholds
  practical weight in the same breath, pairing it with the base rate that makes
  it useless for a decision: most days with heavy drool have no tooth behind them.
bar: the sentence carries the number that shows the smallness, an effect size or
  a predictive value or a base rate, so the reader sees why significance and
  importance part ways here. Not a bare "but it isn't clinically meaningful."
  Reserve the form for a named belief that treats a real association as
  decision-relevant; elsewhere state the finding flat rather than staging a
  contrast.

## David Spiegelhalter, "How dangerous is burnt toast?"
Source: https://medium.com/wintoncentre/how-dangerous-is-burnt-toast-c5e237873097
Craft:
- cadence: builds in order (what the compound is, what toxicology shows, what
  epidemiology has failed to find, whether the warning is warranted), each step
  spending the last. Short declaratives carry the turns.
- argument: separates hazard from magnitude, then asks whether the magnitude
  justifies alarming anyone. A warning must earn its warrant.
- evidence: treats a large negative literature as a result. "There is no good
  evidence of harm from humans consuming acrylamide in their diet" is stated as
  a finding, not a hedge.
- stance: skeptical of the institution issuing the warning, never of the reader;
  faults a campaign for giving no estimate of the harm it claims to prevent.
- notice: what is missing from the alarm, the absent denominator, the
  unestimated benefit, is where he points.
- diction: plain, quantitative, unfrightened. Comparisons the reader holds
  (obesity's measured cancer toll) size the risk being discussed.
- reader: addressed as capable of holding a number and drawing the conclusion.
- the important move: absence of evidence versus evidence of absence, stated
  explicitly, so a null does real work instead of excusing a shrug.

## Emily Oster, "My Baby Is Teething. Now What?"
Source: https://parentdata.org/babies/my-baby-is-teething-now-what/
Craft:
- cadence: reframe, then dismantle. Names the attribution bias (you forget the
  no-tooth days) before touching the data, so the numbers land on prepared ground.
- argument: from the data toward a decision under acknowledged uncertainty; the
  data narrows the choice without making it.
- evidence: "The associations are significant in a statistical sense, but not
  very important." Reports sample size (21 children) and the divergence between
  parent-reported and measured signs rather than smoothing it.
- stance: treats the reader as a peer solving a problem, not seeking
  reassurance. Here is what we know, here is what to do about what we don't.
- notice: predictive value, not mere association. A significant effect that
  cannot predict the next day is called out as such.
- diction: warm and direct in the source; transfer the directness, not the
  conversational persona. The house register stays calm.
- reader: a parent making a real decision this week, addressed without
  condescension.
- the important move: the drop into a flat safety prohibition ("These can, in
  rare cases, be very dangerous for your baby") exactly at the documented hazard,
  against otherwise measured prose. The temperature change is the craft.

## Aaron Carroll, "California, Coffee and Cancer: One of These Doesn't Belong"
Source: https://theincidentaleconomist.com/wordpress/california-coffee-and-cancer-one-of-these-doesnt-belong/
Craft:
- cadence: methodical escalation from an uncontested danger to the disputed one,
  so the disproportion is visible by the time the verdict arrives.
- argument: a warning is justified only when "a danger is clear, a danger is
  large and a danger is avoidable," and a warning spent on a trivial risk
  devalues every warning beside it.
- evidence: sizes each hazard directly. "Cigarettes have a clear and easily
  measured negative impact... Acrylamide, especially the acrylamide in coffee,
  isn't even close."
- stance: reasonable and evidence-respecting, firm without dismissiveness;
  credits the reader with handling nuance rather than a binary.
- notice: the cost of over-warning, that indiscriminate labels teach people to
  ignore the labels that matter.
- diction: proportional. The force of the language is set deliberately to match
  the size of the harm.
- reader: someone who can weigh magnitude and probability, not someone to be
  scared into compliance.
- the important move: firmness is a calibrated instrument. He tunes it up for a
  real hazard and down for a negligible one, and the tuning is itself the
  argument.
