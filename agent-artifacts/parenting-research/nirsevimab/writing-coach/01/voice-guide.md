# Voice guide — parenting-research/nirsevimab (invocation 01)

## Directive

Write calm and exact. The subject frightens people, so the prose must not. Keep
the sentences short and the verbs plain, and let the numbers do the alarming or
the reassuring on their own.

Moves that will change sentences in this article:

- **Pair every relative number with its absolute twin in the same sentence or
  the very next one.** A percent reduction is a ratio; a reader lives in counts.
  When you write that risk fell by three-quarters, give the two counts it is a
  ratio of before the paragraph ends (so many per hundred became so many per
  hundred). Never let a "74%" stand alone on the page.
- **Make the denominator visible.** A rate means nothing until the reader knows
  "out of how many, over how long." Fix the window (one RSV season, roughly 150
  days) and the group (healthy term infants, or preterm infants) the first time
  a figure appears, and hold those exact words when the figure returns.
- **State the direction and the size, then the uncertainty, in that order.** Say
  what the evidence shows, say how big, then say how sure — one clause each. Do
  not open with the caveat; a sentence that leads with its hedge teaches nothing.
  A confidence interval is information, not an apology: give it as a range the
  reader can read as "somewhere between this much help and that much."
- **Separate what a trial measured from what it could not.** When an endpoint was
  not significant, or was never powered, or was studied only in a different
  group, say so in the sentence that reports it, not in a distant caveat.
- **Name the choice as a choice.** Two routes, one child, pick one: write it that
  plainly. Where the answer is individual, hand it to the pediatrician in a full
  sentence rather than gesturing at "talk to your doctor."
- **Ground one abstract claim in a worked count.** Show the arithmetic once —
  how many infants you would have to protect to keep one out of the hospital —
  so "number needed to treat" is a thing the reader watched you compute, not a
  term of art dropped on them.

Keep the reader an intelligent equal. No second person addressed as a patient,
no reassuring pat, no dread. Trust the counts.

Recently used, do not reuse: the "the trial built to catch X actually measured
Y, in the infants it really tested" opener and spine (three recent
parenting-research pieces lean on it; the "who was actually studied" discipline
stays, the stock sentence shape goes). No colon-subtitle headline. No
question-mark (Betteridge) headline. No dek built as a semicolon reversal, a
suspended question, or a comma triad closed with "and."

## Exemplars

## Regina Nuzzo, "Scientific method: Statistical errors" (Nature, 2014)
Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/506150a
Craft:
- cadence: short declaratives that set up a single misconception, then dismantle
  it in the next sentence; she never runs two ideas into one breath.
- argument: builds from a concrete failure (a result that did not replicate) to
  the general statistical point, so the abstraction arrives already illustrated.
- evidence: converts p-values and false-positive rates into plain frequencies —
  "out of a hundred studies like this" — so a non-statistician can hold them.
- stance: sympathetic to the honest researcher, unsparing about the method;
  precise about what a number does and does not license.
- notice: catches the exact place a reader's intuition misreads a statistic and
  names it there, not in a summary.
- diction: everyday nouns for technical objects; the word "chance" doing the
  work of a formula.
- reader: treats the reader as smart and misinformed, never as slow.
- the move the axes missed: she makes uncertainty itself the subject and keeps
  it calm, so quantifying doubt reads as rigor rather than weakness — the exact
  register this piece needs around confidence intervals.
Calibration (texture only): reframing a lone significance threshold as a bet
whose odds are worse than they look, stated in flat, unhurried sentences.

## Atul Gawande, "The Cost Conundrum" (The New Yorker, 2009)
Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2009/06/01/the-cost-conundrum
Craft:
- cadence: patient, clause-light sentences that walk a reader through a chain of
  reasoning one link at a time.
- argument: opens in a single concrete place, then generalizes only after the
  scene has earned the claim; the data confirm a picture the reader can already
  see.
- evidence: numbers arrive attached to people and decisions, never as a table
  dropped into prose; each figure is given something familiar to be measured
  against.
- stance: a clinician's judgment, shown rather than asserted, willing to sit
  with what the evidence does not settle.
- notice: fixes on the ordinary detail that carries the whole argument.
- diction: unshowy, physician-plain; the specific procedure named, not "a
  treatment."
- reader: assumes curiosity and rewards it; explains without condescending.
- the move the axes missed: he lets a comparison do the persuading (two places,
  same medicine, different outcomes) instead of an adjective — a structure worth
  borrowing for nirsevimab versus the maternal route.
Calibration (texture only): a comparison between two otherwise-alike settings
that quietly indicts one, delivered without raising the voice.

## Ed Yong, "How the Pandemic Defeated America" (The Atlantic, 2020)
Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2020/09/coronavirus-american-failure/614191/
Craft:
- cadence: long-breathed but controlled; he varies sentence length so a hard
  fact lands after a run of setup.
- argument: multi-causal without becoming mush — he holds several contributing
  factors in view and weighs them instead of picking one villain.
- evidence: synthesizes many studies into a single legible line and flags where
  the science is still unsettled rather than papering over it.
- stance: calibrated alarm; serious about stakes, disciplined about claims.
- notice: names the systemic reason behind an individual failure.
- diction: concrete and active; jargon translated on first use.
- reader: a general reader treated as capable of holding complexity.
- the move the axes missed: he keeps uncertainty and urgency in the same
  sentence, so caution never reads as indifference — useful when a real benefit
  is large in relative terms and modest in absolute ones.
Calibration (texture only): stacking established facts into momentum, then
pausing to mark exactly what remains unknown before moving on.
