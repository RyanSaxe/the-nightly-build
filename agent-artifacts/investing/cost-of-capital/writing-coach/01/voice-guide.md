# Voice guide: investing/cost-of-capital

Register: a capable person is being walked through the writer's own reasoning
in real time, not lectured at. Write in short, single-purpose sentences and
let one worked case per idea do the defining, not the prose around it.

**Build the abstraction from the trusted number, in one direction only.**
Open the required-return idea from the Treasury yield, a number the reader
already accepts without proof, and add exactly one thing to it at a time:
first the fact that it is a floor because it is riskless, then the premium a
riskier business demands on top of it. Do not justify the Treasury yield
itself — assert it as the anchor and spend every sentence after it on what
gets added, not on defending where it came from. State each addition once,
in a clause, before using the term bare.

**Let the calculation be the definition, not an illustration of one.**
When you show the WACC blend, do it as a single small worked case (a
dollar figure in, a dollar figure out) rather than a formula with variables
named beside it. A definition earns its keep only at the moment a real
number moves through it — set the case up, run it, and let the result state
the rule, instead of stating the rule and then demonstrating it. Where you
simplify (beta, CAPM), say what you left out in one plain clause at the
place you left it out, not in a caveat paragraph afterward.

**Argue by testing the wrong answer first, when a misconception is real.**
If a plausible but wrong intuition exists (e.g., that a highly profitable
business or a high return in isolation proves anything about value
creation), state that intuition, show concretely where it breaks with a
number, and only then give the rule that survives. This works once, maybe
twice, in a piece this length — do not turn it into a rhetorical tic.

**One term per idea, used bare after its first definition.** Pick the term
this piece will live on (cost of capital, hurdle rate — pick one as the
name and use the other only to gloss it once) and never trade it for a
synonym for variety. Reach for the concrete noun over the abstract one:
name the dollar, the percent, the year, not "the return" or "the figure."

**Write the two bookends last, and make them a pair that only this piece
could produce.** After the body is done, write the opener and closer
together. The opener should set up a specific tension the body will
resolve (a company earning comfortably above its hurdle next to one
earning almost exactly at it), and the closer should cash that tension out
in the same terms, not in a general moral about investing. Read them back
to back with no body between them before you call them done.

Recently used, do not reuse: opening on a company fact by reflex;
defaulting to a Costco-and-quarterly-earnings walkthrough as the frame;
the prior lessons' heading shapes; comma-and-clause heading cadence (e.g.
"The scale, and what it is compounding against").

## Aswath Damodaran, "Putting the D in the DCF: The Cost of Capital"
Source: https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2015/01/putting-d-in-dcf-cost-of-capital.html
Craft:
- cadence: short imperative sentences followed by one clause of reasoning,
  never more
- argument: builds outward from a single anchor the reader already trusts
  (the observable risk-free rate) rather than deriving anything from
  formula first
- evidence: tests an assumption by showing where it falls in the real
  distribution of companies (a percentile), not by re-deriving the formula
- stance: direct and prescriptive; names the reader's likely shortcut and
  forecloses it in the same sentence
- notice: what's noticeable is the discipline, not the number — he spends
  his effort forbidding the reader from fussing with the one input that
  is already settled
- diction: "opportunity cost" and "hurdle rate" each used exactly once
  defined, then reused verbatim, never swapped for a synonym
- reader: treated as capable and prone to overthinking, not as a novice
  needing the basics reproven
- the move the other axes miss: he never justifies the anchor number. He
  asserts it as given and puts all his craft into what gets added to it,
  which is the opposite of a formula dump — the anchor is stated, not
  built.
Calibration: "Use the prevailing 10-year US T.Bond rate as the risk free
rate in US dollars... Fight the urge to normalize, tweak or otherwise mess
with this rate. It is what you can make today on a risk less investment,
no matter what your views on it being too low or high."

## Michael Mauboussin and Dan Callahan, "ROIC and the Investment Process"
Source: https://www.morganstanley.com/im/publication/insights/articles/article_roicandtheinvestmentprocess.pdf
Craft:
- cadence: short paragraphs, each landing exactly one idea and ending on
  the sentence that states its stakes
- argument: defines a ratio by naming what each half of it "seeks to
  reflect" before ever giving it its acronym, then proves the ratio's
  meaning with one fully worked number
- evidence: a single worked case ($100 invested at a 7% ROIC against a 7%
  WACC prices back to exactly $100) does the explaining a paragraph of
  formula would otherwise have to do
- stance: confident but self-testing — states the theory, then immediately
  asks whether the market actually bears it out
- notice: the one folk phrase used ("spend money to make money") is cashed
  out in exact terms in the very next sentence rather than left as color
- diction: definition precedes label every time; a term's meaning is given
  in plain words in the sentence before its abbreviation appears
- reader: assumed to know money but not the vocabulary; each term gets one
  plain clause on first use, then goes bare
- the move the other axes miss: the worked example is where the definition
  actually happens. The rule ("value is created only above the hurdle") is
  a restatement of what the $100 case already showed, not a claim the case
  merely illustrates.
Calibration: "In cases where a company's ROIC is equal to its WACC, an
investment of one dollar is worth one dollar. For example, if the ROIC and
WACC are 7 percent, a $100 investment that generates a perpetual return of
$7 would be valued in the market at $100 ($7/.07). No value is created or
lost."

## Howard Marks, "Risk Revisited"
Source: https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2014-09-03-risk-revisited.pdf
Craft:
- cadence: short sentences in series within a longer paragraph, each one
  testing the sentence before it
- argument: first principles by elimination — states the standard answer,
  shows concretely where it breaks, then gives what survives
- evidence: one small invented but concrete case (bought at $10, sold a
  year later at $20) stands in for statistics; an analogy already
  understood (the probability of rain tomorrow) carries the abstract idea
  across
- stance: first person throughout, arguing against named positions (the
  novice, the academic) instead of against an abstraction
- notice: he tells the reader outright which sentence is the point ("Key
  point number one in this memo is...") instead of trusting them to find
  it unmarked
- diction: no replacement term is used before the paragraph has earned it;
  the old word is retired only once its failure has been shown, not
  announced
- reader: addressed as a peer walked through the author's own reasoning in
  real time, dead ends included
- the move the other axes miss: the definition is built by running a wrong
  answer to failure in a worked case first, and naming the right answer
  only after the wrong one has visibly broken — argument as demonstration,
  not assertion followed by example.
Calibration: "If you buy something for $10 and sell it a year later for
$20, was it risky or not? The novice would say the profit proves it was
safe, while the academic would say it was clearly risky, since the only
way to make 100% in a year is by taking a lot of risk. I'd say it might
have been a brilliant, safe investment that was sure to double or a risky
dart throw that got lucky."
