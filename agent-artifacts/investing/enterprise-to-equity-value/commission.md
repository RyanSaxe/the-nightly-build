# Commission: investing/enterprise-to-equity-value

## The concept and why it is the next lesson

The published curriculum has built intrinsic valuation from first principles:
return on capital, cost of capital, present value, free cash flow, the
discounted-cash-flow model, valuation multiples, the value of growth, and the
durability of a competitive advantage. Every one of those values *the business*.
None of them has yet produced the number an investor actually acts on: what one
*share* is worth. That bridge is the missing rung, and it is the prerequisite
for any judgment about an investment and its place in a portfolio, because you
buy shares, not enterprises.

The lesson teaches the enterprise-value-to-equity bridge and the diluted share
count. Two steps, both concept-first:

1. From enterprise value to equity value: enterprise value belongs to all the
   capital providers, so the claims ahead of the common shareholder come out of
   it. Net debt is the largest and most common; the lesson also names the others
   a real bridge includes (minority interest, preferred stock, unfunded pension
   and lease obligations where material) and the non-operating assets added back
   (excess cash, marketable securities, stakes in other companies). Teach *why*
   each item sits where it does in the waterfall, grounded in what enterprise
   value measures.
2. From equity value to value per share: divide by the diluted share count, not
   the basic one. Teach the treasury-stock method for in-the-money options and
   why unexercised options, RSUs, and convertibles are claims on the same equity.

The payoff the lesson must land: leverage and dilution mean two businesses with
the same enterprise value can be worth very different amounts per share, and a
DCF is not a buy signal until it has crossed this bridge.

## Making it real without a company walkthrough

Use one real company's most recent filing to carry the arithmetic end to end, so
the reader sees enterprise value become a per-share number on real balance-sheet
lines. The concept stays the subject; the company is the worked example, chosen
so the bridge visibly moves the answer (material net debt and meaningful option
or convertible dilution both present). Do not turn the lesson into a full
company analysis, and do not default to the quarterly-earnings frame. Keep every
step transferable to the next investment.

## Boundaries and required contribution

- This is teaching, not advice. Do not recommend the example company or any
  security. Personal investment advice is out of scope for the series.
- Rely on the earlier lessons instead of re-deriving them: reference enterprise
  value, DCF, and free cash flow as established, and spend the words on the
  bridge itself.
- Word band 1200–2200; min 6 sources. The lesson template's two bookend cards
  may address the reader directly (the only `spec/slop.md` allowance here); every
  other sentence meets the standard, and an addressed sentence still has to say
  something.

## Recent investing habits not to inherit

- The two-named-companies-with-contrasting-numbers device has anchored piece
  after piece (Copart vs. Crocs, Coca-Cola vs. Verizon, Costco vs. American
  Electric Power). It is now the series' formula. This lesson uses one company
  end to end; if a contrast is genuinely needed, make it the same company under
  two capital structures, not a fresh pair of tickers.
- Recent deks are all one-line surprising quantified claims. A quantified dek is
  fine, but vary its construction from "A does B" and check it against the recent
  deks so it does not read as the next in a stamped series.
