# Voice guide: investing/enterprise-to-equity-value

## How this piece should sound

This is a two-step derivation — enterprise value to equity value, equity value to
value per share — and the reader is asked to trust every subtraction and every
share count along the way. The register that earns that trust is the one
Damodaran, Mauboussin, and Buffett all share below: state the number, show where
it came from, and only then say what it means. None of the three ever puts a
dollar figure or a percentage into a sentence without the arithmetic that
produced it sitting next to it, and this lesson's own bridge — the waterfall
from enterprise value down to a per-share number — has no other way to earn
credibility. When the piece walks the real company's filing, follow Mauboussin's
data-sharing passage: fix one number, move the others, and let the reader
re-derive the result from the parenthetical rather than take the sentence's word
for it. The treasury-stock-method dilution math wants the same treatment
Buffett gives the Goodwill amortization: each figure should visibly produce the
next one, with no step the reader has to take on faith.

Before any line of the waterfall is named, the piece owes the reader the reason
that line sits where it does — why net debt comes out, why a minority stake gets
subtracted before excess cash gets added back. Damodaran's move for this is to
deconstruct the mechanic into something the reader already understands (a return
decomposed into yield and price appreciation) before he states the conclusion
that follows from it. The bridge asks for the same patience: enterprise value
belongs to all the capital providers, so each claim ahead of the common
shareholder has to be argued from that fact, not simply listed as a line item to
subtract.

A number that cannot be scaled needs the same grounding Buffett gives See's
$13 million on $20 million of net tangible assets — a comparison pulled from the
company's own numbers, not a general magnitude. This piece has one company
carrying the arithmetic end to end, so every dollar figure in the bridge should
trace back to a line the reader has already seen on that company's own filing,
the way Buffett's $17 million becomes $425,000 a year becomes $12.5 million
becomes $7.5 million without a gap the reader has to fill in.

The piece is one company under one lens, not two tickers set against each
other, so none of these three writers' habit of contrast-by-comparison-company
is the model here. What each offers instead is a habit of sequence: define the
term the sentence needs, in the sentence where it's needed, the way Buffett
opens with "Thus our first lesson" before the definition rather than after it —
and the way Damodaran names the two misconceptions he is about to correct before
he starts correcting them. Finish one part of the bridge — the enterprise-to-equity
step, then the equity-to-per-share step — before opening the next, and let the
worked company example carry the weight the abstract statement of the rule
cannot.

## Aswath Damodaran, "Myth 5.5: The Terminal Value ate my DCF!"

Source: https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/11/myth-55-terminal-value-ate-my-dcf.html

> "First, there is a perception that if the terminal value is a high proportion of your value today, the DCF is inherently unreliable, perhaps a reflection of old value investing roots. Second, following up on the realization that a high percentage of your current value comes from your terminal value, you may start believing that the assumptions that you make about high growth therefore don't matter as much as the assumptions you make in your terminal valuation. Neither presumption is correct but they are deeply held!"

This opens by naming two specific misconceptions before knocking either down, so
the reader knows exactly what claim is being tested rather than reacting to a
vague "many people think." The phrase "deeply held" names how stubborn the
belief is without yet arguing against it — the argument is saved for what
follows, not front-loaded into the setup.

> "Note that no matter what time period you use in your assessment, the bulk of your return has taken the form of price appreciation and not dividends. Consequently, you should not be surprised to see the bulk of your value in a DCF come from your terminal value. In fact, it is when it does not account for the bulk of the value that you should be wary of a DCF!"

The finding is delivered as an instruction to look back at data just presented,
then the next sentence turns that finding into a rule, and the sentence after
that inverts the rule into a diagnostic test. Three short sentences carry an
observation, a conclusion, and the conclusion's mirror image, each doing one job.

> "Note that assuming a much higher growth rate and return on equity in the first five years has a large impact on my terminal value, even though the terminal growth rate remains unchanged. This effect will get larger for high growth firms and for longer growth periods. The conclusion that I would draw is ironic: as the terminal value accounts for a larger and larger percent of my current value, I should be paying more attention to the assumptions I make about my high growth period, not less!"

He names his own conclusion "ironic" and then states exactly what the irony is
in the clause that follows, rather than leaving the word to do the work alone.
The closing clause, "not less," directly answers the mistaken inference the
piece opened by naming, so the ending resolves the specific claim the piece set
out to correct.

## Michael J. Mauboussin (with Dan Callahan), "Measuring the Moat: Assessing the Magnitude and Sustainability of Value Creation"

Source: https://www.morganstanley.com/im/publication/insights/articles/article_measuringthemoat.pdf

> "Having a positive ROIC is not enough because capital has an opportunity cost, the value of the next best alternative for spending that capital. Imagine the opportunity to acquire two streams of identical cash flows with one priced to yield 5 percent and the other 7 percent. Allocating capital to the series that earns 5 percent fails to earn the opportunity cost of capital because there is an alternative available that earns 7 percent."

The idea — that a positive return is not automatically a good one — is stated
first as a plain claim, then immediately rebuilt with a small, made-up numeric
contrast the reader can hold in their head before the claim has to stand on its
own. The abstract phrase "opportunity cost of capital" is defined once, in
plain words, in the same sentence it first appears.

> "In 1999, Barnes & Noble held inventory for 149 days on average, collected cash in 6 days, and took 75 days to pay its suppliers (mostly the wholesalers). That means that the CCC was about 80 days (149 + 6 − 75 = 80). Another relevant point is that book retailers could return unsold books to the wholesaler, and ultimately the publisher, for a full refund."

Every figure in the passage resolves into the formula given in parentheses, so
a reader can check the 80-day answer instead of taking the author's word for
it. Naming each input by what it measures — inventory days, collection days,
payment days — before summing them is what makes the final number legible
rather than merely asserted.

> "The buyer provides high-quality data about which products are selling, regional patterns of purchase, and price elasticity. This information allows the supplier to better manage its costs, supply chain, and inventory. As a result, the supplier's invested capital turnover rises to 2.0x. The supplier can now cut its prices by 25 percent, reducing its profit margin to 7.5 percent, and still earn an ROIC of 15 percent (7.5% ✕ 2.0 = 15.0%). Supplier surplus rises even if the seller cuts its price by only 15 percent because the lower prices and higher capital turnover lead to a higher ROIC."

One number stays fixed — the 15 percent return — while two others move in
opposite directions, which is exactly the mechanism the paragraph is teaching:
a lower price is compatible with an unchanged return once turnover rises. The
parenthetical multiplication at the end lets a reader re-derive the claim
themselves in a few seconds rather than accept the sentence on faith.

## Warren Buffett, "Chairman's Letter — 1983" (Appendix: "Goodwill and its Amortization: The Rules and The Realities")

Source: https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1983.html

> "Thus our first lesson: businesses logically are worth far more than net tangible assets when they can be expected to produce earnings on such assets considerably in excess of market rates of return. The capitalized value of this excess return is economic Goodwill."

He labels the sentence a lesson the moment before stating it, so the reader
knows a definition is arriving and isn't left to infer what the previous
paragraph's dollar figures were building toward. The definition itself is one
sentence, built entirely from a comparison — earnings against tangible assets —
the reader has already seen worked out in dollars just above it.

> "Let's return to the accounting in the See's example. Blue Chip's purchase of See's at $17 million over net tangible assets required that a Goodwill account of this amount be established as an asset on Blue Chip's books and that $425,000 be charged to income annually for 40 years to amortize that asset. By 1983, after 11 years of such charges, the $17 million had been reduced to about $12.5 million. Berkshire, meanwhile, owned 60% of Blue Chip and, therefore, also 60% of See's. This ownership meant that Berkshire's balance sheet reflected 60% of See's Goodwill, or about $7.5 million."

Every dollar figure traces to the one before it: $17 million becomes $425,000 a
year, which becomes $12.5 million after 11 years, which becomes $7.5 million at
Berkshire's 60 percent stake. Plain transitional phrases — "meanwhile," "This
ownership meant" — carry the reader from one fact to the next without any of
them turning into decoration.

> "But what are the economic realities? One reality is that the amortization charges that have been deducted as costs in the earnings statement each year since acquisition of See's were not true economic costs. We know that because See's last year earned $13 million after taxes on about $20 million of net tangible assets – a performance indicating the existence of economic Goodwill far larger than the total original cost of our accounting Goodwill. In other words, while accounting Goodwill regularly decreased from the moment of purchase, economic Goodwill increased in irregular but very substantial fashion."

He poses a question and answers it with one number pulled directly from the
business — $13 million earned on $20 million of assets — rather than with a
general claim about the company's strength. The last sentence states the
paradox in the plainest possible terms, one quantity falling while the other
rises, instead of naming the situation ironic and moving past it.
