# Voice guide — investing/return-on-capital

## Directive

Register: a teacher working a problem out loud for a smart reader who has not
seen it before. Plain declarative sentences, argued from cause to conclusion.
No cheerleading adjectives on the ideas themselves ("elegant," "powerful,"
"crucial") — let what the number does carry the weight. The reader relationship
is a colleague being walked through a calculation, not a student being managed:
never announce that a point is important, never soften a definition with
"basically" or "simply put." State it once, correctly, and move.

**The worked calculation.** NOPAT ÷ invested capital is one division on two
numbers the lesson has already built. Write it as a sentence that performs the
division, not a boxed formula followed by a paragraph restating what it says.
A table earns its place only where there are multiple named line items to
compare side by side — pulling invested capital's pieces off the balance
sheet, or setting the two contrast companies' ROIC against their cost of
capital. The single ROIC computation for Costco does not need a table; it
needs one clean sentence with the two dollar figures and the resulting
percentage, in that order, so the arithmetic itself is the last thing the
reader reads before the number. Never follow a calculation with a sentence
that just says what it showed — if the number needs a caption to be
convincing, the number wasn't shown clearly enough.

**Conventions.** Invested capital has real, competing definitions (cash
in or out, leases capitalized or not, goodwill kept or stripped). Name the
choice, give the one-sentence reason a working analyst makes it, state which
one this lesson uses, and move on. That is not hedging — it is disclosure.
Hedging is "some argue X, while others believe Y, and there is no single right
answer"; disclosure is "this lesson nets out cash because [reason]; a
different convention would net it back in for [reason], and change the
number without changing the business." Commit to one, name the alternative
in a single clause, don't relitigate it.

**The bookends.** Write them only after the body exists, and make every
sentence specific to what this lesson actually taught: invested capital, the
NOPAT-over-invested-capital division, and the test against the cost of
capital. Why this matters should open on the real confusion the lesson
resolves — that two businesses can report the same profit and be entirely
different investments — using no term the body hasn't yet earned. The
takeaway should hand back the specific comparison the reader can now make
(is this business's return above or below what its capital could earn
elsewhere) without restating how the body got there. Read the two back to
back with nothing between them: the second should answer a question the
first asked, using only words the body defined.

**Sentence rhythm.** Short, single-purpose sentences as the default. Let one
long sentence run when it is tracing a single chain of cause and effect (a
number's source, through a convention, to its effect on the result) — never
to stack unrelated clauses. One earned contrast per piece at most, and only
to correct a real, named confusion (ROIC is not profit margin, because margin
ignores how much capital produced it) — never the reflex "not X, but Y."

**Tells to avoid, and why:**
- *Talking down.* Defining a term the reader has already been given, or
  flagging "in simple terms." The reader is smart and new, not slow; a
  redundant gloss spends words the lesson needs for the next idea.
- *Padding.* Transitions that only announce movement ("Now that invested
  capital is defined, let's turn to ROIC"). The headings already carry that
  structure; a sentence that repeats it teaches nothing.
- *"The takeaway is…" self-grading.* Announcing a conclusion's importance
  is not the same as earning it. Let the calculation or the contrast make
  the case; grading it after the fact reads as a writer unsure the number
  spoke for itself.
- *Hedged not-X-but-Y as a reflex.* Reserve the contrast mold for the one
  real misconception in this lesson (ROIC vs. margin, or "good business" vs.
  "profitable business"). Elsewhere it manufactures a strawman to knock down.
- *Scaffold headings.* This lesson has three concrete argument steps —
  invested capital, the ROIC calculation, the cost-of-capital test. Name
  headings for those steps in this lesson's own terms, never "Background,"
  "Overview," or "Conclusion."
- *Manufactured punchlines.* "That's the whole point" or "here's the
  catch" after a calculation is the tell that the number needed a caption
  to land. Fix the number's presentation instead of narrating its stakes.

**Recently used, do not reuse:** the prior three lessons in this course each
opened on a concrete company fact and then walked one full financial
statement start to finish, with headings that named the parts of that
statement. This lesson is not a fourth statement walkthrough and does not
default to a quarterly-earnings frame. Its headings name the steps of its own
argument — invested capital, the ROIC calculation, the value-creation test —
not a restatement of the prior lessons' statement-by-statement outline, and
its opener is not another "here is a line from Costco's filing" move already
spent three times.

## Exemplars

## Aswath Damodaran, "Return on Capital (ROC), Return on Invested Capital
(ROIC) and Return on Equity (ROE): Measurement and Implications"
Source: https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/papers/returnmeasures.pdf
Craft:
- cadence: long declarative sentences that trace cause to conclusion; he
  names all four components of a definition in one pass before unpacking any
  single one, so the reader knows the shape of the whole argument before the
  detail arrives.
- argument: builds the need for the measure before defining it — value comes
  from returns exceeding the cost of raising capital, so the definition
  arrives already motivated, not as a rule handed down.
- evidence: isolates one variable with a toy number before touching a real
  company — a single $50 million project earning $10 million a year — then
  swaps in Google's real operating income and book value once the reader can
  already predict what will happen.
- stance: commits to the correct convention (book value, not market value,
  for invested capital) and defends it by showing the alternative's specific
  failure, rather than presenting both as equally reasonable.
- notice: catches exactly where a naive calculation would mislead — dividing
  by market value for a growth company understates its return because the
  market price already bakes in future growth the number was never meant to
  capture.
- diction: a technical phrase ("after-tax operating income," "book value of
  invested capital") is defined once and then repeated verbatim for pages;
  no variation for elegance.
- reader: writes as "we," a fellow analyst building the tool alongside the
  reader, not a professor testing one.
- the move the axes miss: he never states a convention as arbitrary. Each
  survives only after he shows what breaks without it, so the reader leaves
  able to judge a convention they haven't seen yet, not just recite this one.
Calibration: "Dividing Google's operating income of $3 billion in that year
by the market value would generate a return on capital of 2%, but that would
not be a fair measure of the quality of Google's investments. Dividing
instead by Google's book value of $15 billion yields the more reasonable
estimate of return of 20% on its existing investments."

## Michael Mauboussin and Dan Callahan, "Calculating Return on Invested
Capital: How to Determine ROIC and Address Common Issues"
Source: https://www.shareholderforum.com/returns/Library/20140604_Mauboussin-Callahan.pdf
Craft:
- cadence: short paragraphs, one claim per paragraph, a plain summary
  sentence before the evidence that backs it.
- argument: opens on the consequence before the formula — Buffett's "$1
  test," worked with a toy factory ($1,000 in, $80 a year fails, $120 a year
  passes) — so the reader wants the ratio before it is named.
- evidence: works one real company, Cisco, end to end in a numbered exhibit,
  every line traceable back to a specific line of the actual 10-K.
- stance: takes an explicit side on an unsettled convention and says so
  plainly rather than surveying opinions without landing: "We are in the
  second camp."
- notice: catches the tell that a convention change, not a business change,
  moved a number — a company's ROIC "jumped overnight" when it adjusted how
  it treated cash, nothing about the business itself changed.
- diction: plain, short words; "you can think of invested capital in two
  ways that are equal" rather than a denser abstraction.
- reader: assumes intelligence, not familiarity — spells out even the
  balance-sheet identity that makes two invested-capital formulas equivalent.
- the move the axes miss: every convention comes with a working test for
  making the next judgment call alone — "ask if the company could generate
  the same level of NOPAT without the item. If not, include it." He teaches
  the test, not just the answer.
Calibration: "Say a company invests $1,000 in a new factory and estimates
that the cost of capital is 10 percent. Were the factory to generate $80 in
after-tax earnings into perpetuity, the market value of the factory would be
$800 ($80/.10) and the investment would fail the $1 test."

## Lilian Weng, "What are Diffusion Models?"
Source: https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2021-07-11-diffusion-models/
Craft:
- cadence: one orienting sentence in plain words before any notation; the
  sentence names what the coming symbol is for, never just that it exists.
- argument: places the new concept against what came before, crediting each
  prior approach a real strength and a specific, named limitation in a
  single sentence, before the new concept is allowed to enter.
- evidence: teaches the payoff before the derivation that produces it — she
  states what a property lets you do before showing the algebra that proves
  it, so the reader has a reason to follow the math.
- stance: matter-of-fact; a technique's value is demonstrated by what it
  does next, never asserted with an adjective.
- notice: introduces new machinery at the exact sentence the reader needs
  it, never a section early, never bundled in with something else.
- diction: one symbol, introduced once, kept identical for the rest of the
  piece — no swapping a variable name for a synonym.
- reader: assumes the reader can follow notation but not the field's
  history, so she situates the idea instead of re-deriving what's assumed.
- the move the axes miss: patience under length pressure — she lets a
  single idea take the paragraphs it needs rather than compressing two
  ideas into one, trusting sequence, not density, to teach.
Calibration: "GAN models are known for potentially unstable training and
less diversity in generation due to their adversarial training nature. VAE
relies on a surrogate loss. Flow models have to use specialized architectures
to construct reversible transform."
