# Voice guide: investing/margin-of-safety (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson teaches margin of safety to a reader who can handle the math but has
not yet used it to judge an investment. The register the passages share is calm
and unhurried: Damodaran, Buffett, and Mauboussin each state a valuation idea in
plain sentences and let the structure of the argument, not emphasis, do the
persuading. The reader already knows discounting and present value as arithmetic.
What they lack is the judgment that decides which cash flows to trust and how
much room to leave for being wrong. Write to that gap.

Damodaran's "never fear. You can still value companies" is written for exactly
this reader: someone who could do the algebra and needs to hear that the algebra
is most of what valuation is. When this lesson introduces intrinsic value or a
discount rate, the same move is available. Name the term, say what it does in
plain words, and leave the mechanics no harder-sounding than they are. For a
reader this quantitative, a stronger background is a reason to be plainer, not
more technical.

The lesson leans on one real company's figures to build a value range, and the
passages show how to let figures carry the point. Buffett reduces a valuation to
three answerable questions, and Mauboussin opens on two companies whose numbers
arrive at the same multiple from opposite directions. In both, a concrete case
comes before the abstraction it supports. Where this lesson reaches its own
numbers, the reasoning can stay visible one step at a time, so the reader watches
the value range get built rather than receiving the result.

Margin of safety is a response to being wrong about value, and the exemplars are
candid about that error. Damodaran says the estimate is almost guaranteed to be
wrong, and more wrong when there is more uncertainty. Buffett marks the cases
where even a wide range of estimates supports no conclusion at all. This lesson
has the same license to say plainly where a valuation is uncertain, and to let
that uncertainty set how wide the value range and the cushion have to be, without
treating the admission as a flaw in the method.

Mauboussin's contrast between assumptions that are explicit and assumptions that
are buried fits a lesson built on the distance between a quoted price and a value
the reader had to estimate. When an assumption goes into the company's numbers,
it can sit where the reader can see it and weigh it. One point of form from the
brief: the passages all address the reader as "you," but the body of this lesson
addresses no one. The plainness of that address carries over; the second person
does not, except in the two bookend cards, where the lesson may turn to the
reader directly.

## Warren Buffett, 2000 Berkshire Hathaway Chairman's Letter

Source: https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2000ar/2000letter.html

> "To flesh out this principle, you must answer only three questions. How certain are you that there are indeed birds in the bush? When will they emerge and how many will there be? What is the risk-free interest rate (which we consider to be the yield on long-term U.S. bonds)?"

Buffett turns the whole problem of valuing something into three questions a reader
can keep in mind at once, and he defines the one technical term, the risk-free
rate, in the same clause that introduces it. The questions are ordered so each is
answerable before the next depends on it. The plainness is the teaching: no
sentence assumes the reader already values assets for a living.

> "At the other extreme, there are many times when the most brilliant of investors can't muster a conviction about the birds to emerge, not even when a very broad range of estimates is employed. This kind of uncertainty frequently occurs when new businesses and rapidly changing industries are under examination. In cases of this sort, any capital commitment must be labeled speculative."

Here Buffett says plainly that some things cannot be valued, even with a wide
range of estimates, and he names the conditions under which that happens. The
passage shows a writer willing to mark the limit of his own method instead of
stretching it to cover everything. "Any capital commitment must be labeled
speculative" states the consequence flatly, with no softening word.

## Aswath Damodaran, "Discounted Cashflow Valuations (DCF): Academic Exercise, Sales Pitch or Investor Tool?"

Source: https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2015/02/discounted-cashflow-valuations-dcf.html

> "Note that there is nothing in this fundamental equation that has not been known to investors and business people through the ages, i.e., the value of a business has always been a function of its cash flows, growth potential and risk and that you certainly don't need to be mathematically inclined to be able to do valuation. So, if you don't remember how to take first differentials or solve algebraic equations, never fear. You can still value companies."

Damodaran is writing for readers who can take first differentials and telling them
they will not need to, which lowers the intimidation without lowering the content.
The voice shows in the direct reassurance, "never fear. You can still value
companies," set down right after a technical sentence. He grounds the claim in
history rather than his own authority: the equation is old, not clever.

> "If you take expected cash flows (where the expectations are across a wide spectrum of outcomes) and discount those expected cash flows at a risk-adjusted discount rate, it should go without saying (but I am going to say it anyway) that the present value that you get is an estimate of value. Thus, you are almost guaranteed to be wrong when valuing assets with any uncertainty about the future, and more wrong when there is more uncertainty. So what?"

Damodaran states that a valuation is only an estimate and that being wrong is built
into the exercise, then answers the reader's likely worry with "So what?" The
aside, "(but I am going to say it anyway)," is the writer noticing that the point
is obvious and choosing to make it plainly anyway. The passage also keeps the size
of the error and the uncertainty that produces it as two separate things.

## Michael Mauboussin, "What Does a Price-Earnings Multiple Mean?"

Source: https://strawman.com/member/uploads/objects/a9/41159492407c5bf57c2e67861056bed2decf7d.pdf

> "How can two companies so unalike have the same price-earnings multiple? Contemplating how these two stocks arrive at the same multiple from very different directions provides a mental warm-up for the process of carefully considering what comprises a price-earnings multiple. Without a proper appreciation for the factors that determine a multiple, there is no way to apply it intelligently in exercises of relative or absolute valuation."

Mauboussin opens on a concrete puzzle, two very different companies at the same
multiple, and uses it to earn the reader's attention before any formula arrives.
He then says why the puzzle matters: without understanding the factors behind a
number, you cannot use the number. The care shows in "mental warm-up," which tells
the reader this part is preparation and not yet the argument.

> "Here's the challenge. With discounted cash flow models, the value is sensitive to the inputs. But the assumptions underlying the inputs are explicit. You can compare them to base rates, discuss them, and debate them. With multiples, those assumptions are buried. The assigned multiple becomes a point of persuasion rather than a thoughtful case based on the economic drivers of value."

Mauboussin draws a clean contrast between assumptions a reader can see and debate
and assumptions hidden inside a single number. The reasoning is first-principles:
he starts from what value is and shows what each method does with the same inputs.
"Buried" and "point of persuasion" are exact word choices that carry a judgment
while the sentences stay plain.
