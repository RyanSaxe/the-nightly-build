# Evidence: investing/margin-of-safety (01)

This record supports a lesson that turns valuation uncertainty into a single
sizing rule. It carries three things the writer needs. First, Graham's own
statement of the margin of safety and, more usefully for this lesson, his line
that the margin depends on the price paid, which is the pivot from business
quality to price. Second, a primary treatment of why an intrinsic value is a
range rather than a number, from Damodaran, including his own six approaches to
valuing under uncertainty. Third, a fully worked per-share intrinsic-value range
for one anchor company, Adobe Inc. (NASDAQ: ADBE), built from its most recent
10-K with every input recorded so the writer can reproduce both ends without
reopening the filing. The anchor is strong for this lesson: a business with a
63% trailing return on invested capital whose value still spans $236 to $518 a
share once the growth input is allowed to move within a defensible range, a
2.2x spread driven mostly by one uncertain number. At the recent price of
$264.02 (Aug 14, 2026 NASDAQ close) the price sits 12% *above* the conservative
value and at 51% of the optimistic one, so the margin against a conservative
estimate is negative even though the same price is a 30% discount to the
range's midpoint. That is the lesson in one company.

The record is thin in two honest places. The Graham quotations are from a book
this researcher could not open directly online; each is verified across several
independent reproductions and the wording is canonical, but page numbers vary
by edition and are not asserted. And the intrinsic-value range is one analyst's
defensible assumptions, not a fact the filing owns: the filing owns the inputs
(revenue, operating income, tax, cash flows, cash, debt, shares); the growth,
cost of capital, and terminal choices are estimates, labeled as such, and the
range would be a fiction if the writer presented the endpoints as anything but
the output of stated assumptions.

Note on the third quote: it is universally attributed to Graham and states this
lesson's thesis more directly than anything in Chapter 20's body, but some
sources place it in *Security Analysis* (Graham and Dodd) rather than *The
Intelligent Investor*. The writer should attribute it to Graham and, if citing a
specific book and page, confirm the edition. The idea is not contested; only the
exact location is.

```text
             uncertainty (from your own imperfect inputs and models) versus
```

## Contradictions

- **The anchor is a very high-quality business, which could look like it works
  against the lesson, and does not.** Adobe earns a 63% trailing return on
  invested capital, an 89% gross margin, and 97% recurring revenue. A stable
  earner is exactly the case the commission warned might make the margin concept
  "barely bite." It does not here, because the source of Adobe's uncertainty is
  not its current numbers but its future: the same generative-AI wave Adobe
  sells into can also commoditize creative software and shorten its moat. That
  uncertainty lives in the growth input, and the worked range shows it moving the
  value from $236 to $518 a share. The business quality is high and the value is
  still a wide range. That gap between "good business" and "known value" is the
  lesson's own point, so the anchor illustrates the framing rather than
  undercutting it. The market's ~29.5% one-year drop in Adobe's capitalization is
  consistent with the fear that growth lands at the low end.

- **The value is an estimate; the filing does not own it.** The filing owns the
  inputs. The growth rate, cost of capital, and terminal assumptions are the
  researcher's, chosen to be defensible, and the endpoints are only as honest as
  those labels. A reader who treats $236 or $518 as a fact the 10-K states would
  be misled. Both must travel with their assumptions.

- **Reported free cash flow flatters the owner.** Operating cash flow of
  $10,031M, less $179M capex, is $9,852M of "free cash flow," but that figure
  adds back $1,942M of stock-based compensation, a real cost that dilutes
  shareholders. The conservative case here is built from after-tax operating
  income ($7,104M), which already treats that compensation as the expense it is,
  rather than from the higher headline cash figure. A version built off reported
  free cash flow would print a larger, softer value. The writer should note which
  base is used and why.

- **Which value the margin is measured against changes the answer.** At $264.02
  the price is a 30% discount to the range midpoint ($377) yet a 12% *premium* to
  the conservative value ($236). An optimist anchoring on the midpoint sees a
  wide margin; a Graham-style investor anchoring on the conservative estimate
  sees none. This is not a contradiction in the data. It is the reason Graham
  ties the margin to a conservative estimate of value, and the writer should
  make the anchoring explicit rather than quote a single margin number.

### The worked range (two-stage free-cash-flow-to-firm model)

Both ends use the same $7,104M NOPAT base, the same +$385M net cash, and the
same 397.5M shares. They differ only in the four estimate inputs below.
Reinvestment each stage = growth / return on invested capital; free cash flow =
NOPAT x (1 - reinvestment rate); the explicit stage runs 10 years; terminal
value at year 10 = year-11 free cash flow / (cost of capital - terminal growth).

```text
Figure: CONSERVATIVE per-share intrinsic value = $236  ($235.66)
Owner:  Researcher estimate. Inputs, each an estimate, not from the filing:
        stage-1 growth 4.0%/yr; ROIC on growth 20% (reinvestment rate 20%);
        cost of capital (WACC) 9.5%; terminal growth 2.0% with terminal ROIC
        15% (reinvestment rate 13.3%).
Scope:  PV of 10-yr free cash flow $43,276M + PV of terminal value $50,014M
        = enterprise value $93,290M; + net cash $385M = equity $93,675M;
        / 397.5M shares. Terminal value is 54% of enterprise value.
```
```text

        stage-1 growth 11.0%/yr; ROIC on growth 40% (reinvestment rate 27.5%);
```

### Which inputs the range is most sensitive to

Moving one input at a time from the conservative base ($235.66), holding the
rest fixed. Growth dominates; cost of capital is second; the terminal-growth and
return-on-capital knobs move the value far less.

```text
Figure: Stage-1 growth 4% -> 11%:        $235.66 -> $329  (+$93 / share)
Figure: Cost of capital 9.5% -> 8.5%:     $235.66 -> $274  (+$39 / share)
Figure: ROIC on growth 20% -> 40%:        $235.66 -> $249  (+$14 / share)
Figure: Terminal growth 2% -> 3%:         $235.66 -> $245  (+$10 / share)
Figure: Terminal ROIC 15% -> 25%:         $235.66 -> $243  (+$8  / share)
Owner:  Researcher sensitivity on the model above
Scope:  One-input swings from the conservative case; growth is the swing factor
```
