# Evidence record: investing/valuation-multiples (01)

The record supports the full commission. The definitions of the price and
enterprise-value multiples, and the derivation that a multiple is a
discounted-cash-flow value in disguise, rest on Damodaran's *Investment
Valuation* Chapter 18 and his Relative Valuation lecture notes, which own those
claims as a valuation authority. The worked contrast rests on three real,
current companies verified against their own filings: NVIDIA (a fast grower),
The Coca-Cola Company (a slow, stable, high-return business), and Verizon (a
low-multiple business that is not cheap). Every fundamental figure (revenue,
earnings, EPS, operating income, debt, cash, equity, D&A) is taken from the
company's own results filing. Every current price and every ready-made P/E is
market data as of the close on August 7, 2026, sourced to stockanalysis.com.

The record is thin, and honestly so, in one place that the writer must handle
with care: the *current multiple* of a fast-growing company depends heavily on
which earnings the denominator uses. NVIDIA trades at 46 times its last full
fiscal year's earnings, about 34 times trailing-twelve-month earnings, and about
22 times forward earnings, because its earnings are still climbing fast. The
contrast the lesson draws is real at every denominator, but the writer must
state the denominator every time a multiple appears. See Contradictions.

The evidence does not undermine the commissioned angle. It supports it, with one
qualification the writer should keep visible: the derivation gives the multiple a
business *deserves* from its fundamentals (the "justified" multiple). It does not
prove the multiple the market is paying equals that justified number. Damodaran
is explicit that relative valuation can carry the market's own mispricing into
the estimate. The lesson's claim should be "different fundamentals justify
different multiples," not "the market's multiple is always the fundamentally
correct one."

             equity multiple whose numerator and denominator both belong to
             equity holders. Deriving the P/E from the stable-growth dividend

             equity (risk): it rises with the payout ratio and the growth rate
             (provided ROE exceeds the cost of equity) and falls as risk rises.
             The same logic on a firm-value model yields EV/EBITDA =
             [(1 - t) - (DA/EBITDA)(1 - t) - (Reinvestment/EBITDA)] / (WACC - g),
             whose five drivers are the tax rate, depreciation as a share of
             EBITDA, the reinvestment needed to fund growth, the cost of
             capital, and expected growth. Higher growth and lower cost of
             capital raise the multiple; heavier reinvestment lowers it.

             standardized values (P/E and variants, Value/EBIT, Value/EBITDA,

Paraphrase:  In relative valuation an asset's value is set by what the market
             pays for comparable assets. Prices cannot be compared directly, so
             they are standardized against a common variable (earnings, book
             value, revenue, EBITDA), and that standardizing creates a multiple.
             Proposition 1: numerator and denominator must belong to the same
             claimholders (equity value over equity earnings; firm value over
             firm earnings). Proposition 2: every multiple embeds all the
             variables that drive a discounted cash flow valuation (growth, risk,
             cash-flow patterns), and simple DCF algebra recovers them.
             Proposition 3: firms cannot be properly compared on a multiple
             unless the relationship between the fundamental and the multiple is
             known, and that relationship is not linear (a firm with twice the
             growth rarely trades at twice the P/E). Proposition 4: a comparable
             firm is one similar in fundamentals (risk, growth, cash flows), not
             merely in the same sector. The notes pose the trap directly: an
             analyst calling low-P/E stocks undervalued, or a low Value/EBITDA
             stock cheap, is only right after controlling for the differences in
             growth, risk, and reinvestment that justify the lower multiple.

## Contradictions

- **The denominator moves the multiple, most of all for the fast grower.** For
  NVIDIA, price / last-full-year GAAP diluted EPS is $223.96 / $4.90 = 45.7. The
  same price against trailing-twelve-month earnings is about 34 (stockanalysis
  trailing P/E 34.30, implying a TTM EPS near $6.53, well above the fiscal-2026
  figure because earnings kept climbing after January 2026). Against forward
  earnings it is about 22. All three are "the P/E," and they disagree by a factor
  of two. Damodaran names exactly this in the P/E definition section: current,
  trailing, and forward EPS give very different multiples for high-growth firms.
  The lesson must state the denominator on every multiple it prints. Coca-Cola
  and Verizon show the same effect faintly (KO 28.6 on FY2025 EPS vs 26.16
  trailing; VZ 11.6 on FY2025 EPS vs 12.26 trailing), because their earnings
  barely moved.

- **Justified multiple vs. observed multiple.** The derivation yields the
  multiple a business *deserves* given its growth, returns, and risk. It does not
  assert the market's quoted multiple equals that number. Damodaran's Proposition
  and his comparable-firm cautions warn that relative valuation inherits whatever
  mispricing sits in the comparison set, and that "controlling for differences"
  is far harder in practice than in theory. A reader should take from the lesson
  that fundamentals *justify* a spread in multiples, and that the observed
  multiple is a hypothesis about fundamentals to be tested, not a verdict.

- **A low multiple with shrinking earnings, not just slow ones.** Verizon is a
  sharper "cheap is not cheap" case than a simple low-growth story: revenue rose
  2.5% but net income attributable to Verizon and EPS both *fell* in 2025 ($17,174
  million vs $17,506 million; $4.06 vs $4.15), against $158.2 billion of debt and
  $17.0 billion of annual capital spending. The ~12x multiple is not a market
  error waiting to be arbitraged; it prices low growth, heavy reinvestment, and
  leverage. This strengthens the commission's angle rather than opposing it.

- **A source-count correction, resolved to the primary.** A secondary summary
  reported Coca-Cola's 2025 net income as $13,137 million. The company's own
  Exhibit 99.1 states net income attributable to shareowners of $13,107 million.
  The record uses the filing.

- **Entity confusion in the search layer, avoided.** A search result labeled
  "Coca-Cola ... full year 2025" pointed to Coca-Cola Consolidated (COKE, the
  bottler, CIK 317540: 2025 net sales $7,228.1 million), not The Coca-Cola
  Company (KO, CIK 21344: 2025 net operating revenues $47,941 million). All KO
  figures here come from CIK 21344's filing. See Discarded.

The teaching reading of the table: NVIDIA's very high multiple is what a 65%
grower earning ~76% on equity should command; the forward multiple (22) is far
below the trailing one precisely because the market expects earnings to keep
rising. Coca-Cola grows barely faster than the economy yet holds a ~27x multiple,
because it earns ~41% on equity at low risk: a stable, high-return, low-growth
business still deserves a premium multiple. Verizon looks "cheapest" at ~12x, but
it grows slowly, its per-share earnings fell in 2025, and it carries $158 billion
of debt against heavy annual capital spending; the low multiple is the price of
those fundamentals, not a discount to them.

### The justified-multiple derivation (formulas, from Damodaran Ch. 18 / notes)

```text
Justified (stable-growth) trailing P/E = Payout ratio x (1 + g) / (r - g)
Justified forward P/E                  = Payout ratio / (r - g)
with the sustainable-growth identity    Payout = 1 - g/ROE
  => Forward P/E = (1 - g/ROE) / (r - g)
Justified EV/EBITDA =
  [(1 - t) - (DA/EBITDA)(1 - t) - (Reinvestment/EBITDA)] / (WACC - g)
Firm growth identity: Expected growth in EBIT = Reinvestment rate x ROC,
  Reinvestment rate = (Cap ex - Depreciation + change in WC) / EBIT(1 - t)
```

Damodaran's own worked numbers, usable as the method's anchor:
- Illustration 18.1 (p. 473): five-year high growth at 25% with 20% payout, then
  8% stable growth at 50% payout, beta 1.0, cost of equity 11.5% -> justified
  P/E = 28.75. Implied ROE = growth / retention = 0.25/0.80 = 31.25% (high
  growth) and 0.08/0.50 = 16% (stable).
- Illustration 18.2 (p. 473): Procter & Gamble, 10% growth for five years then
  3% stable, payout 50% then 75% (75% = 1 - 3%/12% stable ROE), cost of equity
  8% then 8.5% -> justified P/E = 18.04, which times EPS of $3.82 reproduces the
  DCF value of $68.90.
- EV/EBITDA illustration (p. 504): cost of capital 10%, tax 36%, reinvestment
  25% of EBITDA, stable growth 5% -> justified EV/EBITDA = 5.24.

An illustrative reconstruction on the lesson's own companies (the growth rate g
and cost of capital r below are the researcher's clearly-labeled *estimates*,
not figures any filing owns; ROE is computed from the filings above). It exists
to show the writer the formula reproduces the real multiples, not to be printed
as fact:
- Coca-Cola: with ROE 41%, a long-run g of 4%, and a cost of equity r of 7%
  (KO's beta is well below 1), Forward P/E = (1 - 0.04/0.41)/(0.07 - 0.04) = 30,
  close to KO's observed ~27x. Low growth is offset by high ROE and low r.
- Verizon: with ROE 16%, g of 2.5%, and a higher cost of equity r of 8.5%
  (leverage and rate exposure), Forward P/E = (1 - 0.025/0.16)/(0.085 - 0.025) =
  14, near Verizon's observed ~12x. The higher discount rate and lower ROE pull
  the justified multiple down. The writer must own r and g as assumptions and
  can sensitize them; the point is that the formula, not sentiment, produces the
  spread.
