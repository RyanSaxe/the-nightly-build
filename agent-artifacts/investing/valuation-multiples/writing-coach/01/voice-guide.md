# Voice guide: investing/valuation-multiples (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson teaches a reader who can already run a discounted-cash-flow model
but has never been shown what a valuation multiple is. The math is welcome. The
finance is not assumed. Hold the register calm and precise, and build each part
of the multiple before the sentence that needs it. The patience should read as
respect for a capable reader, the way Michael Nielsen keeps addressing the
reader as a peer even while he spells out a cost function term by term.

When the piece introduces the justified multiple and derives it from growth,
reinvestment, and the cost of capital, it may first answer the objection the
reader is already forming: why build a multiple out of a model when the market
quotes one directly. Nielsen does this with his cost function before he uses it,
stopping to ask why a proxy is worth minimizing at all. Answering the question
the reader would raise, rather than walking past it, is available whenever an
equation is about to arrive.

A derived formula stays abstract until it is spent on a real case. The worked
contrast between a fast-growing business and a slow, stable one is where the
justified multiple becomes something the reader can feel, and the lesson's
concreteness is real companies and real figures. Where an analogy still helps
the reader picture what the algebra is doing, Nielsen's ball rolling to the
bottom of a valley shows how much a single familiar image can carry.

The lesson's harder turn asks the reader to read a multiple against the growth,
returns on capital, and risk behind it, instead of off its size alone. Bill
Gurley's essay on the lifetime-value formula reasons through this same kind of
trap: the inputs of a compressed formula pull against one another, and the
number is only as good as the assumptions folded into it. When the article
shows what a multiple silently encodes, Gurley's habit of taking a formula
apart input by input, and of stating plainly how much certainty it does and
does not earn, is the craft to draw on.

Each term of art the reader does not hold can be defined in the sentence it
first appears, the way Gurley states what lifetime value is in one plain
sentence before he uses it. Where an abstraction cannot be avoided, Damodaran
grounds one in something the reader already trusts, comparing a market's pricing
to Rotten Tomatoes and Yelp. And when the reader is likely to ask a direct
question, Damodaran answers it directly and in his own voice rather than
stepping around it.

## Aswath Damodaran, "Finding your investing lodestar: In Search of an Investment Philosophy"

Source: https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/03/finding-your-investing-lodestar-in.html

> "Do you have to pick a single philosophy? Not necessarily! You can meld two or even more than two philosophies together, as long as you meet two conditions."

Damodaran raises the question a reader would ask and answers it in two words
before laying out the conditions. The teaching voice is direct and unhurried,
and the willingness to write "Not necessarily!" and move straight on is his own.

> "When investor characteristics and investment philosophy needs are mismatched, there are two negative consequences. The first is that, lacking staying power, investors will abandon strategies well before they should, simply because they are uncomfortable with how they are playing out. The second is that a mismatch creates an emotional cost, where investors struggle with their portfolios and fail what I call the sleep test, where their portfolio's gyrations keep them awake at night."

He names two consequences, numbers them, and gives the second a plain label, the
sleep test, that the reader can carry away. The writing holds attention by
staying concrete about what a mismatch does to a person rather than asserting
that mismatches matter.

> "I never cease to marvel at markets, where millions of individuals with disparate views and information reach consensus on a price. In an age where we have turned over our choices on what movies to watch to Rotten Tomatoes, and which restaurant to eat at to Yelp!, it is worth remembering that markets were the original fount for crowd wisdom."

Damodaran takes an abstract claim, that a market price aggregates many people's
judgments, and grounds it in two services the reader already uses. His person
shows in the aside, the small marvel at something he has taught for decades.

## Bill Gurley, "The Dangerous Seduction of the Lifetime Value (LTV) Formula"

Source: https://abovethecrowd.com/2012/09/04/the-dangerous-seduction-of-the-lifetime-value-ltv-formula/

> "Lifetime value is the net present value of the profit stream of a customer. This concept, which appears on the surface to be quite benign, is typically used to compare the costs of acquiring a customer (often referred to as SAC, which stands for Subscriber Acquisition Costs) with the discounted positive cash flows that will come from that customer over time."

Gurley defines the formula in one plain sentence before he does anything with
it, and folds the acronym the field uses into a parenthesis so the reader is not
stranded. The prose is calm and exact, and the caution in "appears on the
surface to be quite benign" is the analyst's, set down without drama.

> "Tren's view is that the variables of the LTV formula are interdependent not independent, and are an overly simplified abstraction of reality. If you try to raise ARPU (price) you will naturally increase churn. If you try to grow faster by spending more on marketing, your SAC will rise (assuming a finite amount of opportunities to buy customers, which is true). Churn may rise also, as a more aggressive program will likely capture customers of a lower quality."

He works through the formula's inputs one at a time and shows how moving one
moves the others, so the reader sees the mechanism rather than a claim about it.
The reasoning stays concrete at each step, ARPU against churn and marketing
against acquisition cost, and Gurley's distrust of tidy models is visible all
the way through.

> "It's not "hard science" like say predicting gravity. It's at best a "good guess" about how the future will unfold. Businesses are complex adaptive systems that cannot be modeled with certainty. The future LTV results are simply predictions based on many assumptions that may or may not hold."

Gurley states how much certainty the formula deserves and how much it does not,
and softens neither side. The judgment that a model is a good guess rather than
a law of physics is his own, and he argues it instead of asserting it.

## Michael Nielsen, "Using neural nets to recognize handwritten digits" (Neural Networks and Deep Learning, Chapter 1)

Source: http://neuralnetworksanddeeplearning.com/chap1.html

> "Why introduce the quadratic cost? After all, aren't we primarily interested in the number of images correctly classified by the network? Why not try to maximize that number directly, rather than minimizing a proxy measure like the quadratic cost? The problem with that is that the number of images correctly classified is not a smooth function of the weights and biases in the network."

Nielsen anticipates the objection the reader is already forming, that a proxy
seems like the wrong thing to optimize, and answers it before continuing. The
questions are the reader's own, and his patience shows in the willingness to
stop and justify a choice most writers would slip past.

> "Fortunately, there is a beautiful analogy which suggests an algorithm which works pretty well. We start by thinking of our function as a kind of a valley. If you squint just a little at the plot above, that shouldn't be too hard. And we imagine a ball rolling down the slope of the valley. Our everyday experience tells us that the ball will eventually roll to the bottom of the valley."

He turns an abstract minimization problem into a ball rolling to the bottom of a
valley, an image the reader can hold without any calculus. The writing trusts a
plain physical picture to do real work, and the ease of "If you squint just a
little" is the sound of a person thinking aloud.

> "Please believe me when I say that it really does help to imagine C as a function of two variables. It just happens that sometimes that picture breaks down, and the last two paragraphs were dealing with such breakdowns. Good thinking about mathematics often involves juggling multiple intuitive pictures, learning when it's appropriate to use each picture, and when it's not."

Nielsen admits he has just confused the reader, and reassures them the picture is
still worth keeping. The honesty about his own explanation, and the closing
remark about holding several intuitions at once, show a teacher who respects the
reader enough to be candid about where the material is hard.
