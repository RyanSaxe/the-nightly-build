# Evidence record: investing/the-value-of-growth (01)

The evidence supports the lesson's central claim firmly and from recognized
valuation authorities: growth adds value only when the return earned on the
reinvested capital exceeds the cost of that capital, and growth funded below the
cost of capital destroys value. Two independent formulations carry the claim.
The McKinsey key value driver formula, Value = NOPLAT(1 − g/RONIC)/(WACC − g),
makes the sign change algebraic: with growth and starting profit held equal, a
higher return on new capital raises value, and when the return on new capital
equals the cost of capital the growth term drops out entirely, so growth is
value-neutral. Damodaran supplies the companion identity, growth = reinvestment
rate × return on capital, which shows why growth is never free: the reinvestment
that buys it is exactly what the return-versus-cost test is applied to. The
evidence is thin in one place the writer must respect. The clean sign change
lives inside a constant-growth perpetuity with a constant return on *new*
(marginal) capital; real firms show returns fading toward the cost of capital as
competition arrives, which the same authorities document. The formula supports
the sign-change claim cleanly as a statement about marginal reinvestment inside
the constant-growth model; it needs that scope stated, not softened.

## Contradictions

- **The sign change is clean only inside the model's assumptions.** The value
  driver formula (source 1) and Damodaran's table (source 3) produce the crisp
  create/neutral/destroy rule by assuming a single constant growth rate held to
  perpetuity, a constant return on *new* invested capital, and g below WACC. The
  rule is about the return on the marginal capital reinvested, not the firm's
  average historical ROIC. A writer who states the sign change on *average* ROIC
  overclaims. The honest scope is: within a constant-growth valuation, growth
  changes sign as the return on the reinvested (marginal) capital crosses the
  cost of capital.

- **Returns do not stay constant; they fade.** Sources 4 and 5 (McKinsey's own
  empirical work) show that high returns on capital, while more persistent than
  high growth, still tend to erode as competition arrives, and that firms earning
  below their cost of capital often cannot fund growth at all. This does not
  contradict the sign rule; it contradicts the implicit assumption that a firm
  can reinvest at a fixed above-cost return forever. The two-company worked
  example is a snapshot of the mechanism, not a claim that either firm holds its
  return indefinitely.

- **"Growth is good" as usually stated is the myth being corrected.** Damodaran
  frames the whole point as a correction of the common belief that more growth is
  always better (source 3). This is a genuinely held view the lesson corrects, not
  a strawman: McKinsey reports executives at high-ROIC firms who will not let
  returns fall to chase growth, and executives at low-ROIC firms who chase growth
  instead of fixing returns (source 4). The misconception is real and named, so
  the correction is earned rather than rhetorical.

- **No source disputes the core relationship.** Searching for a treatment holding
  that growth creates value independent of the return-versus-cost spread turned
  up none among recognized valuation authorities. The disagreements are about
  scope, persistence, and measurement (which ROIC, over what horizon), not about
  the direction of the effect.

```text

        Damodaran's "unchanged when ROIC = Cost of capital" (source 3)

        lesson's sign change turns on.
```
