# Voice guide: investing/competitive-advantage

## How this piece should sound

This lesson turns on a fact that is easy to state and hard to make felt: the
number a reader can see today, the current return on invested capital, matters
less than a number nobody can see, how long that return survives competition.
Damodaran's move below, opening on why the standard way of estimating an input
is unreliable before he offers his own, is the shape to borrow for the fade
itself. Name why comparing ROIC to the cost of capital in a single year invites
the reader to stop looking, before the lesson gives them a reason to keep
looking.

The worked calculation is the lesson's center of gravity, and Damodaran's habit
of letting a number carry the point rather than announcing it first is worth
holding onto here. When the lesson runs the same current ROIC and the same
growth rate through two different fade schedules, let the two resulting values
do the arguing. State the assumption, run the number, then say what it means,
in that order, the way his 4-percent-versus-6-percent aside does before he
tells the reader what it implies for a valuation.

Marks's dice paragraph and his ten-dollar-into-twenty-dollar example are both
doing the same job: taking a claim that fights intuition and making it checkable
by hand, in miniature, before it is applied to the real case. The claim this
lesson has to overcome is close cousin to his: a high ROIC today looks like the
whole story, the way a double in a year looks like proof the investment was
safe. A small, self-contained illustration the reader can verify before meeting
the two real businesses does more work here than restating the claim more
firmly.

Where the lesson has to make an abstraction, like the durability of an
advantage or the persistence of excess returns, felt rather than asserted,
Housel's habit of anchoring it in one figure the reader can hold is the model:
not "compounding rewards patience" but 99 percent of Buffett's net worth after
his 50th birthday. The equivalent move here is anchoring how much value rides on
duration in a number the reader can hold rather than in a restated abstraction.
His knob-and-lever image for what erodes a strategy's staying power is also
worth the reminder that a mechanism, like what erodes an advantage, usually has
a plainer image available than the abstract noun the field uses for it.

Damodaran's honesty about where even his preferred method falls short, freely
admitting his implied equity risk premium is a poor short-horizon predictor, is
worth matching wherever the lesson leans on an estimate the reader cannot
verify independently: nobody observes a competitive advantage period directly,
only infers one, and a reader who has just been shown a worked calculation is
entitled to be told plainly where the inputs to it are a judgment call. Per the
brief, the lesson enters through the concrete problem the fade assumption
creates rather than opening on a definition of competitive advantage, and the
body carries none of the direct address the two bookend cards are permitted.

## Aswath Damodaran, "The Price of Risk: An Equity Risk Premium Monologue!"

Source: https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/03/the-price-of-risk-equity-risk-premium.html

> "I start my valuation classes with a question of whether valuation is an art
> or a science, and I argue that it is neither; it does not have the precision
> that characterizes a science and unlike an art, it does come with principles
> that constrain you on what you can and cannot do. I describe valuation as a
> craft, where you learn as you value companies, and in the process, there are
> times where you question how it is practiced, and try to find ways to do it
> better."

This is the opening sentence of the piece, and it does the work an opening
sentence has to do: it sets up a real question (is this an art or a science)
and answers it before moving on, rather than gesturing at the subject's
importance. The first-person voice, "I describe," "I argue," is doing the
teaching here as much as the content is; the reader is watching one person
reason, not being handed a conclusion.

> "Put simply, sticking with a 4% equity risk premium during a crisis, when the
> implied risk premium has surged to 6% will lead you to find most companies to
> be undervalued, almost entirely because you think that the market is
> undervalued (not the company). In my view, a company valuation should be
> market-neutral, and the only way you can get there is by using a current
> implied equity premium."

The two specific numbers, 4% and 6%, do more than illustrate the point; they
are the point, because the sentence would say nothing without them. Notice
that the payoff, "almost entirely because you think the market is undervalued,"
comes right after the numbers rather than before them: he lets the reader watch
the mechanism produce the distortion instead of naming the distortion and
then supplying an example of it.

> "Since a good ERP estimate should have a large positive correlation with
> actual returns on stocks in future years, the current implied premium does
> best for the five-year and ten-year return, and the historical risk premium
> does worst, with actual returns increasing (decreasing) when it decreases
> (increases). In bad news for market timers, none of the equity risk premium
> approaches does well at forecasting next year's actual return, and even at
> the longer time periods, there is significant error in predictions."

This is Damodaran grading his own preferred method and reporting that it still
fails at the one-year horizon. A writer confident enough to report where their
own tool falls short, in the same plain sentence structure used for its
successes, is more credible than one who only reports the wins.

## Morgan Housel, "Last Man Standing"

Source: https://collabfund.com/blog/standing/

> "Everything worthwhile in investing comes from compounding. Compounding is
> the whole secret sauce, the rocket fuel, that creates fortunes. And
> compounding is just returns leveraged with time. Earning a 20% return in one
> year is neat. Doing it for three years is cool. Earning 20% per year for 30
> years creates something so extraordinary it's hard to fathom."

The three short sentences that escalate, "neat," "cool," "hard to fathom,"
build the reader's intuition for what a long time horizon does to a return
before any figure appears. The words are plain and almost conversational,
which is what makes the escalation legible: nothing is competing with the idea
for the reader's attention.

> "The time component of compounding is why 99% of Warren Buffett's net worth
> came after his 50th birthday, and 97% came after he turned 65. Yes, he's a
> good investor. But a lot of people are good investors. Buffett's secret is
> that he's been a good investor for 80 years. His secret is time. Most
> investing secrets are."

Four short declaratives in a row, each one under six words, land the point
harder than a single elaborated sentence would. The specific figures, 99%, 97%,
50th birthday, 65, are what let the reader check the claim rather than take it
on faith.

> "Complexity is another door to interruption. It can produce higher returns.
> But the more knobs you have to fiddle with, the more levers you have to
> pull, the higher the odds that something, at some point, will cause you to
> second-guess yourself, or reveal a risk you hadn't considered, or tempt your
> clients to leave – all of which stops the clock of compounding and can
> outweigh any return advantage you had when the strategy worked."

"Knobs" and "levers" turn an abstract claim about strategy complexity into
something physical the reader can picture, and the sentence keeps piling on
concrete ways complexity bites before it ever states the general principle.

## Howard Marks, "Risk Revisited"

Source: https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2014-09-03-risk-revisited.pdf?sfvrsn=2

> "If you buy something for $10 and sell it a year later for $20, was it risky
> or not? The novice would say the profit proves it was safe, while the
> academic would say it was clearly risky, since the only way to make 100% in a
> year is by taking a lot of risk. I'd say it might have been a brilliant, safe
> investment that was sure to double or a risky dart throw that got lucky."

Marks poses a question a reader can answer for themselves before he answers it,
and gives two wrong answers their full due before offering his own. The two
wrong answers are named, not caricatured, which is what makes his own answer
land as a genuine third option rather than a scored point.

> "For example, every good backgammon player knows the probabilities governing
> throws of the dice. They know there are 36 possible outcomes, and that six of
> them add up to the number seven (1-6, 2-5, 3-4, 4-3, 5-2 and 6-1). Thus the
> chance of throwing a seven on any toss is 6 in 36, or 16.7%. There's
> absolutely no doubt about that. But even though we know the probability of
> each number, we're far from knowing what number will come up on a given
> roll."

The counterintuitive claim, that knowing a probability precisely tells you
nothing about a single outcome, is made checkable before it's applied to
investing: the reader can count the 36 combinations themselves. The precision
of "6 in 36, or 16.7%" is what earns the sentence that follows it.

> "Think again about the quote above from Elroy Dimson: 'Risk means more
> things can happen than will happen.' I find it particularly helpful to
> invert Dimson's observation for key point number four: Even though many
> things can happen, only one will."

Marks names his source, quotes him exactly, and then does visible work on the
quotation, inverting it, rather than dropping it in as decoration. Borrowing a
line and then turning it over is a different move from citing an authority to
close an argument.
