# Evidence record: investing/competitive-advantage (01)

This record supports the commissioned angle that the durability of excess returns,
not their current level, drives most of a company's value and most of the
uncertainty in valuing it. The economic argument and its evidence are established
from sources that own them: Mauboussin's "Measuring the Moat" for the two
dimensions of value creation (the ROIC-minus-cost-of-capital spread and how long
it lasts), the competitive life cycle, the regression-toward-the-mean data by
industry, and the taxonomy of advantage sources; Porter's 2008 HBR article for
the barriers-to-entry taxonomy those sources map to; Warren Buffett's 2007
Berkshire letter for the durable-moat framing and, usefully, his own record of a
moat that vanished (Dexter Shoe); and McKinsey (Jiang and Koller) for an
independent long-run finding that the market's median ROIC is stable and industry
returns persist. The worked example contrasts Copart (a two-sided salvage-auction
network with a land-and-scale moat) against Crocs (a footwear brand whose returns
are exposed to taste and imitation), both earning roughly 27-30% on invested
capital in a normal recent year, with figures taken from their own 10-Ks. Crocs
supplies rare direct fade evidence twice over: an operating collapse in 2008-2009,
and a $737 million write-down of the HEYDUDE brand it bought for $2.3 billion.

The record is thin in three specific places, flagged so the editor can test them.
First, the two anchor companies are not at an identical starting ROIC and are in
different industries, so the worked "same ROIC, same growth, different fade"
calculation must hold those inputs equal by construction; the real figures justify
different fade rates and prove both firms earn well above their cost of capital
today, they do not supply an identical starting point. Second, mean reversion is a
statistical tendency with wide variance driven substantially by luck, not a fixed
schedule; Mauboussin and McKinsey both show meaningful persistence, so the lesson
must not overstate that fade is predictable. Third, identifying a durable moat in
advance is genuinely hard: Buffett, who coined the metaphor, misjudged Dexter Shoe,
and Crocs looked like a high-return brand at its 2007 peak. The evidence does not
undermine the angle; it sharpens the humility the angle already asks for.

## Contradictions

- Mean reversion is a tendency, not a schedule, and the same sources that support
  fade also show durable persistence. Mauboussin's Exhibit 34 reports year-to-year
  CFROI correlations from 0.73 (Diversified Financials) to 0.95 (Food, Beverage &
  Tobacco; Household & Personal Products); a 0.95 correlation means consumer-staples
  returns fade very slowly. McKinsey finds industry return rankings persisted from
  1963 to 2004. A lesson that presents fade as automatic or fast for all firms
  overstates the evidence. The honest reading: returns tend toward a central level,
  but the rate varies widely by industry and firm, which is exactly why the fade
  assumption is where judgment lives.
- Identifying a durable moat in advance is contested and hard. Mauboussin states
  outright that sustained value creation from skill alone is rare and that luck
  drives much of regression, implying that some observed persistence is chance
  rather than moat. Buffett, the source of the moat metaphor, misjudged Dexter Shoe
  and said so in the 2007 letter. Crocs earned $237.8M of operating income at its
  2007 peak and then lost money in 2008-2009. So high current returns are weak
  evidence of a moat; the lesson's own worked example (Crocs) is a case where the
  moat call, made on the numbers alone, would have been wrong.
- The two anchor companies are not at an identical starting ROIC and sit in
  different industries. Copart earns roughly 30% on invested capital; Crocs earned
  roughly 27% in its clean FY2024 and far less in FY2025 once impairments hit. The
  commission's "same ROIC, same growth, different fade" comparison is therefore a
  stylized construction: the writer must hold current ROIC and growth equal by
  assumption and vary only the fade period, using the real figures to justify why
  one fade is slower than the other (Copart's network and land versus Crocs' taste
  exposure) and to show both firms currently out-earn their cost of capital. The
  figures do not supply a naturally identical starting point, and any draft that
  implies they do should be corrected.
- CFROI is not identical to ROIC. Mauboussin's HOLT exhibits use cash flow return
  on investment, an inflation-adjusted cash-based metric, not the accounting ROIC
  the course has taught. The direction of the evidence (returns regress; some
  industries regress slowly) transfers, but the exhibit's exact numbers should be
  presented as CFROI, not relabeled ROIC.
