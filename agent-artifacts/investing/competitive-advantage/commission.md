# Commission: investing/competitive-advantage

## Assignment

One lesson for the Investing Course. Template `lesson` (1200-2200 words, min 6
sources; the `why` and `takeaway` bookend cards frame the lesson and may address
the reader; everything between them is ordinary prose held to the full standard).
Authorized scheduled work from `nb duty`: open slot, one article. The published
lessons are the syllabus; do not repeat a taught concept.

## Where this sits in the course

The course has built, in order: how a business earns a profit; profit versus
cash; the balance sheet; return on invested capital; cost of capital; present
value; free cash flow; discounted cash flow; valuation multiples; and, most
recently, the value of growth. That last lesson established the course's current
frontier: growth creates value only when the return on invested capital exceeds
the cost of capital. It left a load-bearing assumption unexamined. A discounted
cash flow's terminal value, and the whole "growth is worth more at higher ROIC"
result, both silently assume the excess return persists. It usually does not.

This lesson teaches the concept that governs that assumption: competitive
advantage, and the fade of excess returns. It is the natural next prerequisite,
and the course cannot reach an honest valuation judgment without it.

## What the lesson teaches

Teach why a return on invested capital above the cost of capital is competed away
over time, what a competitive advantage is in economic terms (a reason the excess
return does not fade on the usual schedule: switching costs, network effects,
scale economies, intangibles, or a cost or price advantage the incumbent can
defend), and how the durability of the advantage — how long and how completely the
excess return persists — drives value far more than the current level of ROIC
does. Make it concrete: show, with a worked calculation, how two businesses at the
same current ROIC and growth are worth very different amounts when one's advantage
fades in a few years and the other's persists for many, so the reader sees that
the fade assumption, not the headline return, is where most of the value and most
of the judgment live. Connect it back to the terminal value from the DCF lesson,
where the fade assumption is usually buried. Keep what it teaches transferable:
the reader should leave able to ask, of any business, what the advantage is, why
it should last, and what would end it.

## Sources

`min_sources` 6. Anchor to sources that own the concept and its evidence: Michael
Mauboussin's work on the fade of returns on capital and the competitive advantage
period (his measuring-the-moat and cash-economics writing); Warren Buffett's own
statements on durable competitive advantage from Berkshire's letters; Michael
Porter on the sources of competitive advantage; empirical work on the mean
reversion of ROIC across firms; and the specific figures for whichever real
companies anchor the worked example, from their own filings. Prefer the primary
that owns each claim. Every URL resolves to the source's own page.

## Required contribution

The reader finishes able to see that most of a company's value, and most of the
uncertainty in valuing it, lives in how long its returns stay above the cost of
capital, not in how high they are today; able to name the specific economic
sources of a durable advantage; and able to interrogate the fade assumption hiding
in a terminal value. The lesson does original work by making the fade rate, not
the current return, the thing the reader learns to price.

## Habits not to inherit

- The last three lessons leaned on the "ROIC versus cost of capital" framing and
  several reused the same anchor companies (Costco appears repeatedly; Coca-Cola,
  Verizon, Apple, and American Electric Power have all anchored recent lessons).
  Use that framing as the foundation it now is, but pick fresh anchor companies
  for the worked example so the course does not read as one long Costco case.
- Recent lesson deks state a result as a bare declarative ("Growth creates value
  only when it out-earns the cost of capital"; "Every valuation multiple hides a
  discounted cash flow"). Do not stamp this dek to that same mold.
- Recent lesson headings are short flat declaratives ("Growth has a price", "The
  return on that capital sets the value"). Vary how the headings are built and
  keep each one in this lesson's own nouns.
- Do not open on the definition; enter through the concrete problem the fade
  assumption creates. Keep the `takeaway` bookend on what the reader can now do,
  not a generic flourish.
