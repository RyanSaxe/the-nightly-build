# Voice guide: investing/reverse-dcf (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson in the Investing Course, and its subject is the inversion of a
discounted-cash-flow model: holding a company's current price fixed and solving
for the growth, margins, and returns on capital the market must be assuming, then
judging whether a company like this one can deliver them. The reader is
quantitative and has already been taught present value, free cash flow, the DCF
and its terminal value, cost of capital, return on invested capital, the value of
growth, valuation multiples, and margin of safety. Write for someone who follows
arithmetic without hand-holding and has no patience for padding, but who is
meeting this one move, running the model backward from price to expectations, for
the first time.

Let the numbers carry the explanation, the way Mauboussin's $1,000 factory and
Damodaran's $100 million firm do. The inversion is abstract stated in words and
concrete the instant a real price and a real set of implied assumptions sit on the
table. When the method needs a company, one company taken all the way through the
inversion is enough: the reader should watch the price go in and the implied
growth and return assumptions come out, and be able to repeat the step. A figure
the reader cannot scale on their own, an implied growth rate or an implied margin,
asks for the kind of anchor Maggiulli reaches for when he sets $500,000 beside
$153,000, whether that anchor is the company's own past, its industry, or the
record of firms its size.

The plain first person in Damodaran ("I have a simpler proxy," "I think you will
agree") and the reader-facing voice in Maggiulli belong here to the two bookend
cards, the one place the lesson speaks to its reader. The body speaks to no one and
works in the calm, precise register the paper keeps: name the step, show the
arithmetic, and let the order of the sections do the persuading. Where Mauboussin
pauses to say what a calculation is for, this lesson has the same opening to tie
the inversion back to the decision it serves.

The move most worth studying is the one Maggiulli makes against Buy the Dip and
Mauboussin makes with the $1 test: set up the expectation the reader already holds,
grant it, then put a number against it. Reading implied expectations has that
shape, since the market has already committed to a number and the lesson's work is
to expose what stands behind it and ask what it would take to be right. Where the
implied assumptions run past anything a company that size has done, the comparison
that shows it can be laid out plainly, the way Damodaran names the weak points in
his own estimates and still commits to the reading he thinks they support. A
verdict on whether the price's expectations are achievable is earned once the
inversion and that comparison are on the page.

## Aswath Damodaran, "Growth (Part 3): The Value of Growth"

Source: https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/10/growth-part-3-value-of-growth.html

> "Consider a firm that has $100 million invested in capital that generated $10 million in after-tax income in the most recent year. For this firm to generate more income next year, it has to do one of two things:"

He opens the whole idea on a single made-up company with two round numbers, and
everything that follows is worked on those figures rather than stated in the
abstract. The reader can hold $100 million and $10 million in their head, so when
the argument later depends on those figures they are working from numbers they
already have. Damodaran the classroom teacher is visible in starting from the
smallest example that carries the point.

> "For those of you who do not want to go through the process of computing return on capital and cost of capital, I have a simpler proxy for measuring the quality of growth. Start by computing the capital invested thus:"

He notices that not every reader will do the full calculation and hands them a
shorter route without dropping the rigor. The plain first-person offer, "I have a
simpler proxy," is a writer tracking what the person on the other side can and
cannot follow. It shows him meeting a real reader where they are rather than
writing to an idealized one.

> "While you may quibble with the year and have issues with how I computed cost of capital and return on capital, I think you will agree that value destruction is far more common at companies than we would like to believe and that quality growth (that increases value) is rare."

He names the weaknesses in his own numbers before the reader can, then still
commits to the conclusion he thinks they support. The candor of "you may quibble
... I think you will agree" is a person standing behind a claim while admitting its
limits. The judgment is plain and the grounds for it sit in the same sentence.

## Michael J. Mauboussin (with Dan Callahan), "Calculating Return on Invested Capital: How to Determine ROIC and Address Common Issues"

Source: https://www.shareholderforum.com/returns/Library/20140604_Mauboussin-Callahan.pdf

> "Here's an extremely simple example. Say a company invests $1,000 in a new factory and estimates that the cost of capital is 10 percent. Were the factory to generate $80 in after-tax earnings into perpetuity, the market value of the factory would be $800 ($80/.10) and the investment would fail the $1 test. Earnings of $120 would create value of $1,200 ($120/.10), hence passing the $1 test."

He runs the arithmetic inside the sentence for both cases and leaves the division
visible ($80/.10), so the reader can reproduce the result instead of trusting it.
The two earnings figures sit on either side of the break-even point, and the reader
sees exactly where a dollar invested starts to create value. The number is doing
the explaining, which is how the whole piece teaches.

> "ROIC is preferable to ROE because ROIC is unaffected by financial leverage and share buybacks whereas ROE varies based on a firm's chosen capital structure and buyback activity. In measuring a company's economic returns, we don't want to conflate operating and financing issues."

He justifies a choice of measure by saying precisely what each one does and does
not respond to, then gives the one-line reason underneath. The reasoning is shown,
not asserted, and stated plainly enough for the reader to repeat it. This is a
writer explaining why he reached for one tool over another instead of just naming
the one he uses.

> "You should not view the calculation of ROIC as an academic exercise. The goal is to understand the magnitude and sustainability of ROICs in excess of the cost of capital."

In the middle of a technical piece he stops to say what the number is for, tying
the mechanics back to the question the reader actually has. It keeps the
calculation from floating free of any purpose. The teacher who knows why a formula
matters, and not only how it runs, is visible here.

## Nick Maggiulli, "Even God Couldn't Beat Dollar-Cost Averaging"

Source: https://ofdollarsanddata.com/even-god-couldnt-beat-dollar-cost-averaging/

> "Imagine you are dropped somewhere in history between 1920 and 1979 and you have to invest in the U.S. stock market for the next 40 years. You have 2 investment strategies to choose from."

He sets up a concrete game the reader can play before any result appears, which
gives the reader a stake in the answer that a general question would not. The
specific range of years and the two named choices put the reader inside a small,
rule-bound world the numbers will then decide. Maggiulli the data writer is
visible in building that world first and letting the data settle it.

> "Logically, it seems like Buy the Dip can't lose. If you know when you are at a bottom, you can always buy at the cheapest price relative to the all-time highs in that period. However, if you actually run this strategy you will see that Buy the Dip underperforms DCA over 70% of the time."

He states the intuition the reader almost certainly holds, grants that it sounds
right, and only then puts a single number against it. The reversal lands because he
built the expectation first rather than knocking down a position no one held. The
plainspoken "if you actually run this strategy" is a writer who trusts the data
over the story.

> "For example, the best 40-year period between 1920 and 1979 was from 1922-1961, where your $48,000 (40 years * 12 months * $100) in total purchases grew to over $500,000. Compare this to the worst period 1942-1981, where your $48,000 in total purchases only grew to $153,000. That is a difference of 226%, which is much larger than any divergences we saw between the DCA and Buy the Dip timing strategies!"

He anchors an abstract point about luck in two dollar amounts the reader can
compare directly, and shows the arithmetic behind the $48,000 so nothing is hidden.
Putting $500,000 next to $153,000 makes the size of the effect visible rather than
asserted. The surprise in the closing figure is earned by the numbers, which is why
the line reads as a person reacting and not a slogan.
