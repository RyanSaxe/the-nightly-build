# Voice guide: investing/capital-allocation (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson teaches a judgment, not a formula: which of five uses of cash
creates value, and why the answer for a buyback turns on nothing but the
price paid. The reader can already do the arithmetic (return on capital,
cost of capital, present value are prerequisites); what is new is the
discipline of applying one test five times without letting the business's
size or the writer's own enthusiasm soften it. That calls for a voice that
stays plain and declarative through every one of the five cases and resists
the temptation to editorialize about a company's discipline instead of
showing it. The reader should finish able to run the same test on a company
this lesson never mentions.

Two exemplars, read for craft rather than content: a plain-spoken teacher who
makes an abstract discipline vivid with one concrete image or one admitted
failure, and an academic who states a principle in the fewest possible words
before ever reaching for an example. Both are already sources for this
lesson's claims; the passages below are different ones, chosen for how they
teach rather than what they say, so the writer inherits craft and not a
line to reuse.

Watch where the human voice is allowed to speak. In this template that
directness belongs to the two bookend cards, which may address the reader
("you have learned," "by the end you will be able to"). The body between
them explains the test to no one in particular, names the company's
decisions without narrating the lesson itself, and never uses the bookends'
second person.

## Warren Buffett, Berkshire Hathaway's 2012 Chairman's Letter

Source: https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2012ltr.pdf

> "Your chairman has not been free of this sin. In Berkshire's 1986 annual
> report, I described how twenty years of management effort and capital
> improvements in our original textile business were an exercise in
> futility. I wanted the business to succeed and wished my way into a series
> of bad decisions. (I even bought another New England textile company.) But
> wishing makes dreams come true only in Disney movies; it's poison in
> business."

Buffett is explaining an abstract failure mode, reinvesting past the point a
test justifies, and he does it by admitting he committed it himself rather
than describing it in the third person. The concrete detail (twenty years, a
second textile company bought on top of the first) makes the abstraction
checkable, and the plain aphorism at the end earns its place because the
admission just built it. The lesson can borrow the move, not the sentence: a
company's own numbers standing in for a warning, rather than a general
claim about discipline.

> "It's hard to go wrong when you're buying dollar bills for 80¢ or less."

One image carries the entire logic of a disciplined repurchase, stated in a
denomination anyone holds in their pocket. Buffett does not define "value"
or "price" abstractly here; he trusts a single sentence, keyed to money the
reader has actually touched, to do the explaining. Where this lesson turns
the same idea into algebra, the algebra should still resolve to a sentence
this plain.

## Aswath Damodaran, "Returning Cash to the Owners: Dividend Policy"

Source: https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/cf2E/divid.pdf

> "If there are not enough investments that earn the hurdle rate, return the
> cash to stockholders."

Damodaran states the entire payout decision in one sentence before any
company, any number, or any example of a dividend appears. The principle is
named first, at its most compressed, and only afterward does the deck spend
its slides on mechanics. This lesson's orientation section owes it the same
order: state the test the five uses share before walking through what each
use looks like in a single company's filing.

> "The form of returns - dividends and stock buybacks - will depend upon
> the stockholders' characteristics."

A short qualifying sentence, placed immediately after the main rule, that
keeps the professor from overclaiming: the test decides *whether* to pay
out, a separate question from *how*. The lesson's dividend-versus-buyback
distinction, that only one of the two is priced and only one can destroy
value at the wrong price, is the same kind of immediate qualification
protecting a clean rule from being read as broader than it is.
