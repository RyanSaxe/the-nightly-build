# Voice guide — word-of-the-day/shibboleth

## Directive

Register: a well-read technical reader, not a novice. Explain the Hebrew and
the Judges passage precisely, but never pause to define ordinary words or
apologize for the subject. No condescension, no padding — this is the
edition's shortest read and every sentence should earn its place.

Moves that will actually change sentences in this draft:

1. **Open on the scene, not on a definition or a namer.** The fords of the
   Jordan, the men of Gilead checking every fugitive who tried to cross, is
   the natural opening because shibboleth has no coiner to put in a sentence's
   subject slot. Start where the test is happening, with the people doing it,
   before naming the word. This also sidesteps the two openings the recent
   library has used up (see below).

2. **Keep the origin and the modern sense on one line of argument, not two
   sections.** The commission's whole payload is the throughline from
   "a word an outsider's mouth cannot form" to "a marker an outsider cannot
   fake" — say the mechanism once, in the ancient scene, then show it is the
   same mechanism at work in the modern example. Do not narrate the ancient
   part, stop, and restart on "today." The reader should feel one argument
   extending, not two essays stapled together.

3. **Make the Hebrew a fact, not a hedge.** The š/s distinction and the
   ear-of-grain/stream ambiguity are real, checkable linguistic facts —
   state them the way you'd state a measurement, not as a "some say... others
   say" shrug. It is fine to say scholars disagree about the word's literal
   sense; it is not fine to let that disagreement soften the sentence's
   confidence about what happened at the river.

4. **Sentences short, one clause doing one job, with a single longer sentence
   allowed at the pivot** from the ancient scene to the modern one, since that
   is the one place the piece needs momentum rather than a full stop. Elsewhere,
   trust the period. At 550-800 words there's no room for a sentence that
   needs a second read.

5. **Name the mechanism at the end, don't moralize it.** The close should
   state, in the piece's own terms, what makes a shibboleth different from a
   password or a test — that the tell is involuntary and bound to identity —
   and then stop. No "and that's what makes it so powerful," no lesson about
   belonging in general.

6. **Headings, if used, name a step in this argument** (what the test found,
   how the word traveled, what it marks now) — never a label that would sit
   over any word's history unchanged.

Recently used, do not reuse: a coiner or namer as the sentence's grammatical
subject ("X named it," "Y coined it"); the "named for the wrong person"
reveal; the eponym frame of "a person's name becomes a common noun through a
[historical event]" (already the shape of quisling — shibboleth has no
eponym, so this should not come up, but do not reach for an equivalent "word
becomes shorthand for X through Y" formula in its place); Background/Origins/
Today-style scaffold headings; a heading built as two clauses joined by a
comma and "and."

## Exemplars

### Merriam-Webster, "Top 10 Words from People's Names" (boycott, maverick entries)
Source: https://merriam-webster.com/words-at-play/top-10-words-from-peoples-names/boycott
Craft:
- cadence: short, plain declarative sentences, one fact per sentence, no throat-clearing before the scene starts.
- argument: compresses a whole biography to the single event that produced the sense, then states the semantic leap flatly, in one sentence.
- evidence: one concrete, dated scene (a rent standoff, a bad debt paid in cattle) stands in for the whole history rather than a survey of later citations.
- stance: no editorializing; the incongruity of the situation (a lawyer who becomes a word for refusing to conform) is left to supply the interest on its own.
- notice: picks the one detail that explains *why* the sense generalized — Maverick's cattle stood out because he had no ranching habit, not just because they were unbranded.
- diction: unadorned nouns and verbs; adjectives are rationed.
- reader: assumes curiosity, not expertise; never explains what a debt or a rent increase is.
- the move the axes miss: it stops the instant the semantic shift is named. No coda, no "and that's why we still say."
Calibration: "Samuel A. Maverick is famous for what he didn't do."

### Ben Zimmer, "Word on the Street" (Wall Street Journal language column)
Source: https://benzimmer.com/wall-street-journal/ (column index; see "Refugee, From French Protestants to Mediterranean Immigrants," Sept. 5, 2015)
Craft:
- cadence: one continuous line from the originating event to the present controversy, no hard section break between "then" and "now."
- argument: follows a single word across what Zimmer himself calls its passage through "cultural, social, and political worlds" — one thread pulled taut, not a survey of every sense the word has carried.
- evidence: anchors each stage to a named, dated event (17th-century Huguenot flight) before naming the modern parallel, so the modern claim is never asserted without the history under it.
- stance: lets the word's own history carry the weight of the modern comparison; the parallel is shown by the sequence of facts, not announced.
- notice: chooses a modern flashpoint where the old and new senses are in genuine tension, not merely an instance of the word in the news.
- diction: reporter's plain style — dates, named actors, no "in ancient times" vagueness.
- reader: general newspaper reader, no linguistics background assumed, full current-events literacy assumed.
- the move the axes miss: the etymology is never trivia for its own sake — the old scene is included only because it explains the shape of the modern dispute.
Calibration: "a form of narrative lexicography, telling stories about words from their origins to how they moved through various cultural, social, and political worlds" (Zimmer's own description of his method).

### John McWhorter, "English Is Not Normal" (Aeon)
Source: https://aeon.co/essays/why-is-english-so-weirdly-different-from-other-languages
Craft:
- cadence: opens on a scene the reader already has in their own memory before naming the linguistic claim it illustrates.
- argument: piles up concrete, checkable examples first and lets the thesis arrive late, as a short flat sentence the examples have already earned.
- evidence: specific facts (a counting rhyme's borrowed numbers) rather than general claims about language.
- stance: confident, willing to contradict a popular assumption without hedging it or softening it with "not X but Y."
- notice: finds the strange fact hiding inside something the reader considered ordinary or childish.
- diction: conversational, contractions, direct address, but each sentence still does exactly one piece of work.
- reader: treated as smart but not a specialist — explained without being talked down to.
- the move the axes miss: the topic sentence is withheld until the examples have made it inevitable, so it never reads as an assertion the reader has to take on faith.
Calibration: "When saying 'eeny, meeny, miny, moe', have you ever felt like you were kind of counting? Well, you are — in Celtic numbers."
