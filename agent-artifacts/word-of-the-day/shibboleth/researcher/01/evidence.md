# Evidence record — word-of-the-day/shibboleth

The evidence supports every claim the brief asked for: the dictionary definition and
its two live senses, the Judges 12:5-6 episode read in two named translations plus the
Hebrew text itself, the sh/s phonetic mechanism, the 42,000 figure, the Hebrew
literal-meaning dispute (with the dispute honestly unresolved in scholarship), an
English-borrowing date, and a genuine, resolvable modern use in its generalized sense.
It is thin in exactly one place the brief anticipated: the OED itself is paywalled
(403, confirmed gated not dead) — I substituted etymonline (a scholarly-sourced,
citation-transparent etymology reference compiled from historical dictionaries
including the OED) plus a linguistics-department page and two philological references
on the Hebrew word, which together cover what the OED would have supplied. No source
below was recorded without being read directly; two SEO/listicle sites and one
paywalled dictionary were tried and discarded (see Discarded).

## Sources

### 1. Merriam-Webster, "shibboleth" (noun entry)
**URL:** https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/shibboleth
**Kind:** Primary — it is the dictionary; it owns the definition and pronunciation claims.
**What it establishes:** Part of speech (noun); MW respelling pronunciation
`ˈshi-bə-ləth` (MW uses its own respelling system, not IPA — do not present this as
IPA in the word card); two live sense clusters:
- Sense 1a: "a word or saying used by adherents of a party, sect, or belief and
  usually regarded by others as empty of real meaning"; 1b: "a widely held belief";
  1c: a truism/platitude.
- Sense 2a: "a use of language regarded as distinctive of a particular group"; 2b:
  "a custom or usage regarded as distinguishing one group from others."

The brief's two required senses map directly: the spoken-password sense is closest to
2a (distinctive language use); the broader custom/phrase/belief sense spans 1a/1b and
2b. MW's own etymology paragraph traces the word to Hebrew *shibbōleth*, glossed there
as "stream," and to Judges 12:4-6, and gives 1638 as the date of first known use in
English.
**Locator:** Noun entry, senses 1a–1c and 2a–2b; Etymology and "First Known Use" fields
on the same page.
**Candidate pull-quote (word card):** "a use of language regarded as distinctive of a
particular group" (sense 2a) — cleanest single-clause definition for the card.
**Candidate pull-quote (body, second sense):** "a custom or usage regarded as
distinguishing one group from others" (sense 2b).

### 4. Strong's Concordance / BDB lexicon data on Hebrew שִׁבֹּלֶת (transliterated shibbōleth), via BibleHub
**URL:** https://biblehub.com/hebrew/7641.htm
**Kind:** Primary — a lexicon entry is itself the reference work that owns the
"what does the Hebrew word literally mean" claim (it is not commentary about a
claim, it is the claim, sourced to Brown-Driver-Briggs and Strong's).
**What it establishes:** Hebrew שִׁבֹּלֶת (Strong's H7641), noun feminine, glossed
as "ear of grain, stream, or flood," related to H7640 (shobel, "skirt," a root sense
of "hanging/flowing down"). 19 total occurrences across the Hebrew Bible, including
grain senses (Genesis 41, the Pharaoh's-dream ears of grain; Ruth 2:2, gleaning) and
flood/water senses (Psalm 69, flood imagery; Isaiah 17:5, 27:12). Confirms Judges
12:6 as the occurrence where the word is used as the dialectal test.
**Locator:** Strong's H7641 entry, BibleHub Hebrew lexicon page.

### 5. Jewish Encyclopedia (1906), "Shibboleth"
**URL:** https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/13573-shibboleth
**Kind:** Secondary — a scholarly reference encyclopedia commenting on and explaining
the biblical text and the philological dispute; it does not itself own the Hebrew
text or the dictionary definition, but it is authoritative philological commentary,
not a listicle.
**What it establishes:** States the word's three attested literal senses in the
Hebrew Bible — "flood" (Isaiah 27:12; Psalms 69:3, 16), "ear of grain" (Job 24:24;
Genesis 41:5-6), and "branch" (Zechariah 4:12) — and explains the phonetic mechanism:
different Israelite tribes had different dialects, and the Ephraimite dialect lacked
the "sh" sound, rendering it "s." Notes explicitly that "any other word beginning
with 'sh' would have served as well" — i.e., the specific word's own meaning was not
the point of the test; its initial consonant was.
**Locator:** Full article text, Jewish Encyclopedia (1906), entry "Shibboleth."
**Candidate pull-quote (body, on the mechanism):** the observation that any sh-word
would have worked is useful for the article's core teaching point — the test
exploited an involuntary phonetic gap, not any property of "shibboleth" itself.

### 6. Douglas Harper, Online Etymology Dictionary ("etymonline"), "shibboleth"
**URL:** https://www.etymonline.com/word/shibboleth
**Kind:** Secondary — a compiled etymology reference (draws on the OED and other
historical dictionaries with citation-transparent methodology); used here as the
brief's permitted "scholarly substitute" because the OED itself returned 403
(confirmed paywalled, see Discarded).
**What it establishes:** English first attested "late 14c., in Bible translations."
Notes the Hebrew word meant "flood, stream" or "ear of corn," and that "modern
commentators believe it was used in the 'river' sense, referring to the Jordan" —
i.e., context (a river crossing) favors "stream," even though "ear of grain" is
independently well attested elsewhere in the Bible. Gives the figurative English
sense timeline: by the 1630s, "watchword or test-word or pet phrase of a party,
sect, school, etc."; by 1862, further widened to "outmoded slogan still adhered to."
Also gives a genuine historical parallel: Sicilian Vespers (1282), where Italians
used the word *cicera* ("chickpea") to expose French speakers who could not
pronounce it — a useful, real analog if the article wants one comparison beyond the
Bible, though the commission asks for one clean origin line, so this is background,
not a second example to include in the piece.
**Locator:** etymonline.com, "shibboleth" entry, full page.
**Note on dates:** MW's "1638" first-known-use and etymonline's "late 14c." (first
biblical-translation attestation) plus "1630s" (figurative sense) are not
contradictory — they describe different things: earliest English attestation of the
word at all (translations of the Bible, 14th c.) versus earliest attestation of the
*figurative* test-phrase sense (1630s/1638). Keep these distinct in the article; do
not collapse "shibboleth entered English" and "shibboleth became figurative" into one
date.

### 7. Balashon: Hebrew Language Detective, "shibolet" (2010)
**URL:** https://www.balashon.com/2010/01/shibolet.html
**Kind:** Secondary — a philology blog by a Hebrew-language researcher, engaging
directly with named lexicographers and their arguments; used for the literal-meaning
dispute specifically.
**What it establishes:** Lays out the two attested senses with their Biblical
occurrences — "ear of grain/corn" (Ruth 2:2, Isaiah 17:5) and "flowing stream/current"
(Psalms 69:2, Isaiah 27:12) — and the scholarly disagreement over whether they share
one root. Cites E. A. Speiser as arguing (in one analysis) that the two senses have
*separate* etymological roots, while Ernest Klein argues they share a single root
(a base sense of "hang down" or "move along," attested across Aramaic, Syriac,
Arabic, and Akkadian cognates), with grain "hanging" from a stalk and water "flowing
down" both derived from the same underlying idea. Also cites David B. Guralnik's view
that the "stream" sense fits the Judges narrative best because the episode happens at
a river crossing.
**Locator:** Balashon blog, "shibolet" post, January 2010.

is used by one set of people to identify another person as a member, or a
non-member, of a particular group."

## Contradictions

1. **Hebrew literal meaning: "ear of grain" vs. "stream/flood."** Both senses are
   independently well attested elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible (grain: Genesis 41,
   Ruth 2:2, Job 24:24; water: Psalms 69, Isaiah 27:12), and lexicographers disagree
   about which one the word carries in Judges 12 itself, and even about whether the
   two senses share one root or two. Strong's/BDB (source 4) lists both without
   resolving; the Jewish Encyclopedia (source 5) lists three senses (flood, ear of
   grain, branch) without picking one for Judges; Balashon (source 7) reports Speiser
   arguing for two separate roots and Klein arguing for one shared root, and reports
   Guralnik's view that context (a river crossing) favors "stream" for this
   particular episode. Etymonline (source 6) also flags "modern commentators believe
   it was used in the 'river' sense" without treating this as settled. **The article
   should state the ambiguity honestly rather than pick a side**: give both senses,
   note that context (a river ford) makes "stream" attractive to some scholars, and
   that this dispute is unresolved rather than a matter of one source being wrong.
   MW's own etymology paragraph picks "stream" without flagging the alternative —
   flag that MW is not exhaustive on this point.

2. **Date granularity.** MW's "1638" (first known use) and etymonline's "late 14c."
   (first English attestation, in Bible translation) and "1630s" (figurative
   test-word sense) are not in conflict but describe different milestones — see the
   note under source 6. Do not merge them into a single "coined in [year]" sentence
   (also disallowed by the commission's structural guidance against "coined in YEAR"
   openers).

3. **No contradiction found on the 42,000 figure or the sh/s phonetic mechanism.**
   Two independent named translations (JPS via Sefaria, NRSVUE via Bible Gateway)
   and the Masoretic Hebrew text itself all agree, and the Jewish Encyclopedia and
   Kemmer's linguistics page both restate the same figure without variation.

## Numbers

- **42,000**: the count of Ephraimites the text states died at the Jordan fords.
  Owning primary: Judges 12:6, Masoretic Hebrew text (אַרְבָּעִ֥ים וּשְׁנַ֖יִם
  אָֽלֶף, "forty and two thousand"), confirmed in both the JPS (Sefaria) and NRSVUE
  (Bible Gateway) English translations. Unit: a body count of one named tribe's
  fighters/fugitives, as given by the narrative itself; the text supplies no
  independent corroboration (e.g., archaeological or external record) and none was
  sought or claimed by any source here — treat as the number the text gives, not an
  externally verified historical death toll.
- **1638**: MW's "first known use" date for the English word. No comparable
  independent number to chart; this is a single dictionary-attestation date, not a
  series.
- **1630s / 1862**: etymonline's two milestone dates for, respectively, the
  figurative "test-word" sense and the further-widened "outmoded slogan" sense. Also
  single attestation dates, not a series.
- No chart is warranted. There is no numeric series here, only a single body count
  and a small number of attestation dates — prose citations suffice; see also
  Source assets below.
