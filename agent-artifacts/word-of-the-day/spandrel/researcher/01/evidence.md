# evidence: word-of-the-day/spandrel (01)

The record answers all four commission questions from primary sources and does
not undermine the commissioned angle. It supports firmly: the architectural
definition (Merriam-Webster and Britannica, both read); the 1979 coinage with
full citation and the exact San Marco passage read from the paper itself; the
terminological objection that the San Marco structures are pendentives, and
Gould's own 1997 answer to it, read from his reply; the present-day biological
sense as a defined term (Gould's 1997 definition); and a real, quoted use of
the word outside biology (Pinker and Bloom's 1990 language paper, which runs a
whole section titled "Arguments for Language Being a Spandrel"). The record is
thinnest on the pendentive objection's earliest and structural forms: Robert
Mark's 1996 structural rebuttal is behind a paywall and reaches the record only
through Philip Steadman's blog, the one secondary source here. Gould's paper,
however, names the two authors (Dennett and Houston) who pressed the
terminological objection he answers, so that objection is sourced first-hand.
One precision point recurs below: a spandrel is the byproduct itself; its later
co-option into a use is exaptation, a distinct term the commission's phrase
"came along for free and only later found a use" folds together.

## Contradictions

- The architectural terms are genuinely distinct, and the primaries confirm the
  objection is well founded on its face. Merriam-Webster and Britannica define a
  spandrel as the flat, roughly triangular space beside an arch; Britannica
  defines a pendentive as a curved segment of a sphere that carries a dome. The
  San Marco features under the dome are, by these definitions, pendentives.
  Gould concedes exactly this in 1997 ("using the pendentives of San Marco ...
  as an example") and does not claim they are spandrels in the strict sense.
  His defense is lexical, not factual: he broadens "spandrel" to "spaces left
  over." The article can hold the distinction honestly and still report Gould's
  answer, because Gould held it too.

- A second, stronger objection cuts at the metaphor, not the word, and partly
  works against the commission's premise that these are "necessary byproducts."
  Per Steadman, Robert Mark argued the pendentives are essential load-bearing
  structure, chosen deliberately, not incidental leftovers. This is the
  conceptual critique (ii) that Gould lists and answers in 1997 by insisting the
  properties he relied on, the number four and the triangular shape, are forced
  regardless of the pendentives' structural role. The commission itself walls
  this off ("the biology is here to explain what the word now marks, not to
  adjudicate the whole 1979 critique"), so it is context, not the spine. Sourced
  only through Steadman, it should be attributed carefully or left out, not
  stated as established fact.

- Precision, not a disagreement between sources: a spandrel is strictly the
  byproduct. When a byproduct is later co-opted into a use, that is exaptation
  (Gould and Vrba 1982), a separate term both Gould's 1997 paper and Pinker and
  Bloom keep distinct from "spandrel." The commission's gloss, "came along for
  free and only later found a use," describes an exapted spandrel, not the bare
  spandrel. The writer should keep the byproduct sense and the later-use sense
  named separately if both appear.
