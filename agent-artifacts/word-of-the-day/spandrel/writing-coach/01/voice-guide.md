# Voice guide: word-of-the-day/spandrel (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is the paper's smallest read: a few hundred words on one word, for a reader
who will follow a term of art the moment you define it and will not sit through
any throat-clearing before you do. Spandrel carries two architectural senses that
a loose sentence would blur, and a biological one laid on top: the spandrel proper
is the flat space beside an arch, the pendentive is the curved triangle under a
dome, and the sense Gould and Lewontin gave the word names a structural byproduct.
Give each of those the precision Anatoly Liberman gives the suffix of *hemlock*,
defining it where it first appears and keeping the sentence quick as you do. The
distinction between spandrel and pendentive is much of the pleasure of this piece,
so nothing is gained by smoothing it.

The commission asks you not to open on the abstract frame that states the
distinction before any story arrives. Melinda Baldwin's "scientist" piece and
Mark Forsyth's list both begin instead on something you can point at: a dated
scene, a named person, an oddity. Spandrel has San Marco, a 1979 paper, and two
authors carrying an architectural word across into evolutionary biology. If one of
those particulars is concrete enough to start on, the reader arrives at the word
already inside its story.

The pendentive objection is a genuine quarrel, and the commission asks you to keep
it standing rather than settle it. Baldwin lets Huxley and Argyll reject
"scientist" in their own phrasing and reports how many objected; the complaint
reads as a real complaint because she does not paraphrase it into something
milder. Where the architects' objection to Gould's usage is on the record, their
own terms can stand the same way.

Where the origin runs through contested or uncertain ground, two models keep you
honest without going slack. Forsyth sets two accounts of "Peru" beside each other,
dates the Spanish arrival, and says which reading he prefers without claiming the
matter is closed. Liberman ends *hemlock* hedged and specific, crediting his
source and stating exactly how much is known. Either fits a word whose crossing
from architecture to biology is documented and still argued over.

A dry wit is available, and the reliable kind comes from the true detail rather
than from a line built to be funny. Forsyth's Séchelles sentences work because a
two-year finance minister really is a sovereign state on the map, and the rhythm
lands because the sentences shorten toward the end. Spandrel's own facts can carry
whatever lightness the piece wants, so no sentence here needs to be engineered for
quotability.

## Mark Forsyth, "Countries Named After People"

Source: https://blog.inkyfool.com/2025/10/countries-named-after-people.html

> "The Seychelles were named in his honour because he happened to be the minister of finance when the islands were acquired by France in 1756. He had been in the post for a whole two years, and then he had a stroke and was replaced. His life was not extraordinary or eminent and yet he is on the map. He is a sovereign state. He has a seat at the UN."

The facts do the work: a two-year finance minister, a stroke, islands acquired in
1756. Forsyth keeps the sentences short and lets them get shorter at the close, so
three flat statements land the oddity with no joke laid on top. The delight is
Forsyth's, and it comes from the record rather than from a line written to be
funny.

> "Peru was named after a chap called Biru, who may have been a regional king. However, one version of the story was that he was a perfectly ordinary local chap whom the Spanish conquistadors mistakenly thought was a king when they arrived in 1522. I prefer the latter version of the story."

Forsyth has two accounts of where "Peru" comes from, gives both with the date the
Spanish arrived, and then says plainly which one he prefers. The judgment is small
and owned, and he does not pretend the record is settled. You can hear a
particular writer choosing between sources without overclaiming.

## Anatoly Liberman, "A wary approach to hemlock" (The Oxford Etymologist)

Source: https://blog.oup.com/2025/02/a-wary-approach-to-hemlock/

> "Today's story is about a deadly plant or rather, about its moribund etymology. And yet, when you reach the end, the word's origin may appear somewhat more transparent, even though the plant will remain as deadly as ever."

Liberman says at the top what the piece will and will not deliver: the etymology
may come out clearer, the plant stays as deadly as ever. The sentence is exact
about its own limits and still turns dryly at the end. The etymologist is visible
in how carefully he keeps the word separate from the thing it names.

> "To quote Skeat, the first syllable of hemlock meant 'something bad,' while the second is a suffix, whose meaning was forgotten even before the settlement of Britain by Germanic tribes. Thus, it seems that we do know something about the etymology of hemlock. Yet it is still reasonable to stay away from the plant."

The conclusion is hedged and honest. He credits Skeat, states exactly how much is
known, and closes on the same dry practical note he opened with. Liberman lands a
piece whose subject never fully resolves without pretending that it did.

## Melinda Baldwin, "The history of 'scientist'" (guest post at The Renaissance Mathematicus)

Source: https://thonyc.wordpress.com/2014/07/10/the-history-of-scientist/

> "J.T. Carrington, editor of the popular science magazine Science-Gossip, achieved a remarkable feat in December of 1894: he found a subject on which the Duke of Argyll (a combative anti-Darwinian) and Thomas Huxley (a.k.a. 'Darwin's bulldog') held the same opinion."

Baldwin opens on one dated scene with named people rather than on a definition of
the word. The surprise is concrete, two famous opponents agreeing in December
1894, and the word itself is withheld for a beat. You can see a historian choosing
the vivid particular over the summary.

> "He himself felt the word was 'not satisfactory,' and he wrote to eight prominent writers and men of science to ask if they considered it legitimate. Seven responded. Huxley and Argyll joined a five-to-two majority when they denounced the term. 'I regard it with great dislike,' proclaimed Argyll. Huxley, exhibiting his usual gift for witty dismissals, said that the word in question 'must be about as pleasing a word as \"Electrocution.\"'"

The dispute is carried by hard facts and the antagonists' own words: eight letters,
seven replies, a five-to-two count, and Huxley's and Argyll's actual phrasing
quoted rather than characterized. Baldwin lets the objection sound like a real
objection. The precision with numbers and quotation is where the historian shows.

> "The English academic William Whewell first put the word 'scientist' into print in 1834 in a review of Mary Somerville's On the Connexion of the Physical Sciences. Whewell's review argued that science was becoming fragmented, that chemists and mathematicians and physicists had less and less to do with one another."

Baldwin fixes the coinage to a year and a specific review, and says what Whewell
was worried about when he made the word. The moment the word enters print gets a
date and a place. That exactness is the historian's habit on display.
