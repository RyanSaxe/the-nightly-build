# Editorial review: word-of-the-day/spandrel (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: Gould and Lewontin borrowed an architectural word in 1979 to name a
non-adaptive byproduct; the architects were right that San Marco's structures
are pendentives, not spandrels; Gould conceded the building in 1997 and kept
the word by widening it; the word survives because it travels, carrying a
distinction (byproduct versus later use) worth keeping. Four load-bearing
claims: the coinage and its meaning, the pendentive correction, Gould's
concession, and the reach into linguistics.

The coinage claim holds against the 1979 paper. September 1979, Proc. R. Soc.
B, both authors at Harvard's Museum of Comparative Zoology, the forced four
tapering triangles, and the quoted "Spandrels do not exist to house the
evangelists" all match the evidence read from the paper. The in-text sense
("necessary byproduct... not an adaptation built for a use of its own") tracks
Gould's own definition.

I pushed hardest on the claim I most wanted to keep, the pendentive
correction, because it is where a piece like this usually smooths the quarrel
into a footnote. It does not. The article states the plain architectural fact
(a spandrel is the flat space beside an arch; the curved dome-carriers are
pendentives, segments of a sphere), sources each half to its dictionary and
encyclopedia primary, and then reports Gould conceding exactly that point.
The distinction is the hinge, not a hedge. Confirmed against Britannica's
pendentive definition and Merriam-Webster's sense 1 by opening both links.

The Gould-1997 concession is reported honestly and is not inverted into a
denial: "he began by conceding the building. He called the structures the
pendentives of San Marco himself," with his lexical defense (extend the word
to "spaces left over") quoted verbatim in the note and attributed to PNAS
1997. This is the round's central correctness item and it holds.

The spandrel/exaptation distinction is held as a named separation, not folded:
the spandrel is the byproduct, the later co-option is exaptation, the two
"fix different moments," and Gould "kept them apart even while defending the
first." The commission's own conflating gloss ("came along for free and only
later found a use") is used as the misconception the section corrects, not
smoothed in as the definition. Correct.

One break: the correction section read "said as much **at the time**," which
asserts the terminological objection was contemporaneous with the 1979 paper.
Neither source supports that timing. Steadman dates nothing, and Gould's 1997
reply attributes the objection to Dennett and Houston, both later. Fixed by
cutting the temporal claim (see Edits); the sourced fact, that sticklers
objected, stays.

Robert Mark's structural objection is not raised, consistent with the
commission boundary and its single-source (Steadman) status, so no
misattribution risk there.

Display text checks clean descriptor by descriptor. Dek names the tapering
triangles (the neutral, correct description, not "spandrels") and claims only
what the piece proves. Every subhead is a concrete step in the piece's own
nouns. Names, titles, dates, page facts, and the four-spandrel count all
trace to the owning primaries. One note, not a break: the headline credits
"Gould" alone for a paper Gould co-authored with Lewontin. It is a defensible
compression, since Gould is the through-line who answered the objection and
the body credits both authors, so I leave it rather than route it.

Every `data-nb-kind` matches the evidence record: the two dictionaries, the
1979 paper, Gould 1997, and Pinker and Bloom are primaries; Steadman is the
lone secondary, correctly labeled and used only for the objectors' own words.
All six hrefs land on the source as printed. I opened Steadman, Britannica
pendentive, and Merriam-Webster directly and confirmed the cited text; the
Pinker and Bloom PDF resolves as the authors' posted paper; the Royal Society
and PNAS DOIs are the correct canonical article pages (gated to automated
fetch, but the source itself for a reader).

## Cut

Slop pass over every sentence including display text and the note prose. One
sentence failed: the byproduct section opened on "Keeping the word means
keeping one of its edges sharp," a signpost that announces the section's
purpose without doing any of its reasoning and would survive being moved to
any word-distinction piece. Cut. The section now opens on the concrete claim
that carried the content anyway.

Two negative-parallelism contrasts survive because both are earned and named,
not invented: "byproduct... not an adaptation built for a use of its own"
(the adaptationism the 1979 paper exists to correct) and language as "a
byproduct of a larger brain rather than a system selection shaped" (the real
Gould/Pinker-Bloom disagreement). Two is the ceiling the standard tolerates;
neither is a strawman, so both stay.

No prompt leakage survives scrutiny. The one phrase drawn from the briefing
stack, "came along for free and only later found a use," is deployed as the
everyday conflation the piece corrects, which is its legitimate job, not an
instruction restated as reporting. No self-grading, no method summary, no
"fulfilled the assignment" claim anywhere.

Checked distinctive phrasing against the voice-guide quotations from Forsyth,
Liberman, and Baldwin: no borrowed clauses. The one semicolon ("The San Marco
triangles are the byproduct; the mosaics are what later filled them") joins a
genuinely matched pair and reads as deliberate parallel, not reflex, so I left
it. Grammar and syntax are clean throughout, display text and note included.

Recent-pattern check: the opener avoids the flagged copular "X marks the
difference between..." frame and starts on a documented particular (September
1979, two named authors, a ceiling). The arc is treated as a borrowing and a
quarrel, not forced into the recent run's eponym shape. Subheads vary in
construction and none copies a prior formula. The `rs-word-card` and Sources
heading are the mandated furniture and are exempt. The single `nb-note` earns
its place by carrying Gould's exact concession, the pivot of the story, not
as decoration.

## Reader

Read straight through, the piece gives what the sources alone would not: it
maps the spandrel/exaptation distinction back onto the San Marco image the
coinage rests on, so the triangle is the byproduct and the mosaic is the later
use, and the reader sees why the casual "free trait that found a use" gloss
quietly names an exapted spandrel rather than a bare one. That mapping is the
handoff's stated act of original work, and it survives the read. The prose
sits closer to the voice-guide exemplars than to a median summary: it opens on
a dated concrete scene like Baldwin, keeps the architects' objection standing
in plain terms rather than paraphrasing it milder, reports Gould's concession
with Liberman's kind of honesty about what is and is not settled, and lets the
true details ("looking up at a ceiling," "conceding the building") carry the
dry lightness without a line engineered to be quotable. The headline reads as
a real claim the piece defends.

## Edits

- Cut "at the time" from the correction section: "Sticklers for the technical vocabulary said as much at the time, pointing out..." → "Sticklers for the technical vocabulary said as much, pointing out..." (unsupported timing; the objection was pressed later, by Dennett and Houston per Gould 1997, and Steadman dates nothing).
- Cut the signpost opener of the byproduct section: "Keeping the word means keeping one of its edges sharp. A spandrel is the byproduct itself." → "A spandrel is the byproduct itself." (fails the slop test; loses no fact, claim, or reasoning step).
- Ran `nb stamp`: words 774 → 761, reading 3 min, sources 6.

## Decision

Approve. Both correctness watch-items (spandrel/pendentive, spandrel/exaptation)
and the Gould-1997 concession are held honestly, sourcing and links check out,
and the two surgical cuts resolved the only prose faults; nothing
publication-blocking remains.
