# Voice guide — word-of-the-day/zugzwang

## Directive

Write for a reader who already thinks in game trees and payoff matrices and
does not need decision theory explained. Spend every sentence on what that
reader does not already have: the documented history and the precise
distinction the word marks. Nothing in the piece should read as filling
space until the next fact arrives.

**Opening.** Skip the dictionary throat-clear ("The word X, meaning Y, comes
from...") and skip the single-cause claim ("A single move decided..."). Start
inside a documented instance — a move, a position, a dated citation — and let
the reader arrive at the concept through it, the way a specific example
precedes its generalization. The abstraction gets named only once the
instance has earned it.

**Teaching the distinction.** Build the compulsion-to-move idea through one
worked position, not a run of restated definitions. State what the word is
first mistaken for, then show — in the position, not in a second definition —
why that reading fails: what changes if the side to move could pass. One
example carrying the whole argument beats three examples each restating it.
Once the reader has the distinction from the position, the transfer to the
non-chess case can be short, because the position already did the teaching.

**Term of art, technical reader.** Assume game theory; define only what is
local to chess (what "the side to move" and "zugzwang position" require
structurally) in the sentence that first needs it, then never re-explain it.
Keep two claims visibly separate: what a dictionary certifies as the current
meaning, and what a documented history shows about where the word came from.
Do not let a sentence about origin double as a sentence about meaning, and do
not narrate an unverified claim as though it were settled — mark degree of
certainty in the sentence itself ("first recorded," "credited to," "the
documented trail stops at") rather than smoothing it into flat narrative.

**Sentence-level, at 600 words.** Every sentence must be load-bearing; cut
any that a knowledgeable reader could supply themselves. Vary sentence length
for rhythm but keep each sentence doing one job. End on the distinction
itself, sharpened by the transfer example — not on a lesson about the word or
about the reader.

Recently used, do not reuse: the "a single [move/episode] decided everything"
opening cadence; a colon-subtitle headline; opening a word piece with the
word's dictionary definition before its documented history; a closing
sentence that generalizes the word into a life lesson.

## Exemplars

### Edward Winter, "Zugzwang"
Source: https://www.chesshistory.com/winter/extra/zugzwang.html
Craft:
- cadence: short paragraphs, each adding one documented fact or one gap in
  the record, never both compressed into a single claim.
- argument: builds by elimination — states what is claimed, then what the
  archive actually supports, and lets the gap stand rather than papering it.
- evidence: page numbers, publication names, and exact dates carried inline,
  not footnoted away from the claim.
- stance: skeptical of chess folklore by default; treats a repeated claim as
  needing its own citation, not as validated by repetition.
- notice: flags exactly where a citation trail goes cold ("has yet to be
  located") instead of letting the narrative glide past the gap.
- diction: plain and archival; no editorializing adjectives on the sources
  themselves.
- reader: a reader who wants to know what is actually established, not a
  good story.
- the move the axes miss: he separates the claim from the verification of
  the claim into two visible sentence-level moves, so uncertainty is never
  hidden inside confident prose.
Calibration: "The Lasker article (in a Dutch newspaper?) has yet to be
located."

### Anatoly Liberman, "A Retrospective on 'Origin Uncertain'"
Source: https://blog.oup.com/2024/03/a-retrospective-on-origin-uncertain/
Craft:
- cadence: alternates a short declarative with a longer reflective sentence,
  giving the prose room to think without losing the thread.
- argument: treats "origin unknown" as a starting position to be argued past,
  not a wall — the piece is the case for how far the evidence actually goes.
- evidence: prior scholarship named and weighed, not gestured at.
- stance: comfortable stating what remains unresolved; confidence is
  proportioned to what the record supports.
- notice: catches the difference between a word's documented route and the
  looser popular story people tell about it.
- diction: precise, faintly formal, never cute about the word itself.
- reader: assumes patience and rewards it with an actual answer, not a tour.
- the move the axes miss: he keeps etymology and current meaning as two
  separate lines of argument even while writing about both in the same piece.
Calibration: "The formula 'origin unknown' had to give way to a full-fledged
discussion of the state of the art."

### Ben Zimmer, "It's Spelling Bee Time Again!"
Source: https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wordroutes/its-spelling-bee-time-again/
Craft:
- cadence: opens inside a specific, dated instance and only generalizes after
  two or three instances have accumulated.
- argument: inductive — the pattern is the payoff at the end of the
  instances, not the premise stated up front.
- evidence: named competitors, named years, named words; nothing generic.
- stance: engaged but unshowy; the writing trusts the instances to carry the
  interest rather than announcing that they are interesting.
- notice: catches what a string of specific cases has in common before
  naming it as a rule.
- diction: conversational without slackness; a short column with no wasted
  clause.
- reader: assumes the reader can follow a pattern being built and does not
  need it flagged before it arrives.
- the move the axes miss: no sentence in the opening restates what kind of
  piece this is; it just starts reporting the instance.
Calibration: "That's when they choose the very hardest words to spell, which
are often relatively recent borrowings from foreign languages."

### Julia Evans, "Teaching by filling in knowledge gaps"
Source: https://jvns.ca/blog/2021/09/20/teaching-by-filling-in-knowledge-gaps/
Craft:
- cadence: short paragraphs organized one gap at a time, no throat-clearing
  before the gap is named.
- argument: identify the one thing readers already get wrong, then close
  exactly that gap and stop.
- evidence: a real worked case (a bug, a concrete failure) rather than an
  abstract description of the concept.
- stance: assumes competence; never re-derives what an experienced reader
  already has.
- notice: catches the exact point where a plausible-sounding explanation
  is actually wrong.
- diction: plain, direct, first person without being chatty.
- reader: writes as if for someone met once, so the point must land without
  relying on shared history with the reader.
- the move the axes miss: she scopes the piece to a single gap on purpose,
  resisting the pull to also explain the adjacent concepts the reader
  already holds.
Calibration: "I try to: only address one major gap at a time, and make it
super clear what the gap I'm addressing is."
