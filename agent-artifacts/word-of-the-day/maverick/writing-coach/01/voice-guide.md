# Voice guide: word-of-the-day/maverick (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a 550–800 word piece, so every sentence has to be doing the word's
work and nothing else. The definition card opens the page with no heading
above it, so the first prose the reader meets is already inside the origin
story, not warming up to it. Quinion's opening move in the "Serendipity"
passage below — the definition folded into one sentence, an aside carrying
the qualifier, then a colon handing straight to a dated citation — is a model
for that kind of start: it commits to a fact immediately rather than
gesturing at the subject before naming it.

The documented-origin-versus-modern-sense gap is the article's real content,
and Liberman's closing passage below is the register for holding that kind of
gap: state the uncertainty as the finding, not as an apology for failing to
resolve something. Where the record on Samuel Maverick's motives runs out,
the piece can say so as plainly as Liberman says a word's origin is disputed,
without softening it into "historians disagree" throat-clearing. The same
passage's candor — listing what several hypotheses claim and letting none of
them win — is the shape for the legend-versus-record distinction this piece
has to draw: lay out what the folklore says, what the documented record
actually supports, and let the gap between them do the work instead of
announcing that a gap exists.

The piece is also allowed to be funny, and Forsyth's passages show where the
fun should come from: a real biographical detail pushed one sentence further
than it needs to go, or two separate documented facts set next to each other
so their combination produces the surprise. That is a different move from a
joke about the reader's own habit of looking words up, which the template
rules out anyway since the subject is the word, never the experience of
looking it up. The unbranded-stray image is this word's equivalent of
Forsyth's two-facts-collide move: it is a literal, checkable image (an
unmarked calf that anyone could claim) sitting directly under a moral one
(the admired holdout), and the piece can let that literal image do comedic or
ironic work the way Forsyth lets "a church that sells cigarettes on the side"
do it, rather than explaining the irony afterward.

Liberman's digressions are also useful for what to avoid: his asides run
inside the sentence and report a real, specific constraint (a date that
didn't fall on a Wednesday, a name and two dates for a scholar's life) rather
than a general throat-clearing about how interesting the topic is. If this
piece digresses into the cattle-ranching mechanics of branding or into
Maverick's own biography, keep it anchored the same way: a fact with a name
or a number attached, not a sentence that could describe any historical
figure's ambiguous motives.

The house register is calm and precise, and the series wants a documented
episode with named people, not a bare etymology. All three exemplars below
hit both a scholarly and a readable register in the same sentence rather than
alternating between a serious paragraph and a fun one; that is the balance
to hold across 550–800 words, where there is no room to be dry for a while
and then perform being fun.

## Michael Quinion, "Serendipity"

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/ww-ser1.htm

> "This word — meaning the ability to make unexpected and fortunate
> discoveries — has been around for more than two centuries, but only really
> began to be used much in the twentieth century, to the extent that the
> adjective *serendipitous* is not recorded before about a century ago:"

The definition, the historical qualifier, and a specific claim about when a
related word entered use are all inside one sentence, and the sentence ends
on a colon that hands off to a dated 1923 newspaper citation. Nothing here is
vague: "more than two centuries" and "about a century ago" are both doing
real work rather than standing in for "long ago."

> "If you read the story you will find that the princes were well educated
> and intelligent men and their good fortune (which was a bit slap-dash: they
> got thrown in jail as suspected camel thieves at one point) was based on
> careful deduction and sagacity, not chance. As the saying has it, 'fortune
> favours the prepared mind', just as discoveries today that are said to be
> *serendipitous* are so often the result of experience and good
> observation."

The joke ("slap-dash," "camel thieves") sits inside the parenthetical of a
sentence that is otherwise making a scholarly point about the difference
between luck and sagacity. Quinion doesn't pause the argument to be funny and
then resume it; the joke and the argument share a clause.

> "The three princes came from a country the Persians called *Sarandib* but
> which we now know as Sri Lanka, or in earlier times Ceylon. The Persian is
> a corruption of the Sanskrit *Sinhaladvipa*, 'the island where lions
> dwell', hence the name *Sinhala* or *Sinhalese* for the most commonly
> spoken Sri Lankan language."

Three languages, three names, one chain: a Persian name for a place, the
Sanskrit term under it, and the modern language descended from it. Quinion
never announces that he's tracing a lineage; the "hence" is the only
connective tissue, and the specificity of each named term carries the rest.

## Anatoly Liberman, "All the way back: 'South'"

Source: https://blog.oup.com/2024/11/all-the-way-back-south/

> "I wanted to follow with something about Halloween (ghosts, witches,
> pumpkins, mopsticks, or Celtic mythology), but last week was too early, and
> this week is too late: October 31 did not fall on a Wednesday. Therefore, I
> am picking up where I left off on 10/31."

This is the column's opening, and it spends two sentences on a scheduling
problem before it reaches the word. It earns its place because the
constraint is a specific, checkable fact (a weekly column, a date, a
weekday) rather than a general remark about how there's always more to say.

> "The public, aware of every step of popular rappers and comedians, knows
> nothing about even the most distinguished linguists. Therefore, I hope to
> be forgiven for this digression."

One sentence of complaint, immediately followed by an apology for having made
it, and the apology doesn't retract the complaint. Liberman marks that he
knows he has wandered off the word and chooses to leave the wandering in
rather than cutting it.

> "What a sad story! Even though the last chapter (*west*) has not yet been
> told, we can see how helpless we are while trying to guess the process of
> word creation in the remotest past. Yet we need not be surprised. Hundreds
> of words that appeared in the memory of the people still living are equally
> obscure. [...] It is the details that escape us, and the god of etymology
> is in the details."

The exclamation would be empty on its own, but it opens directly into the
actual finding: generations of competing hypotheses, no winner among them.
Liberman states the unresolved question as the conclusion instead of
apologizing for not resolving it.

## Mark Forsyth, "Countries Named After People"

Source: https://blog.inkyfool.com/2025/10/countries-named-after-people.html

> "The Seychelles were named in his honour because he happened to be the
> minister of finance when the islands were acquired by France in 1756. He
> had been in the post for a whole two years, and then he had a stroke and
> was replaced. His life was not extraordinary or eminent and yet he is on
> the map. He is a sovereign state. He has a seat at the UN."

Three short declaratives close the paragraph, each restating the same fact
from a slightly larger frame: on the map, a sovereign state, a seat at the
UN. The joke depends on the biographical detail that came before it (two
years in the post, then a stroke) — take that fact away and the three
sentences say nothing.

> "Trinidad is Spanish for Trinity, which is the composed of God the Father,
> the Holy Spirit (both of whom are of No Fixed Abode), and Jesus of Nazareth
> (see above). Tobago is *probably* named after tobacco, which was smoked
> there. So the name is really Trinity and Tobacco, which makes it sounds
> rather like a church that sells cigarettes on the side."

Two separate, ordinary word derivations, reported straight, and then set next
to each other so their combination produces an image neither fact would
produce alone. The comedy comes from the juxtaposition of two documented
origins, not from a comment laid on top of them.

> "And there we have it. The least controversial blog post to mention Israel
> and Palestine this week. Only two people were natives of the country named
> after them. Jesus and his mum are tied as most influential with two
> countries each. And Biru, or whoever he was, lives on."

The close tallies the specific results of the piece — how many people, how
many countries, which figure remains uncertain — rather than drawing a
general lesson about naming. It ends on the same kind of concrete, named
figure the piece has been tracking throughout.
