# Commission: word-of-the-day/maverick

## The word and why it earns the slot

The word is **maverick**. It qualifies on both of the series tests. Its origin is
a documented episode with named people, not a bare etymology: the term descends
from Samuel Augustus Maverick, a nineteenth-century Texas landowner whose
unbranded cattle gave English first a ranching term for an unbranded stray and
then the figurative sense in use today. And it stays useful far outside the
cattle range: a maverick is an independent who refuses the mark of the herd,
a sense that now travels through politics, science, finance, and ordinary
speech.

The article's real work is the gap between the word's moral flavor and its
documented origin. The modern sense carries approval, the principled
nonconformist who won't be branded. The record underneath is more ambiguous:
whether Samuel Maverick left his cattle unbranded out of principle, neglect, or
land-speculator indifference is exactly the kind of thing the folklore settled
and the sources do not. An unbranded calf was also a stray anyone else could
claim, so the same word once meant "unowned and available to be taken." Trace
how the term climbed from that to "admirable independent," and say plainly where
the humane-motive story is legend rather than record. `spec/headlines.md` and
the series prompt both insist etymology is history, not proof of a present
meaning; this word is a clean case to hold to that.

## Boundaries and required contribution

- The subject is the word, never the experience of looking it up (template
  identity). Open on the definition card, then tell the origin, the semantic
  development, and the present use in whatever order teaches best.
- Give the reader the documented sense distinctions the word preserves: what
  "maverick" says that "rebel," "nonconformist," or "independent" does not. The
  unbranded-stray image is the live metaphor under the modern word; a reader
  should finish able to use it precisely.
- Cite an authoritative dictionary (OED preferred) for the definition and
  origin, and go to primary or scholarly records for Samuel Maverick and the
  word's nineteenth-century spread. Do not launder the folk story as fact.
- Word band is 550–800 words; this is the paper's smallest read. Depth comes
  from the one distinction and the one documented tension, not from surveying
  every later use.

## Recent word-of-the-day habits not to inherit

The last several words leaned political (boondoggle, gerrymander, luddite,
byzantine) and scientific (galvanize, spandrel, anosognosia); an eponym from the
American frontier already breaks that run. Break these recurring shapes:

- The dek keeps arriving as a wry one-line correction of the assumed origin:
  "Procrustes never adjusted the bed", "The jolt in 'galvanize' used to be
  literal", "Gould borrowed 'spandrel' from the architects, who said he had it
  wrong". A dek built to that mold now reads as the section's formula. Write the
  dek to this piece's own surprise.
- Several recent closers land on a variation of "why the word still earns space
  in a working vocabulary." The template wants the last section to land the
  present use; do it in this word's own terms, not that recurring phrasing.
