# Evidence record: word-of-the-day/maverick (01)

The evidence supports the commission's central claim: the documented historical
record on Samuel A. Maverick's unbranded cattle is a mundane story of absentee
ownership and understaffed labor, not the humane-motive legend that circulates
in popular retellings, and the two do not merely differ on motive — they differ
on how Maverick got the cattle in the first place. The scholarly record (Texas
State Historical Association, drawing on a dedicated 2017 biography of the word
and a 1989 biography of the Mavericks) says he bought a peninsula farm that
included the cattle; the popular retellings (Merriam-Webster's own "Did you
know," NPR) say a client paid a legal debt in cattle. That is a real,
citable fork, not a minor wording difference, and it is recorded in
Contradictions below. The dated attestations for the word's semantic path are
solid on both ends (literal 1872–1914, figurative 1924) though I could not
independently view the underlying page scans for three of the four period
quotations — a disclosed limitation, not a gap in the claim itself. The one
real gap is the OED: both `maverick_n` and `maverick_v` are gated behind a
subscription login I do not hold, so the dictionary-definition and
first-attested-figurative-use claims rely on Merriam-Webster (the brief's
named acceptable secondary) and on etymonline's and Wiktionary's compiled,
dated print citations rather than on OED's own text. This is recorded plainly
below and should be flagged to the writer and editor.

## Contradictions

**1. How Maverick acquired the cattle at all — not just why they went
unbranded.** The scholarly record (TSHA "Mavericks and Mavericking," citing
Fisher 2017 and Dary 1981) says Maverick bought a Matagorda Peninsula farm
from Charles Tilton in February 1847, and the farm came with roughly 400
longhorn cattle already carrying Tilton's brand — an ordinary land purchase.
The popular retellings — Merriam-Webster's own "Did you know" essay and NPR's
2008 segment — instead say "a client gave him 400 cattle to settle a debt,"
casting Maverick as a lawyer paid in livestock. These are not compatible
accounts of the same transaction; one is a land deal, the other a legal fee.
I found no primary document (letter, deed, or the Maverick Family Papers
themselves, which I did not access) cited by either version. The scholarly
account is footnoted to two dedicated biographies; the popular version is not
footnoted to anything at all, in either source that carries it.

**2. Why the cattle stayed unbranded: three explanations in circulation, only
one documented.** (a) Humane motive — Maverick "didn't want to be cruel to
animals" — appears in the NPR/Safire 2008 segment as something Maverick
himself is said to have claimed, with no source named. (b) Profit motive —
Maverick wanted to be able to claim any unbranded stray as his own — appears
in the same NPR segment (as a rumor among his neighbors) and is independently
named, and independently rejected, by both the South Carolina Encyclopedia
("Legend has it...") and Texas Standard ("Some say... Not true"). (c) Neglect
and indifference — an overburdened enslaved teenager left alone with the
herd, and an owner distracted by land speculation and legislative work — is
the account given by all three scholarly/journalistic sources that treat the
question in depth (TSHA, SC Encyclopedia, Texas Standard), and is the only
one of the three tied to specific, named, checkable facts (Jack's unanswered
request for help; the 1854 roundup; the shrinking herd; the 1856 sale). None
of the three sources that discuss motive in any depth (TSHA, SC Encyclopedia,
Texas Standard) repeats the humane-motive claim; it appears only in the
NPR/Safire segment (and in restatements that trace back to it, e.g.
Wikipedia's "Samuel Maverick" article and a Quick and Dirty Tips post that
mirror the same wording — treated here as one retelling, not independent
confirmation).

**3. Minor numeric variance.** TSHA gives "approximately 400" head of cattle;
Merriam-Webster's own essay gives "about 450." Neither source treats the
figure as load-bearing, and the discrepancy does not affect the branding
question, but a writer citing a specific number should attribute it to
whichever source supplies it rather than presenting either as settled.

## Analysis note for the writer: the sense the word preserves

This is researcher synthesis for the writer to test and cite as their own
earned analysis, not a claim sourced to any dictionary usage note — none of
the dictionaries or thesauruses read here (Merriam-Webster's dictionary or
thesaurus, etymonline, Wiktionary) draw the distinction explicitly.

The distinction runs through the literal sense itself, and the 1914 and 1924
quotations above show it precisely. In the literal sense, a maverick is not
an animal that broke away from a herd it belonged to — it is one that was
never branded in the first place, so it belongs to no one until "the man who
finds it and brands it" claims it (Zane Grey, 1914). That is a state of being
unmarked and unclaimed, not an act of leaving or defying a group one was
already inside. "Rebel" names an act of opposition from within a structure
the rebel was bound by. "Nonconformist" names a refusal, aware and pointed,
to match a norm the nonconformist recognizes. "Independent" is the most
neutral of the three and comes closest to "maverick," but carries no image of
having once been eligible for a claim that was never made. The 1924 Collier's
line shows the difference in its own grammar: the speaker doesn't say he left
a party, he says "I'm a maverick, don't belong to any party" — a standing
condition, not an act of departure. That is the specific thing the
unbranded-stray image adds and the near-synonyms don't carry: "maverick"
names an entity that was available to be claimed and wasn't, which is also
exactly why the same image can just as easily read as "unowned and up for
grabs" as it can "admirably self-possessed" — the tension the commission asks
the article to hold.
