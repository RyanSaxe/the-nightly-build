# Voice guide: word-of-the-day/boycott (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is one word and the documented story behind it, told in 550 to 800 words
for a well-read reader who arrives knowing the verb but not Captain Boycott or
the autumn of 1880 in County Mayo. Hold the register Quinion holds in
"Hooligan": lively but exact, a writer plainly enjoying the material while
getting the dates and names right. The Land League's organized shunning has
people and events enough to carry itself, so the documented facts can open the
word the way Quinion lets his stacked press quotations open his, instead of an
abstract lead about protest.

The origin invites embroidery, and the commission asks you to keep the record
and the retelling apart: the exact words attributed to Parnell at Ennis, who
first put "boycott" into print, the real cost of the relief expedition against
the value of the crop. Liberman's bigot essay shows the handling. He gives a
colorful legend its full airing, dialogue and all, then weighs which parts can
be true and names a folk etymology invented in retrospect where he finds one.
Where the popular version of Boycott's story has hardened past what the sources
support, the piece can say so in one plain sentence, as Liberman does, without
flattening the anecdote that surrounds it.

When a verdict is available, Quinion's flat "the evidence is that spirited and
enlivened are euphemisms" is the model: one short sentence the quotation just
before it has earned, with the dryness carrying the humor. The entertainment
this series wants sits in the facts themselves. A land agent who cannot bring in
his own harvest, a surname that crosses into other languages within the year:
Forsyth's "Louis the Sluggard" lands because the nickname is real, and boycott's
own record holds equivalents worth finding.

The piece has to trace how a proper name became a common verb, and then land the
distinction that is boycott's alone: a voluntary, collective, bottom-up
withdrawal of dealing, held apart from the embargo or sanction imposed from
above, the ban, and the blacklist. Forsyth walks through several sense-changes
in a single quick sentence while still naming each one; the development from
eponym to verb can move with that economy, since a word this short has room to
trace the shift but not to survey it. Ground the present sense where the
definition card and the closing section carry it, in real quoted current use,
and let the last section reach why the word still earns a place in a working
vocabulary.

## Michael Quinion, "Hooligan"

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/weirdwords/tw-hoo1.html

> "In the distinctive language of British journalism, the English football fans who caused so much trouble in Marseilles in June 1998 'went on the rampage', 'ran amuck', were guilty of 'thuggish behaviour', or 'caused mayhem'. They were described in news stories as louts, yobs, thugs and ruffians, but the word that was universally employed was hooligan."

He opens on the word doing its work in real reporting, stacking the actual
phrases and epithets the press reached for before he names the word itself. The
pile of quoted verbs and the plain list of near-synonyms set the word up out of
collected evidence rather than an abstract introduction, and you can see how
closely he read the actual coverage.

> "Several compounds appeared in newspapers within weeks (a sure sign of popularity and acceptance), including hooliganism, hooliganesque, hooliganic, and the verb to hooligan, of which only the first has survived."

He tracks the word's spread by naming the exact derivatives that appeared and
then saying which one lasted. The parenthetical judgment is stated plainly and
kept short, and the list is specific down to forms that died. It is reporting
that also teaches how a coinage takes hold.

> "Another writer, Earnest Weekley, said in his Romance of Words in 1912: 'The original Hooligans were a spirited Irish family of that name whose proceedings enlivened the drab monotony of life in Southwark about fourteen years ago'. The evidence is that spirited and enlivened are euphemisms."

He quotes a 1912 source at face value and then, in one flat sentence, tells you
how to read it. "The evidence is that spirited and enlivened are euphemisms" is
a verdict the quotation just given has earned, and the dryness is the joke. The
person shows in the restraint: he lets the primary source overclaim and corrects
it plainly.

## Anatoly Liberman, "Nobody wants to be called a bigot"

Source: https://blog.oup.com/2011/10/bigot-2/

> "in October 1997, the Catholic Digest published on pp. 117-120 an article titled 'Asphalt, Bigot, and Comma.' It informed the subscribers that asphalt goes back to Leopold von Asphalt (1802-1880), that bigot derives from Nathaniel Bigot (1575-1660), an English Puritan preacher ... Many other gentlemen, including Mr. Botch, Mr. Doldrum, and Mr. Fiasco, enlivened the pages of that publication. I wrote a politely indignant letter to the editor but received no answer. Beware of amateur etymologists."

He confronts a printed fake-eponym story by citing it exactly, page numbers and
invented birth-and-death dates included, then lists its parade of imaginary
namesakes and closes with a plain warning. The specificity is what makes the
mockery land, and the detail about the unanswered letter is real rather than
decorative. His skepticism toward tidy origin stories is visible in every date
he bothers to quote.

> "According to an oft-repeated story, preserved in an old chronicle, Rollo of Normandy, on receiving the dukedom from Charles the Simple, refused to kiss the king's foot and said (in English!): 'Nese bi god,' that is, 'No, by god.' Allegedly, this is how bigod, later bigot, became an opprobrious moniker of the duke and then of the Normans. That Rollo should have offended the king and said something in English to him is beyond belief, but it is not improbable that some such taunting name of the Normans (who had the reputation for bad manners and swearing) existed."

He gives the colorful legend in full, with its dialogue and its "(in English!)"
aside, before weighing whether it can be true. He separates what is beyond
belief from what is merely unproven, and later names the sign of a folk
etymology invented after the fact. The reader sees exactly which parts he credits
and which he does not.

## Mark Forsyth, "Fainéant"

Source: https://blog.inkyfool.com/2010/10/faineant.html

> "The original fainéants were the rois fainéants, the French kings of the late Merovingian dynasty who did nothing at all and earned themselves marvellous monikers like Louis the Sluggard."

He lands a real historical fact, the Merovingian rois fainéants, with a light
touch, and the humor comes from the documented nickname rather than from
anything he added. "Marvellous monikers like Louis the Sluggard" is playful and
still exact. The writer's enjoyment of the material is on the page and the fact
stays central.

> "Yet the origin is hiding in a more ancient bedroom. A faignant, in Old French, was sluggard who feigned some excuse (French faindre) and feign relates to feint whose meaning meandered from lie to lazy to weak to faint ..."

He traces a chain of sense-change inside a single sentence, marking each step
from feign to feint to faint so the reader follows the drift as it happens. The
line moves quickly and every link is still named. It shows how to walk through
several shifts in meaning at speed without losing the individual ones.
