# Voice guide: word-of-the-day/ostracize (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a Word of the Day on *ostracize*, the paper's smallest read. The word
comes from ostracism, the Athenian procedure of exile by written vote, and its
origin is a physical object, the inscribed potsherd the Athenians called an
*ostrakon*. Hold the register precise and light. The documented particulars do
the entertaining here, so the prose can stay plain and let the facts be strange
on their own. Carlos Noreña's opening about Cleopatra standing nearer to us than
to the pyramids, "by a half-millennium, no less," shows the move: one exact,
dated fact resets something the reader thought they understood, and no adjective
is asked to make it vivid. Where the ostracism particulars are securely
attested, the once-a-year vote in the assembly, the fixed term of exile, a name
scratched on a shard, the absence of any charge, they can carry the read the
same way.

Much of what makes ostracism a good story reaches us as anecdote, and the reader
should be able to tell which is which at every step. Anatoly Liberman's word
histories keep that line visible without draining the pleasure. He can write "we
do not know what exactly pólis meant in fourth-century Greek" inside a paragraph
full of evidence, and he closes the hamburger piece on "A hamburger is a
relatively simple thing. All the rest is questionable and complicated." Plutarch's
story of Aristides asked by an illiterate voter to inscribe Aristides' own name,
and the number said to make a vote valid, are places where this piece can state
plainly what is documented and what comes down as a good story, in Liberman's
manner, instead of reporting the legend as settled fact or softening the whole
account into vagueness to stay safe.

The article also has to move from a specific civic mechanism to the general
modern sense of shutting someone out of a group. Merriam-Webster's filibuster
history traces exactly that kind of drift, from a word for pirates through
mercenaries to a Senate tactic, keeping each step concrete and dated and naming
the senators in the room. Trace *ostracize* the same way where the record allows,
letting the sequence itself carry the change in meaning. Define *ostrakon* and
*ostracism* in the sentence each first appears, the way Liberman defines
"substrate" at the moment he uses it, since the declared reader may not hold
them.

The close lands on why the word still earns a place in a working vocabulary.
Merriam-Webster ends the filibuster piece on the bare label "The nuclear
option." and stops. Liberman ends on the hamburger and the questionable rest.
Ground the present sense of *ostracize* in real, cited modern use, and let that
use be the reason the word survives, without a closing lesson about exclusion or
human nature. The strongest ending here points at something the reader can
check.

## Merriam-Webster, "The Curious History of 'Filibuster'"

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/how-senators-are-like-pirates

> "Although little in use today, another word for pirate is freebooter. It comes into English from the Dutch word vrijbuiter, meaning "pirate" made from vrij, meaning "free" and buiter, meaning "booty." The French have a similar word, fribustier or flibustier. The Spanish also have a word which they probably got from the French, taking the form filibustero and referring especially to West Indian pirates."

The writer walks the word across four languages without hurrying, giving each
form its gloss (*vrijbuiter*, *fribustier*, *filibustero*) and the one detail
that fixes it, West Indian pirates. The reader watches a single word change
costume, and the sequence is left to be interesting on its own rather than
labeled interesting.

> "Meanwhile back in the Senate, senators were making long and windy speeches and discovering the power of long speeches to slow up or prevent legislation – and in 1853 the term filibuster was used to describe these stunts. A senator from North Carolina rose to object to filibustering in the military sense in regard to activity in Cuba. This drew a rebuke from the senator from Mississippi who accused the North Carolina senator of "filibustering ... against the United States.""

A dated moment (1853) and two named senators turn an etymology into a scene, and
the quoted rebuke lets a real voice from the record do the work. The house
word-history desk keeps itself out of the way and trusts the archive to be
vivid.

> "And so we go from pirate ships to U-boats. And the military escalation regarding filibusters continues to this day. What is the term for a procedural change to eliminate the filibuster? The nuclear option."

The close moves from pirate ships to U-boats and ends on the plain institutional
label with no lesson attached. The deadpan is the writer's own, and it lands the
word's living use by pointing at it, not by commenting on what it means.

## Anatoly Liberman, "An etymological hamburger" (The Oxford Etymologist)

Source: https://blog.oup.com/2026/04/an-etymological-hamburger/

> "The trouble with the origin of burg ~ borg is that we have a great lot of information and cannot always decide which bit of it to use. The nouns attested in the oldest Germanic languages and cited above meant "height, wall; castle; city." The Gothic Bible was translated from Greek. The Greek word the translator saw was pólis "town," but we do not know what exactly pólis meant in fourth-century Greek."

Liberman names the real difficulty, that here there is too much evidence rather
than too little, and defines his terms as he goes. The admission "we do not know
what exactly *pólis* meant in fourth-century Greek" sits calmly inside a
paragraph of confident detail, so saying what he cannot settle reads as command.
The scholar is visible in the exactness and in the willingness to mark the gap.

> "It was therefore suggested long ago that we are dealing with a so-called migratory word, probably pre-Indo-European. In such situations, linguists often refer to the substrate, that is, some unknown ancient language of an extinct tribe. But a migratory word is not even a borrowing from a substrate: it is a term that travels all over the enormous continent (in this case of Eurasia). Of course, it had some source, but we can no longer discover it."

He introduces a technical idea, the migratory word, and defines the neighboring
term, "substrate," in the sentence that needs it. Then he states the limit
plainly: the word had some source, and the source can no longer be found. The
reader is taught something and told exactly where the knowledge stops, in the
same breath.

> "As far as I can understand, some link between the words in Germanic and Slavic has been recognized, but the borrowing by Slavic from Germanic does not look like a viable option. Nor do Slavic etymological dictionaries refer to substrates or migratory words. A hamburger is a relatively simple thing. All the rest is questionable and complicated."

After a paragraph of careful qualification, he closes on two short sentences that
set the everyday object against the tangle behind it. The dryness is earned by
the work above it, and the piece ends without pretending to have solved what it
has just shown to be unsolved.

## Carlos Noreña, "Beyond Greece and Rome" (Aeon)

Source: https://aeon.co/essays/uncovering-a-global-ancient-history-beyond-greece-and-rome

> "When Cleopatra became queen of Egypt, in 51 BCE, the oldest pyramids that graced her realm, constructed in the 27th century BCE, had already been standing for more than 2,500 years. Cleopatra is one of the most iconic figures from antiquity, yet she is nearer in time to the present than she was to those first pyramids – by a half-millennium, no less."

One dated fact does the whole job. Noreña takes something the reader assumed they
grasped, the depth of ancient time, and makes it felt with two construction
dates and a subtraction, adding only the quiet aside "no less." The historian's
judgment shows in picking the single comparison that lands the scale.

> "We now know, for example, that trans-Saharan trade can be traced back to the first two centuries CE, at the height of the Roman Empire, when Roman products such as wine, olive oil and silver were exchanged – largely through merchants operating in the southwestern Libyan desert – for gemstones, ivory and slaves. Hoards of Roman coins have been found well beyond the frontiers of the empire, from Scandinavia to India. A papyrus from Egypt in the middle of the 2nd century CE records the details of two contracts for the import of various goods from India, including pepper, pearls, silk and gemstones, shipped to Alexandria from the port of Muziris in Kerala, southwest India."

The paragraph earns a broad claim by naming specifics: the goods moving each way,
the coin hoards from Scandinavia to India, and a single papyrus recording two
contracts for pepper, pearls, and silk shipped from Muziris to Alexandria. The
named cargo and the named port carry the point, and the writer never reaches for
"extensive" when he can list what was in the hold.
