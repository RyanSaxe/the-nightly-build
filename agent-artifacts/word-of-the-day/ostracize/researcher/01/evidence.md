# Evidence: word-of-the-day/ostracize (01)

The evidence securely supports the commission's spine: "ostracize" descends from
the Greek *ostrakon* (potsherd), the physical ballot of a formal Athenian
procedure, not from any person's name. The institution is well documented from
primary sources: an annual assembly question on whether to hold an ostracism
(Aristotle, *Athenaion Politeia* 43.5), banishment for ten years with no charge,
no trial, and no loss of property (Plutarch, *Aristides* 7; corroborated by
Lendering), the vote cast by scratching a name on an *ostrakon* (Plutarch,
*Aristides* 7.4-7.5), and the law's first use around 488 BC against Hipparchus
son of Charmus (*Ath. Pol.* 22.3-4). Thousands of the actual sherds survive from
the Kerameikos and Agora. Two things are thin and must be handled honestly.
First, the famous Aristides "the Just" anecdote comes only from Plutarch, writing
around AD 100, roughly six centuries after the event, in an explicitly moralizing
biography; it is a literary anecdote, not contemporary record. Second, the "6,000"
figure is genuinely contested between two ancient sources (Plutarch and
Philochorus) as to whether it is a total quorum or a threshold against one man.
The OED could not be opened (subscription-gated; see Discarded), so the required
"OED" leg of the etymology is carried by Merriam-Webster (an authoritative
dictionary) and Etymonline, whose dates agree; the specific OED first-citation
authors surfaced in search are not independently verified here. Nothing found
undermines the commissioned angle; it sharpens it by separating record from
legend. A modern usage grounding the present sense is confirmed.

## Contradictions

- **The 6,000 figure is contested at the level of what it counts.** Plutarch
  (*Aristides* 7.5) has it as a total-turnout quorum: fewer than six thousand
  voters and the whole ostracism was void. Philochorus (a 3rd-century BC
  Atthidographer, cited via Lendering) has it as a threshold against the
  individual: the exiled man had to receive 6,000. Lendering reports the split
  without adjudicating; the writer should present it as unsettled, and may note
  the common modern preference for Plutarch's quorum reading (it matches the
  6,000 required for later citizenship grants) — but that preference is not
  something I confirmed from a primary source here, so flag it as scholarly
  opinion, not fact.
- **The ten-year term is attested, but not by every source that describes the
  procedure.** Plutarch states it plainly (*Aristides* 7.2, 7.5) and Etymonline
  and Lendering agree. Aristotle's *Ath. Pol.* 22, which owns the origin and the
  first case, does not state the term. So the ten years is secure, but its
  primary owner is Plutarch, not Aristotle.
- **The Aristides "the Just" anecdote is the single most embellished element and
  must be labeled.** Its only source is Plutarch (*Aristides* 7.6), writing c. AD
  100 in a moralizing Life, roughly six centuries after the ostracism of the
  480s BC. No contemporary (5th-century) source records it. It is a good story
  and safe to tell AS a Plutarchan anecdote; it is not safe to present as
  documented fact. Plutarch himself frames ostracism moralistically ("a merciful
  exorcism of the spirit of jealous hate"), which is interpretation, not record.
- **Themistocles' ostracism, by contrast, is securely attested** — named by
  Plutarch (*Themistocles* 22.3), and independently confirmed by the mass of
  surviving ostraka bearing his name (Smithsonian, citing the Kerameikos and
  Acropolis-well finds). The commission may lean on Themistocles as the *documented*
  case and use Aristides as the *legendary* one; the two are not on the same
  evidentiary footing.
- **First-attestation dates differ trivially between references and could not be
  checked against the OED.** Merriam-Webster gives first known use 1588
  (ostracism) and 1649 (ostracize); Etymonline gives the 1580s and the 1640s.
  These agree. Search results attribute the OED's earliest quotations to Robert
  Greene (1588, for ostracism) and to a 1649 elegy on Lord Hastings (for
  ostracize), but the OED entries themselves returned no content (gated), so
  those specific author attributions are unverified and should not be stated as
  fact. Cite the dates to Merriam-Webster; do not cite the OED as if opened.
