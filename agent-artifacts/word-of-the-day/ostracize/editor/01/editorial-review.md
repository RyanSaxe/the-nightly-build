# Editorial review: word-of-the-day/ostracize (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: ostracize descends from *ostrakon*, the inscribed potsherd that served
as the ballot in a formal Athenian procedure of exile by popular vote, and the
verb outlived the institution into the general sense of shutting someone out of
a group. The claims it stands on:

1. The word comes from the ballot-object, not a person. Held. Merriam-Webster
   (s1) glosses *ostrakizein* "to banish an individual chosen after a vote taken
   by writing names on potsherds," from *ostrakon* "earthen vessel, potsherd."
   Opened and verified.
2. The procedure: an annual assembly vote on whether to hold an ostracism (s2,
   *Ath. Pol.* 43.5 — opened, confirms the sixth-presidency vote); ten-year
   exile with property and its income kept, recallable, no charge (s4 Plutarch
   *Aristides* 7 for the ten years and property income; s3 Livius for retained
   possessions and recall — both opened and confirmed); Cleisthenes' law, first
   used c. 488 BCE two years after Marathon against Hipparchus son of Charmus
   (s5 *Ath. Pol.* 22 — opened, confirms all three). Held.
3. The ballots physically survive: ~8,500 from one dump tied to a vote c. 471
   BCE, ~9,000 catalogued from the Kerameikos; a pre-made, unused cache against
   Themistocles from a well on the Acropolis's north slope. Held (s6 Smithsonian
   — opened, all figures and the Themistocles cache confirmed verbatim).
4. Aristides "the Just" is a single late witness; the quorum is contested. Held
   and handled honestly (see round focus below).
5. Present sense grounded in cited modern use (s9, 2020 Frontiers study —
   opened, opening line confirmed verbatim). Held.

Pushed hardest on the claim I most wanted to keep, that the excavated sherds
make the account trustworthy: the physical-survival figures are the load-bearing

what the evidence supports.

## Round focus

- Aristides anecdote quarantined correctly: prose states "The most repeated
  story rests on one late witness," the quotation sits in an nb-note labeled
  "Plutarch's anecdote" / "the voter's reply, in Plutarch, *Life of Aristides*
  7," and the follow-up says plainly no fifth-century source records it and
  Plutarch set it down c. 100 CE, six centuries later, in a Life "written to
  improve its readers." Reads as anecdote, not fact.
- 6,000 quorum given as contested, not asserted: Plutarch's total-cast reading
  set against Philochorus's per-man threshold, under "the rule that made a vote

- Definition and etymology cited to Merriam-Webster (authoritative dictionary);
  OED is not cited anywhere, and no OED-attributed first-citation author appears.
- Opens with the `rs-word-card` alone, no heading above it; traces
  potsherd-ballot to the modern verb; grounds the present sense in the cited 2020
  study; lands why the word earns its place with no moral.
- Band: 798 words before my cut, ~786 after — inside 550-800.

## Cut

Sentence-by-sentence and edge passes against `spec/slop.md`: the prose is dense
with load-bearing nouns and carries almost no filler. One required change.

The opener copied the recent `defenestration` article's construction. Published
`defenestration`: "Most words for getting rid of a person are polite.
Defenestration is not. It names one thing...". Draft `ostracize`: "You expect a
word like this to be somebody's surname. Ostracize is not. It comes from a
thing." Same three-beat mold — general frame, "[Word] is not.", then the reveal
— which the recent-pattern notes flag as formula. It also told the reader their
own mind ("You expect"), a reader-address the word template carries no written
allowance for, and "It comes from a thing" reached for the generic where the
specific was one sentence away. Replaced the three sentences with a single plain
declarative that leads on the fact and lets it stand: "Ostracize traces back to
a potsherd." Distinct from every recent word-of-the-day opener, and it trims the
piece off the top of the band.

Edge sentences elsewhere hold: "Little of this depends on trust, because the
ballots survive" carries the section's reasoning step; "The excavated sherds are
firm ground. The anecdote is a good story, attested late." lands the
record-vs-legend distinction the piece is built on rather than signposting it.
The closer ("...the act the Athenians surrounded with a yearly vote, and the
rest of us manage without one.") points at the checkable present use with no
moral, and its construction differs from the recent closers I compared. No slop
sentences remained after the opener fix. No prompt leakage, no borrowed phrasing
from the voice-guide exemplars, punctuation clean.

## Reader

Read straight through, the piece gives what no single source does: the
potsherd-to-verb arc plus a working rule for trusting it — thousands of
excavated, inscribed ballots as firm ground, set against the one late Plutarchan
anecdote and the genuinely unsettled quorum, then carried to a cited modern use.
The draft-handoff's original-work sentence claims exactly that standing of record
against legend, and it survives the read. The prose sits closer to the
voice-guide exemplars (Liberman's marked gaps, Merriam-Webster's plain
sequencing) than to a median summary: it states what is documented and what
merely comes down as a good story, and lets the strange facts carry themselves.
