# Editorial review: word-of-the-day/palimpsest (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: the erasure of a palimpsest never fully takes, and that surviving-under
-the-overwrite is what the word carries into its modern extended sense. The
piece stands on four load-bearing claims.

1. **The literal origin scene is real and precise.** A 13th-century monk
   scraped a 10th-century Constantinople copy of Archimedes, cut and rotated
   the leaves, and bound them into a prayer book finished in 1229. Verified
   against the Archimedes Palimpsest project history (s3) and the Walters
   "Lost and Found" page (s2): the cut-fold-rotate mechanics and the 1229
   colophon both resolve. Held.

2. **The recovered texts are correctly stated.** "Two of the seven treatises,
   The Method and the Stomachion, survive nowhere else; On Floating Bodies
   survives here alone in its original Greek." I pushed hardest here because it
   is the most quotable factual claim. It holds exactly: s3 says the Method and
   Stomachion "exist nowhere else in the world"; the Walters XRF page (s4) names
   On Floating Bodies "the only surviving copy in the original Greek." The dek's
   unifying compression — "three Archimedes treatises, preserved nowhere else in
   Greek" — is true for all three under the "in Greek" qualifier (two survive
   nowhere at all, one survives elsewhere only in translation). A careful, not a
   loose, construction.

3. **The extended sense is attested, not inferred from the Greek.** This is the
   piece's real argument and it clears the series' "etymology is not proof of a
   present meaning" bar. The modern sense is grounded in three independent
   attestations, each opened: Merriam-Webster sense 2 ("something having usually
   diverse layers... apparent beneath the surface," s1), De Quincey's 1845 "a
   natural and mighty palimpsest" (s5, quoted verbatim against the Standard
   Ebooks primary), and Canadian Geographic's landscape definition (s6). The
   Greek "scraped again" sits in the origin section as history, never as the
   engine of the present meaning. Held.

4. **The dates and provenance verify.** Undertext recognized 1906 (s4, "In
   1906, the undertext was recognized by J. L. Heiberg"); Christie's New York
   sale October 29, 1998, $2,000,000 (s3, "At 2pm on October 29th, 1998");
   ~80% recovered by multispectral imaging (s4). The writer correctly took the
   29th over the lone "Oct. 28" outlier on s2, and correctly kept the 1906
   recognition (s4) distinct from Heiberg's 1910-1915 publication (s3) rather
   than conflating them.

Three writer-flagged notes, all verified as correct and NOT errors:
- **"Johan Ludvig Heiberg"** is the correct Danish spelling (confirmed against
  Wikipedia's biographical entry, b. 1854). The evidence record's "John Ludwig"
  is the anglicized outlier carried by s3's page text. Kept "Johan Ludvig" as
  instructed; not a fabrication.
- **"an iron-based ink"** stays inside the record: s2/s4 attest iron in the ink
  and X-ray detection of it, not the specific compound. The writer correctly
  avoided "iron gall."
- **Pronunciation /ˈpa-ləmp-ˌsest/** is Merriam-Webster's own from the cited s1
  entry (MW prints "ˈpa-ləm(p)-ˌsest"; the article renders the optional p as
  pronounced). From the cited authority.

Computing check: the resonance is a single analogy-only clause ("A programmer
meets the same shape in a deleted file whose bytes linger on a disk...") with no
citation, and the closer's "a disk" reprises it as image, not attested usage.
Correct — it never wears a source it lacks.

data-nb-kind audit: five primary (s1 MW dictionary; s2, s4 Walters reporting its
own conservation; s3 the project's own history; s5 the De Quincey text that owns
the usage), one secondary (s6 Canadian Geographic, reporting on the word). Matches
the 5/1 claim; no mislabel hiding a missing independent source. Every href was
opened and resolves to the source's own page. No OED cite (gated); none required.

One break found: "the X-ray work read much of the rest" attached a magnitude the
evidence does not support (s4 attests XRF read specific pages multispectral could
not, e.g. under the forged icon, not "much of the rest"), and it duplicated the
X-ray capability already established one sentence earlier. Fixed by cutting (see
Edits).

## Cut

The article runs tight; the earns-its-place test left almost everything standing,
because nearly every sentence is a dated fact, an attestation, or the argument
that joins them. No prompt leakage against the writer brief (the brief's
"geology, urban studies, memory, computing" menu is not restated, and nothing
claims the assignment was fulfilled). No scaffolding headings — the four subheads
are the argument's steps in the piece's own nouns, and skimming them reconstructs
it. No colon headline; not eponym-biography, abstraction-first, or
"coined-term-escapes-its-field," so the variety the recent-pattern note asked for
holds.

Worst tell: the "read much of the rest" clause, cut. It was the one place the
prose reached past its evidence, and it dragged a semicolon the punctuation
standard disfavors. Cutting it retired all three problems (unsupported magnitude,
redundancy, semicolon) at once and kept the 80% figure with its s4 citation. The
one remaining semicolon (the Method/Stomachion vs. On Floating Bodies pair) is an
earned contrast of two survival facts and stays. No em-dashes in the body. The
closer lands on earned facts (the recovered layer; coastline, memory, disk from
the attested/analogized uses) rather than a generic "layers of meaning" moral, so
it clears the closing-image license instead of tripping the punchline ban.

## Reader

Read straight through, the piece gives what the sources cannot alone: the sources
hold these facts apart — a manuscript recovery here, a dictionary sense there, an
1845 essay, a geography column — and the article makes them one claim, that the
erased-but-legible layer is the through-line from the literal parchment to every
modern use. That matches the draft-handoff's named original work exactly, and the
join is visible in "The sense outgrows the parchment" and the close. The prose
sits with the voice-guide exemplars (Forsyth/Kelly), not a median summary: a
picturable scene opens it, the etymology is spent in dated beats, and the back
half is carried by real quoted usage. The headline reads true as the largest
claim.

- Ran `nb stamp`: words 684 -> 676, still in the 550-800 band; sources 6.
