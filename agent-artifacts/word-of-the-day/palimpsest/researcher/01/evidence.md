# Evidence: word-of-the-day/palimpsest (01)

The record fully supports the article's three loads. The **definition** (both the
literal manuscript sense and the extended sense) is anchored to Merriam-Webster,
which also owns the **etymology** (Greek *palimpsēstos*, "scraped again," from
*palin* + *psēn* "to rub, scrape"). The **origin** — a 10th-century Constantinople
copy of Archimedes scraped and overwritten as a 13th-century Greek prayer book,
its undertext recognized in 1906, auctioned at Christie's in 1998, and recovered
at the Walters Art Museum with multispectral and X-ray-fluorescence imaging that
read out *The Method*, the *Stomachion*, and *On Floating Bodies* — is sourced
firsthand to the Walters Art Museum's own pages and to the Archimedes Palimpsest
project's own history. The **present extended sense** ("a surface carrying legible
traces of what it overwrote") is attested both historically, in De Quincey's 1845
"The Palimpsest of the Human Brain" (the literary text that pioneered the figurative
use), and in present-day geographic usage (Canadian Geographic, 2015), and is
codified as Merriam-Webster's sense 2.

Where the record is **thin**: the OED could not be opened (403), so the second
authoritative dictionary the commission hoped for is absent; Merriam-Webster
carries the dictionary load alone. The commission's suggested **computing
resonance** (overwritten-but-recoverable data called a "palimpsest") was **not**
attested to a primary computing source and should be handled as analogy, not as a
sourced term of art. One dating contradiction on the 1998 auction is recorded
below.

## Contradictions

- **Auction date, 1998.** The Archimedes Palimpsest project history and the
  Walters XRF page place the Christie's sale on **October 29, 1998** (the project
  page specifies 2 p.m.). The Walters "Lost and Found" page says the palimpsest was
  purchased "**On Oct. 28, 1998**." The project's own detailed history is the more
  authoritative account and the article should use **October 29, 1998**; the "Oct.
  28" reading is a lone outlier on a summary page. Flagged so the writer does not
  reproduce the discrepancy.
- **Heiberg dates.** The Walters XRF key-date list dates the undertext's
  recognition to **1906** (the commonly reported identification year, and the date
  the commission carries). The Archimedes project history page, as retrieved,
  emphasizes Heiberg's examination and his **1910-1915** publication of the text
  and does not itself print "1906" in the passage retrieved. These are compatible
  (recognition in 1906; publication 1910-1915), but the writer should attribute the
  **1906** identification to the Walters and treat 1910-1915 as the publication
  window, not conflate them.
- **On Floating Bodies.** The Walters XRF page names *On Floating Bodies* as the
  palimpsest's only surviving copy in the original Greek; the "Lost and Found" page
  names only *The Method* and the *Stomachion*. Not a conflict — different pages
  emphasize different treatises. The precise, defensible claim: the manuscript
  carries seven Archimedes treatises; two (*The Method*, the *Stomachion*) survive
  nowhere else, and *On Floating Bodies* survives here uniquely in the original
  Greek.
- No source contradicts the commission's core angle. The extended sense is
  genuinely attested and not merely asserted from etymology.
