# Voice guide: word-of-the-day/procrustean (01)

## How this piece should sound

The whole read turns on one move: a roadside bandit's name became an English
adjective. Tell that origin as documented events with real people and dates, the
way Michael Quinion states a record. But where Quinion can date *posh* to a 1918
issue of *Punch* and quote the sentence it first appeared in, this piece owes the
same for the modern sense of *procrustean*. The myth is the history, and history
is not proof that the word still means anything today. Ground the present sense
the way Quinion grounds his: in a real, cited sentence where *procrustean* is
doing work, not only inside the story of the bed.

Readers reach for *procrustean* as a dressed-up word for *strict* or
*standardized*, and correcting that is the piece's real work. Its edge is
narrower: the standard is arbitrary, set by one party, and the case is forced to
fit it rather than the standard adjusted. Kory Stamper's method suits this. She
separates *jargon* (sense 1) from *jargon* (sense 2) in a single sentence and
holds the two apart, and she corrects the plausible wrong reading of
*inflammable* with the documented Latin instead of asserting it. Hold
*procrustean* against *rigid* and *standardized* that same way, so a reader
finishes knowing when *procrustean* is the right word and when a plainer one is
honest.

Keep it lively without leaving the paper's calm register. Quinion is the register
here, dry and unhurried; Stamper and Forsyth both run warmer than this piece
should. Take from Mark Forsyth only the one move where the wit comes out of the
word's own literal sense — *spaghetti* as little strings, *paste* surfacing again
in *pastiche*. Anything that entertains here should come out of *procrustean*'s
own meaning and its documented story, never from an aside laid on top.

## Michael Quinion, "Posh"

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/qa/qa-pos1.htm

> "The trouble is there's absolutely no evidence for it and P&O flatly denies any such term existed. It's just a legend, though a very persistent one. One argument against it is that posh is known from 1918, while the story of its origin first appears only decades later."

Quinion gives the verdict on the famous story flatly — no evidence, the company
denies it — and then the single dated fact that settles it: the word is attested
in 1918, the origin story only decades later. The person shows in the dry
"though a very persistent one"; he is unmoved by a good story and says so without
raising his voice.

> "Whatever its source, it looks from the evidence that posh in the modern sense was at first a military slang term of the First World War. Its first appearance is in the magazine Punch in September 1918, in which an RAF officer is saying to his mother, \"Oh, yes, Mater, we had a posh time of it down there\"; the verbal phrase to posh up, to make oneself smart, is of the same period."

He lands the documented sense by dating its first real appearance and quoting the
actual sentence it turned up in, rather than paraphrasing it. "It looks from the
evidence" is doing careful work: it says how far the record reaches and no
further, which is why the reader trusts the claim under it.

## Kory Stamper, "A Lexicographer's Guide to Real Words"

Source: https://korystamper.com/2018/05/25/a-lexicographers-guide-to-real-words/

> "Jargon is, properly, the technical language of a particular group or activity. It can also refer to obscure and often pretentious language marked by circumlocutions–a definition that is pretty damned jargony. But not all jargon (sense 1) is jargon (sense 2)."

Stamper splits one word into two numbered senses and holds the line between them
in a single sentence, so the reader can see exactly where one meaning stops and
the other starts. The person shows in the aside that the sense-2 definition is
itself "pretty damned jargony"; she is amused by the word turning on itself and
lets it show.

> "You may think that \"inflammable\" to mean \"flammable\" is illogical, because \"in-\" means \"not,\" but you would be wrong. \"Inflammable\" comes from the Latin inflammare, which means \"to inflame\" or \"to burst into flame.\" The \"not\" \"in-\" has nothing to do with it."

She takes an assumption a reader would find reasonable and corrects it with the
documented Latin, then names precisely which part of the guess failed. The
confidence is earned on the etymology she cites, not asserted over the reader.

## Mark Forsyth, "The Meaning of Pasta"

Source: https://blog.inkyfool.com/2021/03/the-meaning-of-pasta.html

> "Linguine is a diminutive, as is spaghetti. The Italian for string is spago; the diminutive form, the little string, is a spaghetto; and the plural is spaghetti."

Forsyth unpacks a familiar word down to the literal picture inside it: spaghetti
is little strings, walked plainly from *spago* to *spaghetto* to *spaghetti*. The
person shows in the quiet confiding tone — he has just told the reader they have
been eating little strings all along, and lets that sit.

> "Pasta, of course, is just a paste, a dough. ... If you mash up a genre into a paste, you get a pastiche."

He follows one root, *paste*, forward into a word that hides it, so the link to
*pastiche* is a small surprise the reader can check for themselves. The pleasure
comes from the literal meaning itself, not from a joke set on top of it.
