# Evidence record: word-of-the-day/procrustean (01)

The evidence supports every commissioned question. Two dictionaries (Merriam-Webster, read in full; the Oxford English Dictionary, whose entry is paywalled but whose attestation data was surfaced from its own page) give the definition and an English first attestation of 1647 for the adjective. Two classical primary texts, read in translation from their own editions, carry the myth: Plutarch's *Life of Theseus* 11 and Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 1.4. They diverge on the mechanism in a way the article should keep: Plutarch describes one bed and Theseus killing Procrustes by his own method, while Apollodorus describes two beds of deliberately wrong sizes. The Greek derivation of the name is documented. Three modern uses show the arbitrary-standard sense; two of them (a 2021 law-review article and a 1963 Supreme Court opinion) were read in full at their own pages, and the third (a 1955 state opinion) is recorded from search of its host page because the host bot-blocks automated fetches.

The record is thin in two places, both recorded honestly below: the OED adjective entry could not be read past its paywall, so its 1647 date rests on the attestation OED itself lists rather than on a reading of the full entry; and the secondary classical accounts named in the brief (Diodorus Siculus, Pausanias) could not be opened at a resolving full-text page, so only Plutarch and Apollodorus are cited for the myth.

## Contradictions

The classical sources diverge on how the myth is told, and the divergence is worth keeping in the article rather than smoothing over.

- **One bed or two.** Plutarch (Theseus 11) writes of a single bed and of Theseus making Procrustes "fit his bed." Apollodorus (Epitome 1.4) gives two beds, one short and one long, so that no guest is ever the right size. The two-bed version is the one that makes the "always the wrong standard" point most sharply; the single-bed version is older-sounding and plainer. Merriam-Webster's own etymology note blends the two, describing one bed on which the too-tall are cut and the too-short are stretched.
- **The name.** Plutarch calls him "Damastes, surnamed Procrustes." Apollodorus calls him Damastes, also named Polypemon and Procrustes. "Procrustes" is the epithet ("the stretcher"), not the birth name, in both. The article should treat Procrustes as the nickname the deed earned.
- **The date of first English use, by sense.** Merriam-Webster and the OED both put the adjective at 1647, in the sense "of or relating to Procrustes" (M-W sense 1). Etymonline instead dates the adjective to 1822, and to the figurative sense specifically ("violently making conformable to standard"), with a variant spelling "Procrustian" by 1776. These are not necessarily in conflict: the literal "relating to Procrustes" sense is attested earlier (1647) than the fully generalized "forcing to an arbitrary standard" sense (which etymonline places in the 1820s). The article should not claim 1647 for the modern figurative meaning without this distinction.
