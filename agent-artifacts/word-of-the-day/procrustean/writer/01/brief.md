Focus this round: the paper's smallest read (word band ~550-800 words). Tell the
documented Procrustes myth from the primary sources, trace how it became the
English adjective, and ground the present sense in a real modern use. Draw the
distinction the evidence supports: procrustean is the arbitrariness of the
standard and the forcing of the case to fit it, not a synonym for strict. Where
classical sources give variants (the alternate names, the bed count), report what
they say rather than smoothing to one tale. Cite dictionaries for the definition
and classical primary sources for the myth.

Recent habits to break (see commission): do not use the "was a X before it was a
Y" or "named for the wrong X" headline mold, and give the closing section its own
concrete heading rather than the recurring "the distinction worth keeping" / "the
line the word holds" mold.

From the researcher's flags (act on these): distinguish two attestation dates and
do not conflate them — 1647 is firm for the literal "of/relating to Procrustes"
adjective, while the figurative arbitrary-standard sense the piece is about dates
to 1822 (a "Procrustian" variant by 1776). Do not attach 1647 to the modern
meaning. Ground the present sense in the two fully-verified modern uses (Ker v.
California, 1963, Justice Clark, "not susceptible of Procrustean application"; and
Krotoszynski's 2021 "Procrustean bed"); the Clements v. Constantine line is
corroboration only and was not read directly, so lean on the two verified ones.
The myth rests on Plutarch (Life of Theseus 11) and Pseudo-Apollodorus (Epitome
1.4), both read in full; Diodorus and Pausanias could not be opened, so do not
cite them as if read.
