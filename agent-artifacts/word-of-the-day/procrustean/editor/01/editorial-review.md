# Editorial review: word-of-the-day/procrustean (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: *procrustean* names a standard that is arbitrary and set by one party,
with the case forced to fit it, and that edge is what separates it from *strict*
or *standardized*. The dating carries the thesis: the literal "of/relating to
Procrustes" adjective is attested in 1647, but the figurative arbitrary-standard
sense arrives only in the 1820s.

The claims it stands on, and how each held:

- **Myth, one bed (Plutarch).** "Damastes, surnamed Procrustes," at Erineus,
  killed by Theseus by being made to fit his own bed. Cited to s2 with locator
  Life of Theseus 11.1. Matches the evidence quotation exactly, singular "bed."
  Held.
- **Myth, two beds (Apollodorus).** Short guests hammered out on the big bed,
  tall guests sawed down on the small bed; the third name Polypemon. Cited to s4
  with locator Epitome 1.4. Matches the evidence quotation. Held. Diodorus and
  Pausanias, which the researcher could not open, are correctly absent.
- **Name derivation.** From *prokrouein*, "to beat out or stretch out";
  Procrustes as "the stretcher." Cited to s3 (etymonline). Matches evidence.
  Held.
- **1647 literal attestation.** Cited to s1 (M-W) and s5 (OED). I reopened M-W:
  First Known Use 1647 in the meaning at sense 1. Corroborated. Held, and the
  article is careful to attach 1647 only to the "relating to Procrustes" sense,
  never the modern one.
- **1822 figurative / 1776 variant.** Cited to s3. I reopened etymonline: 1822
  for the figurative "violently making conformable to standard," *Procrustian*
  by 1776. Both verbatim correct. Held.
- **Definition.** "Imposing a single arbitrary standard and forcing each case to
  fit it." Cited to s1. A faithful paraphrase of M-W sense 2 ("marked by
  arbitrary often ruthless disregard of individual differences"). Held.
- **Modern use, Ker v. California.** Blockquote "standards of reasonableness
  under the Fourth Amendment are not susceptible of Procrustean application,"
  Justice Clark, 1963. Cited to s6, locator 374 U.S. 23, Part I. I reopened
  Cornell LII: the sentence appears verbatim, opinion by Mr. Justice Clark, 1963.
  The blockquote is a faithful sub-quote of the fuller sentence. Held.
- **Modern use, Krotoszynski 2021.** "a great deal of contemporary First
  Amendment law arguably resembles a Procrustean bed." Cited to s7. Matches
  evidence. Held. Clements v. Constantine, read only from search, is correctly
  not cited.

This round's focus, all confirmed: the two attestation dates are never
conflated (dek and the "adjective arrives twice" section keep 1647 literal and
1822 figurative apart); the myth rests only on the two primaries actually read;
the modern examples are the two verified opinions; the arbitrary-standard
distinction is drawn and cited to M-W.

`data-nb-kind` audit: s1/s2/s4/s5/s6/s7 primary, s3 (etymonline) secondary — all
match the primary/secondary test and the evidence record. No mislabel hiding a
missing independent source; the myth carries two independent primaries and the
modern sense two more.

Display text checked descriptor by descriptor: headline, dek, the three

break. Corrected in place to match M-W, the source the card's definition already

No broken central claim, no miscitation, no source-policy failure to route.

## Cut

Two sentences removed, no slop survivors left standing.

- **Signpost / formula echo (closing section).** "That is the distinction the
  word keeps, and the reason to keep it." Cut. It graded the argument and
  announced the point instead of making it; the two sentences after it ("When a
  standard is fair... *strict* is the honest word. When the bed was built to fit
  no one, *procrustean* is the only word that says so.") are the actual reason
  and land it. It also echoed the recent-pattern "the distinction worth keeping"
  / "the line the word holds" closer mold in prose, which the commission and
  brief flag as a habit to break. The section heading ("When 'strict' is the
  honest word") already avoids that mold; this sentence reintroduced it.
- **Uncorroborated derived-date padding (writer flag 1).** "The fixed phrase
  *Procrustean bed* is recorded from 1789 and the verb *Procrusteanize* from
  1846." Cut. Both dates rest on OED attestation surfaced past the paywall, not a
  reading of the full entry, and neither is load-bearing to the 1647-vs-1822
  argument. In the paper's smallest read, a bare list of two derived-form dates
  is padding. Writer flag 1 asked for a decision on the three uncorroborated OED
  dates; my decision follows below.

Writer flag 1, decided: of the three OED-surfaced-only dates, the 1789 phrase
and 1846 verb are cut (above). The 1581 noun ("English took in the man before it
took in the adjective. The noun *Procrustes* is recorded by 1581, in a
translation by John Studley") is kept: unlike the other two, it anchors a real
teaching beat that opens the section and sets up "the adjective arrives twice,"
and the date is OED's own published first-use attestation, adequately sourced for
a date claim even without the quotation paragraph. This is the earns-its-place
test applied per sentence, not a blanket keep or cut.

Slop, negative parallelism, and prompt-leakage passes: the dek's "attested in
1647, but... took until the 1820s" is a genuine two-date contrast, not an
invented strawman, and survives. The closer's "the fault is the shape of the
standard, not its severity" corrects the exact misconception the piece names
(strict vs procrustean), so it earns the "not" form. The commission's framing of
the distinction is the reported meaning of the word, grounded in M-W sense 2 and
the myth, not leaked instruction. No phrasing borrowed from the Quinion, Stamper,
or Forsyth exemplars. Punctuation is controlled; the one em-dash aside is a true
definitional aside. Headings each name a step in the piece's own nouns.

## Reader

Read straight through as the declared reader, I finish with something no single
source gives: the two-bed variant plus the 1647/1822 dating gap plus two legal
uses, assembled into a working rule for when *procrustean* is the right word and
when *strict* is the honest one. The evidence record holds those as separate,
unconnected facts; the article is what connects them. The draft-handoff's
original-work sentence claims exactly that synthesis, and it survives the read.
The prose sits closer to the voice-guide's Quinion register — dated facts stated
flatly, the present sense grounded in a real cited sentence (the Ker quote) the
way Quinion grounds *posh* in its 1918 *Punch* line — than to a median summary.
The headline, "Procrustes never adjusted the bed," is the largest claim and the
piece defends it: the standard never moves, the case is forced.
