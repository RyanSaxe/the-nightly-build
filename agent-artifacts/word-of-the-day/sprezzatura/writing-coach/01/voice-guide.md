# Voice guide: sprezzatura

## Directive

Register: calm, exact, quietly amused. You are certain enough about the
material to underplay it. Never lecture the reader toward the interesting
part; build the sentence so the interesting part is what it says. Reader
relationship: a smart equal who has not met this word, not a student being
taught it — earn attention through the sentence's own construction, not by
flagging that something is coming ("what's remarkable is...", "here's the
thing").

Do not open by asserting the definition or the coinage date as a fact to be
accepted. Open on the specific scene instead: the paradox itself stated as an
observation about the world, or the exact textual moment (who is speaking, in
what room, saying what) before the word's meaning follows as a consequence of
that moment rather than a claim planted in front of it. A definition earned
through demonstration reads as discovery; a definition announced up front
reads as a label.

Treat the coinage, the classical lineage, and the modern use as one argument
tracked across three eras, not three facts in sequence. Each new fact should
answer a question the previous one raised (why did the coiner need this word;
what older idea was he drawing on; does the pattern still hold) rather than
simply advance the clock. Resist headings that sort the piece into chronology
("Origins," "Then," "Now") — name each section for what it argues.

For the Italian coinage: name it once, gloss it in the same breath in plain
English, and then use the English sense for the rest of the piece. Do not keep
swinging between the italicized foreign word and its translation once the
reader has the meaning — pick the name you will use and hold it. When you
quote the coining passage, name whose translation it is in the same sentence
that gives the quotation, as a fact about the text, not a hedge apologizing
for secondhand access. The same rule holds for the Latin lineage: quote it
once, source it, move on.

Diction: concrete over abstract, always. Name the book, the room, the
courtier, the year, not "a text" or "a historical figure" or "the period."
Once you name a person or thing, keep that exact name — a synonym reached for
variety (the writer / the diplomat / the courtier, for one man) reads as a
second person entering the piece. Let short declarative sentences carry the
heaviest claims. Earn the one longer sentence you allow yourself for the
paradox at the word's center — everywhere else, cut rather than pack. A
technical or Latin term gets built in the sentence where it first appears,
then used bare.

Land the close on the modern instance or the pattern itself, doing its work,
not on a summary of what the piece just showed or a moral drawn above it.

Recently used, do not reuse: opening on a bare definition-as-claim ("X
means..."); opening on "X coined it in YEAR for..."; a dek that reports
who-did-what-when as a fact rather than a stance; the eponym-correction shape
(word is popularly misused for a name, here is the real story); closing on a
reading list or a pointer away from the piece.

## Mark Forsyth, "Preposterous"
Source: https://blog.inkyfool.com/2010/11/preposterous.html
Craft:
- cadence: two short paragraphs. A coined word is taken apart live, morpheme
  by morpheme, before the coiner is named at all.
- argument: the word is its own evidence — dismantling its construction proves
  the claim the piece is making about it.
- evidence: the etymology shown, not asserted. The reader watches "before" and
  "after" get welded into a single absurd word and feels the logic land.
- stance: amused and certain, no hedging. The coiner's name arrives as a
  tossed-off aside ("by the way"), not a reveal the sentence has been building
  toward.
- notice: the sentence order itself performs the word's meaning — form matches
  claim.
- diction: plain short words framing the Latin roots so the roots stand out
  by contrast.
- reader: assumes curiosity, not expertise. Never explains why an etymology
  should matter to you.
- the move the axes miss: he never states the definition as a claim to
  accept. He makes the reader derive it by watching the word get built, so the
  "definition" disappears into demonstration instead of sitting as a
  standalone sentence.
Calibration: "It is beforeaftererous. It is preposterous."

## Merriam-Webster, "The Invention of 'Serendipity'"
Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/happy-birthday-serendipity
Craft:
- cadence: opens on two other authors' words as a foil, builds through
  comparison, and only then arrives at the subject.
- argument: coinage is rare and documentable, and most invented words fail —
  this one is offered as proof that intent, not luck, can still be called by
  its own name.
- evidence: a dated letter, the coiner's own definition inside it, the source
  story behind the term, the two-century gap before the word caught on — laid
  out as a narrative with a shape, not a list of facts.
- stance: dry, willing to flag its own irony rather than smooth past it (the
  adjective form may exist because of someone else's mistake, and the piece
  says so plainly).
- notice: catches that the word for a happy accident may itself have been
  born from one, and lets that irony do the closing instead of stating a
  moral over it.
- diction: concrete counts instead of vague magnitude — a specific number of
  letters, a specific number of words traced to them, an exact date.
- reader: general but literate. Assumes the reader already knows what
  "coined" and "etymology" mean and never stops to define them.
- the move the axes miss: it never separates "where the word came from" from
  "why it still matters" into different sections. The closing irony pulls
  both threads together in one move, so the piece ends where its argument
  peaks rather than after it.
Calibration: "Jane Austen didn't invent baseball. Charles Dickens didn't
invent boredom."

## Kory Stamper, "Assembling the Treasury, Wordhoard, Synonymicon, Thesaurus"
Source: https://korystamper.com/2012/05/24/assembling-the-treasury-wordhoard-synonymicon-thesaurus/
Craft:
- cadence: a long, confident opening sentence undercut immediately by a short
  one that names the emotional reality underneath the claim.
- argument: professional objectivity is a performance, and the honest
  description of the daily craft is offered as proof of the opposite of what
  the profession claims about itself.
- evidence: her own working process, named step by step, including one term
  she coins for a single use and then retires.
- stance: an insider being wry about her own field rather than deferential to
  the expertise she holds.
- notice: she catches herself being wrong — defensive about a colleague's
  edit — and reports the correction plainly instead of writing around it.
- diction: a compact invented name for a phenomenon ("Well-Hung Syndrome")
  that compresses a whole observation into one tag, used once and not
  repeated as a refrain.
- reader: assumes no background in the craft. Builds each term in the
  sentence where it is needed before using it bare.
- the move the axes miss: she stages her own certainty and then revises it in
  front of the reader, so the piece's turn is lived in real time rather than
  announced as a lesson learned.
Calibration: "They get worked up into a veritable froth if you suggest
otherwise, maybe even raising their voices to conversational levels and
daring to make eye contact when they tell you that you are utterly wrong."
