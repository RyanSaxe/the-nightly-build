# Editorial review: word-of-the-day/sprezzatura (editor, 01)

## Three required reads

Skeptic: thesis "Castiglione coined sprezzatura as a cure for affectation, built
the word's own failure test into the same passage that coins it, drew on an
unnamed classical current that art should hide itself, and the standard it names
is still literally scored today"; tested 6 claims (headline, dek, and the four
subheads, each as claim and as label); broke: none. Every quotation checks
verbatim against the evidence record: the Opdycke coinage line, the Italian
"una certa sprezzatura, che nasconda l'arte," the Pierpaolo "legs stiff to the
toe" line, the Messer Roberto diagnosis ("striving with all his might to seem to
be taking no thought, and this is taking too much thought"), the unnamed-orators
passage, Cicero (De Oratore 2.4), and the De Cruz figure-skating "effortless
power" language. The costliest checks pass: the Latin tag "ars adeo latet arte
sua" is attributed to Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.251-252 (the Pygmalion episode),
NOT the Ars Amatoria the commission first named; no OED claim ("studied
carelessness," the 1878 Cornhill date) slipped in; no Hoby wording
("reckelesness"/"disgracing") is quoted. All eight `data-nb-kind` labels are
correct (Castiglione x2, Cicero, Ovid = primary; Merriam-Webster, Britannica,
De Cruz, Treccani = secondary). Etymology (Treccani) stays quarantined in the
closing paragraph as history, distinct from the present sense (MW/De Cruz). Two
minor, non-blocking notes: the speaker's name is spelled "Lodovico da Canossa"
where the evidence record (Britannica) has "Ludovico" — both are attested
Renaissance spellings, so I left it; and the dek's "The same test that... now
grades..." mildly echoes zugzwang's recent heading "The same test off the
board," but the dek is a specific stance (Renaissance dancer to figure skater),
not a stamped mold, so it stands.

Cut: 3 edits (2 deletions, 1 reword); worst tell: the Merriam-Webster sentence
stated its single point twice inside one sentence, "Four centuries on...
still tags it an Italian noun, not a naturalized English headword: after four
hundred years in English, sprezzatura has never quite become just an English
word" — the clause after the colon restated the clause before it with no new
fact, so it went.

Reader: this gives me a single argument the sources hold only as separate facts
— that Castiglione wrote the cure's own failure test into the very three
paragraphs that coin it, that the pattern is an old classical current he never
credits, and that a figure-skating judge scoring "effortless power" and a count
docking a dancer for visible strain are running the same arithmetic the word's
own root ("to devalue," from pretium) sets up, so a performance is priced higher
the less it looks like it cost. That throughline and its closing move are the
draft's own work, absent from the evidence record, and they match the handoff's
original-work sentence. The prose sits closer to the voice-guide exemplars
(etymology shown not asserted, the irony left to do the closing) than to a
median summary. The headline holds as the largest claim: Castiglione's own text
does diagnose its fakers.

## Direct edits made (prose only, no markup/assets touched)

1. Orientation, para 1: reworded a redundant lead-in. "He warns the room to
   avoid affectation, '...'" repeated "warns the room away from affectation"
   from the sentence two clauses earlier. Changed to "Affectation is to be
   avoided, he says, '...'" — removes the doubled verb+object, keeps the quote
   and the attribution, stays faithful to Opdycke ("to avoid affectation to the
   uttermost").
2. Orientation, para 2: deleted the self-repeating tail of the Merriam-Webster
   sentence (": after four hundred years in English, sprezzatura has never quite
   become just an English word"). The point — MW still labels it an Italian noun
   — survives intact with its citation.
3. Closing section, para 2: deleted the announce-the-irony frame "The word
   carries its own irony." The etymology sentences that follow show the irony;
   naming it first told what the next two sentences demonstrate. The review
   brief flagged this closer specifically; it now lands as argument, not label.
