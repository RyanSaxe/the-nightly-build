# Voice guide: word-of-the-day/silhouette (01)

## Directive

This is the paper's smallest read, held to the exactness of its longest. Keep the
house baseline — calm sentences, structure doing the persuading — then earn one
thing the baseline does not grant: it is allowed to be funny. The comedy is
deadpan and it rides on the record. State the documented facts flat and let their
incongruity land on its own. Do not tell the reader a fact is funny, ironic, or
surprising; if the sentence needs that label, the arrangement has failed.

Write for a well-read peer who enjoys a small, strange, true story told without
hand-holding. Never address that reader as "you." Never stage a scene for them to
picture. Never perform delight on their behalf. The pleasure is in the facts set
in order, not in the narration flagging its own charm.

Three moves will change sentences in this piece that the default would not
produce:

- **Let the record carry the joke.** The austerity and the cut-paper hobby are
  already absurd against each other. Report them plainly, in order, and stop. The
  writer supplies no wink.
- **Tell the origin as compressed narrative.** Named people, firm dates, one
  event per sentence, each beat cited. History moves the meaning forward; it is
  not backdrop.
- **Adjudicate the competing accounts in plain sentences.** Give each account its
  strongest form, cite it, and say exactly how far the documented record reaches
  before certainty runs out. Naming the limit is the finish, not a hedge.

## Licenses

form: deadpan understatement on documented incongruity
move: Liberman and Forsyth report an odd fact in a level register and let the
  reader feel the absurdity; the sentence never grades its own material. Deploy it
  where the historical facts already collide (a minister of thrift, a fashion for
  free portraits).
bar:  delete the humor and a true, sourced fact must remain in the sentence. If
  what is left is only a quip, cut it.

form: compressed narrative of origin, with named actors and dates
move: the exemplars advance by concrete event — a person, a year, a thing that
  happened — rather than by summary of a period. Each step is the next step of the
  meaning's development.
bar:  every narrative beat carries an inline citation and moves the sense forward.
  A sentence that only sets atmosphere is cut.

form: open adjudication of rival etymologies in the writer's own voice
move: Liberman states each theory at its strongest, weighs it against the
  evidence, and declines false certainty, marking plainly where the record ends.
bar:  each account gets its best case and a cited source; the verdict, or the
  refusal to reach one, is grounded in what the sources do and do not show, never
  asserted.

form: the wry parenthetical or short aside
move: a true aside that carries a real fact and a dry angle at once, used once or
  twice across the piece, not as a reflex.
bar:  the sentence survives without it and the aside carries information, not
  commentary. House punctuation limits still apply; at most once in a stretch.

## Recently used, do not reuse

- The coiner-and-a-year opener ("In 1759, Étienne de Silhouette..."). Recent word
  pieces open on the person and a date by reflex; find another entry into the
  story.
- The "X means Y" title-and-first-line mold, and any headline of that shape.
- The breezy second-person hook the brief rules out: "If you have ever...", "You
  know the...", "Picture a...", and every opener that addresses or instructs the
  reader.
- The three banned dek molds: the semicolon reversal, the suspended question
  ("...and the real question is whether"), and the comma triad closed with "and."
- Scaffolding headings ("Origins", "Background", "Legacy", "The Word Today").
  Vary the heading shapes; name the step of the argument in the piece's own nouns.

## Anatoly Liberman, "Fine and Dandy (In All Except Etymology)"
Source: https://blog.oup.com/2009/11/fine-and-dandy/
Craft:
- cadence: unhurried and even; declaratives that state a finding, then qualify it
  in the same breath ("Its origin (most probably, dialectal) remains a mystery").
- argument: builds by elimination — lay out the candidate origins, test each,
  keep the survivors, refuse to crown one when the evidence will not bear it.
- evidence: dated first appearances, dialect range, cognate forms; the reasoning
  from evidence to conclusion is shown, not summarized.
- stance: honest uncertainty treated as a result, not a failure; "we will
  probably never know for sure" is a finding he is willing to publish.
- notice: the complications that break a tidy story — the other, unrelated senses
  of a word that any origin theory has to account for.
- diction: technical terms sit beside plain ones without apology; precision does
  not require stiffness.
- reader: addressed as a peer following an investigation, never flattered, never
  instructed.
- the important move: he separates a fanciful suggestion from a working
  hypothesis and says which is which, so the reader can weigh the accounts too.

## Mark Forsyth, "Fluke"
Source: https://blog.inkyfool.com/2011/02/fluke.html
Craft:
- cadence: brisk, forward-leaning; short steps that accelerate as the trail warms,
  each paragraph advancing the hunt.
- argument: a piece of detection — pose the puzzle, consult the record, test a
  theory against a checkable fact (a book's sales, a surname's age), report where
  it holds.
- evidence: named source, edition, date; he verifies a claim rather than
  asserting it, and shows the check.
- stance: closes on qualified persuasion, not proof ("all of which convinces
  me..."); probability stated as probability.
- notice: the detail that quietly decides the case — that a name predates the book
  that supposedly coined it.
- diction: registers mixed on purpose, scholarly citation next to plain speech,
  never dense.
- reader: warm and companionable — the very "dear reader" familiarity this article
  must NOT borrow; take his detective momentum and epistemic honesty, leave the
  address behind.
- the important move: he holds several origins live at once without flattening
  them into one false certainty, which is exactly the discipline the competing
  accounts of this word require.

## Merriam-Webster, "The Odd History of 'Sanguine'"
Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/sanguine-word-history
Craft:
- cadence: alternates crisp declaratives with the occasional longer explanatory
  sentence, so the meaning-shift never reads as a list.
- argument: traces a single semantic journey — a physical substance to a
  temperament to a modern trait — one clean link at a time.
- evidence: the humoral scheme laid out concretely (phlegmatic, bilious) so the
  shift is explained, not just stated; the mechanism of the change is the content.
- stance: light and assured; it trusts the shift itself to be interesting and does
  not oversell it.
- notice: the buried literal sense inside a word now used only figuratively — the
  blood still sitting inside "optimism."
- diction: plain and exact, the gruesome root named rather than softened.
- reader: its second-person opener ("If you're an optimist...") is the breezy hook
  this article rejects; take the clean one-link-at-a-time tracing of meaning, drop
  the "you."
- the important move: it makes the mechanism of a meaning-shift the payoff, which
  is the model for moving "à la silhouette" from a jibe to a portrait to an
  outline.
