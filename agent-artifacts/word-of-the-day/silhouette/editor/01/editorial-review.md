# Editorial review: word-of-the-day/silhouette (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: *silhouette* is an eponym for a short-lived French finance minister, but
the record does not settle *why* his name attached to the cut-paper profile, and
by the time the word entered wide use it had outlived the man entirely. The
claims it stands on:

1. The word is named for Étienne de Silhouette, Louis XV's controller-general in
   1759 (headline, orientation).
2. His 1759 ministry was an austerity program that made his name a byword for
   cheapness (austerity section).
3. Why the name fastened to the shadow-profile is genuinely unsettled: three
   distinct mechanisms compete and no contemporary source decides (contested
   origin).
4. A reported 1758 usage predating the ministry undercuts the frugality
   etymology (the note).
5. The present "outline" sense is attested in real usage, evidenced by two Doyle
   passages (present use).

I opened all eight hrefs as printed and read each against the sentence citing
it. Every link lands on the source itself.

- Claim 1 holds. MW's etymology attributes the word to "Étienne de Silhouette
  †1767 French controller general of finances" (s1); Wikipedia carries the
  biography (s2). Pronunciation `\ˌsi-lə-ˈwet\` matches MW exactly.
- Claim 2 holds. The budget figures (286M vs 503M livres, ≥94M debt service),
  the general subvention, the 26 October plate-melting order, Voltaire's
  objection, and the 21 November dismissal all match Wikipedia (s2); the
  Pompadour patronage and eight-month tenure match Kemp (s3). These figures are
  secondary (Wikipedia), not verified to a primary financial record, but they
  are attributed and not contested, so they clear the bar for context.
- Claim 3 holds and is handled honestly. Each of the three accounts gets its
  best case with a cited owner: ridicule/"culottes à la Silhouette" (s3, s4),
  the profile-cutting pastime with Kemp's quote verified verbatim against the
  Oxford blog (s3), and MW's own "perhaps from his ephemeral tenure" (s1). The
  verdict is not asserted beyond what Word Histories states — it "judges the
  hobby account the most persuasive... while conceding that none is proven,"
  which matches s5 (Tréguer finds the inventor/hobby theory "more convincing"
  but "unprovable").
- Claim 4 is correctly attributed, not asserted. The note credits Word Histories
  via Dulaurens's 1763 *L'Arétin moderne* for the 1758 "portraits à la
  Silhouëtte," and flags that "the Dulaurens text could not be examined here."
  The section heading reads "A profile reported before the ministry" — reported,
  not stated. No display text asserts the contested date. Verified against s5.
- Claim 5 holds. Both Doyle quotations are exact against the Gutenberg texts:
  "pass twice in a dark silhouette against the blind" (s7, "A Scandal in
  Bohemia," 1891) and "those black silhouettes which our grandparents loved to
  frame" (s8, "The Adventure of the Empty House," 1903). Both are correctly
  labeled primary.

One break. The disputed-debut sentence reads: "Merriam-Webster dates the noun to
1783, while the OED and etymonline place it at 1798, in The Monthly Review,"
cited to s1, s4, s5. I opened etymonline (s4) twice: its entry dates the English
portrait sense to **1792** ("earlier as *portrait à la silhouette* (1766)") and
the number 1798 appears nowhere in it. Etymonline does not place the word at
1798; the 1798 Monthly Review attestation is owned by Word Histories / the OED
(s5). This is a miscitation — a true fact (the debut is a live range) carrying a
false label (etymonline as a 1798 source). The evidence record and both briefs
grouped "OED/etymonline 1798," but the source itself does not bear it. The range
survives on MW 1783 (s1) versus OED 1798 (s5); etymonline must come out of the
1798 clause. This is a citation/markup change, so it routes to the writer.

`data-nb-kind` audit: 3 primary (s1 MW, s7/s8 Doyle) and 5 secondary (s2
Wikipedia, s3 Oxford blog, s4 etymonline, s5 Word Histories, s6 Britannica) —
matches the claimed 3/5. MW as primary is defensible for the lexicographic facts
it owns (definition, pronunciation, first-use date, etymology); Doyle's texts
own their own wording. No secondary is mislabeled primary; no missing
independent source is hidden.

The definition card is cited to MW (s1), stands first with no heading above it,
and carries the pronunciation. Its wording is a faithful paraphrase of MW's
sense (MW: "cut from dark material and mounted on a light ground"; the card:
"cut from black paper and mounted on a light ground"), consistent and not a
false claim. Acceptable for a card attributed to the dictionary.

## Reader

Read straight through, the piece gives what no single source gives: it takes the
popular one-cause "named because he was stingy" story and splits it into three
documented mechanisms, then sets the reported 1758 pre-ministry dating against
them so the tidy frugality etymology fails on the page instead of by assertion.
The juxtaposition a source-by-source reader would not assemble — a minister of
thrift lending his name to a fashion for cheap portraits, who left no portrait of
himself — is the payoff, and it is earned from the record, not narrated. The
original-work sentence in the draft handoff claims exactly this synthesis, and
the article delivers it. The prose sits closer to the voice-guide exemplars
(Liberman/Forsyth deadpan and honest uncertainty) than to a median summary. The
headline commits to the eponymy (uncontested) without overclaiming the disputed
mechanism, and holds as the largest claim.
