# Evidence: word-of-the-day/laconic (researcher/01)

The definition, etymology, and first-attestation date are solidly established
by two independent dictionaries (Merriam-Webster and the OED, the latter
gated but corroborated). The Philip-Sparta anecdote is genuinely Plutarch's,
but it is **not** where the commission and brief expect it: the one-word "If"
reply does not appear in "Sayings of Spartans" or "Sayings of Kings and
Commanders." It is in a third Moralia essay, "On Talkativeness" (*De
garrulitate*), chapter 17, Stephanus 511A — confirmed by reading the passage
itself, after checking and ruling out the other two named locations. Plutarch's
own wording of Philip's threat is "I shall turn you out," not "raze," which
the commission's framing had used; the writer should quote the former. The
Peloponnese campaign's historical setting (338 BC, after Chaeronea) is
confirmed by secondary classical-history sources but not tied to a specific
day. The modern-usage requirement is met by Merriam-Webster's own attributed
citation corpus; I could not independently re-open the underlying news
articles (search budget for this task was exhausted mid-research and the
outlets' own search pages block automated fetches), so treat those specific
quotes as MW-attributed rather than independently re-verified at the
original outlet.

## Contradictions

- The commission's framing states Philip "warned that if he entered Laconia he
  would raze Sparta." Plutarch's actual line, in the passage that carries the
  "If" reply, is "If I invade Laconia, I shall turn you out" — a threat of
  expulsion, not of razing the city. The word "raze" does appear in the
  Plutarch corpus, but attached to a different city and a different anecdote
  (Agesipolis's remark that Philip "razed Olynthus... in a few days," Sayings
  of Spartans). The writer should quote Plutarch's actual wording for the
  Sparta threat rather than "raze."
- The commission and brief both point to "Moralia, 'Sayings of Spartans' /
  'Sayings of Kings and Commanders'" as the location of the "If" reply. Neither
  contains it. It is in a third essay, "On Talkativeness" (De garrulitate),
  chapter 17 (511A). "Sayings of Spartans" does contain two closely related
  exchanges with Philip ("Neither," at 233E-F, and "No," at 235B) — which is
  presumably why the commission assumed the "If" line lived there too — but
  not the "If" line itself. De garrulitate 513A also has its own telling of the
  "No" exchange, meaning Plutarch recorded the "No" reply twice, in two
  different essays, while the "If" reply appears only once, in De garrulitate.
- Wikipedia's "Laconic phrase" article, when asked, initially cited the "Sayings
  of Spartans" (233E) for the "Neither" half of the exchange and separately
  named De garrulitate for the "If" half — which turned out to be exactly
  right once checked against the primary text. It is listed in Discarded below
  only because it was used purely as a lead to the correct primary locator, not
  as evidentiary support in itself.
