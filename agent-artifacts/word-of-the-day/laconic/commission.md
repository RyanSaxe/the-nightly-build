# Commission: word-of-the-day/laconic

## The word and why it earns the slot

"Laconic": saying much in few words, terse to the point of bluntness. The word is
its own best argument for keeping it, because the useful idea and the origin story
are the same thing. It descends from Greek `Lakonikos`, "of Laconia," the region
around Sparta, whose people were known for compressed speech. The documented
anecdote that fixes the sense: Philip II of Macedon warned that if he entered
Laconia he would raze Sparta, and the Spartans answered with a single word, "If."
The word carries a recognizable pattern well beyond Sparta, which is what a Word
of the Day is for.

## What the article does

Open with the card: one plain, cited definition from an authoritative dictionary,
standing first with no heading above it. Then tell the rest as history in two to
five flexible sections, in whatever order teaches best: the ethnonym and the
Spartan reputation for terse speech, the documented anecdote (Philip's threat and
the one-word reply, and the register it belonged to, alongside other recorded
Spartan retorts), the word's arrival in English (its seventeenth-century
attestation as an adjective) and the settling of its present sense, and real
present-day use. The last section lands why the word still earns a place in a
working vocabulary. Etymology is history, not proof of the present meaning; ground
the present sense in actual use, not in the origin alone.

## Sources to begin from (researcher confirms and reads the primary)

- An authoritative dictionary for the definition and etymology: the Oxford English
  Dictionary and/or Merriam-Webster (`laconic`; `Laconia`/`Laconian`), for the
  cited one-sentence definition and the first attestation.
- Plutarch as the original source for the "If" reply and the Spartan sayings
  (Moralia, the "Sayings of Spartans" / "Sayings of Kings and Commanders"); read
  the passage, do not repeat it secondhand.
- A reliable classical reference for Philip II's campaign against the
  Peloponnese, to place the anecdote.
- A real modern usage example (a dictionary usage note or an attested quotation)
  that shows the present sense.

## Habits to break (from the recent word-of-the-day record)

The maverick piece (2026-08-14) closed on "Never claimed, not merely departed," a
negative-parallelism closer, and several recent word pieces (maverick, boondoggle,
gerrymander) ran a "the story you know is wrong" debunking frame. Laconic's origin
is well documented, so do not manufacture a debunking or contested-origin angle,
and do not close on a negative-parallelism line ("not X, but Y"). Tell the
documented story straight and land the usefulness. Keep the definition to one
cited sentence.
