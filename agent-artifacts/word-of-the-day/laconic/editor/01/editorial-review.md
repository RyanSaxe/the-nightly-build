# Editorial review: word-of-the-day/laconic (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: "laconic" is a place-name adjective that came to name a manner
independent of the place. The article stands on four claims: the word descends
from Laconia, Sparta's region; the Spartans had a documented reputation for
terse speech; Plutarch records the reputation in action (Philip's threat, the
one-word "If," and a flat "No"); and the word, borrowed into English long after,
still names that manner in live use today.

I opened all eight citation hrefs as the article prints them.

- **The Plutarch anecdote (this round's first focus) holds exactly.** s4 lands on
  *De garrulitate* 17 (511A): "when Philip wrote to them, 'If I invade Laconia, I
  shall turn you out,' they wrote back, 'If.'" — verbatim as quoted. s5 lands on
  chapter 21 (513A): the large "No." The writer's correction of the commission's
  wrong locator and wrong verb ("turn you out," not "raze") is confirmed against
  the primary. The article correctly cites Plutarch, not etymonline, for this;
  etymonline (s7) still carries the "raze Sparta to the ground" paraphrase, and
  the draft rightly does not use it.
- **The modern-usage citation (second focus / writer's open question) holds.**
  s8 (Merriam-Webster's citation corpus) does carry the Dexter Filkins line
  attributed to *The New Yorker*, 10 Dec. 2022, worded exactly as printed. The
  sentence claims only what MW attributes and asserts nothing about which article
  it ran in, so it does not overclaim what it cannot confirm. No change needed;
  the open question is resolved.
- **The definition, etymology, and 1589 dating (s1, s6) hold.** MW's definition
  is quoted verbatim; the 1570/1576/1589 family dates match the evidence; s6
  (OED) resolves to the correct entry behind a login wall (gated, not dead) and
  is independently corroborated by s1, which the article already cites for that.
- **One break: the terseness quote was miscited.** The article attributed
  "famous for their terseness of speech" to "Merriam-Webster's companion entry
  for the ethnonym" (s2, *Laconian*). I opened s2: it reads only "of, relating
  to, or characteristic of Laconia, a region of ancient Greece" — no Sparta, no
  terseness, nothing the sentence claimed. The verbatim phrase actually lives on
  the *laconic* entry itself (s1), in its word history: "the Spartans were famous
  for their terseness of speech." The right source was already in the article, so
  I fixed it: the quote now cites s1, and s2 is re-pointed to the region claim it
  genuinely supports. No fact changed; the citation now lands where the words are.
- **A break in display text: "two centuries later" is off by an order of
  magnitude.** The dek and the "reaches English" heading both said English
  borrowed the word "two centuries later." The article dates the anecdote to 338
  BC and the attestation to 1589, a gap of roughly 1,900 years. I corrected both
  to "nearly two millennia later." The dek is duplicated in the nb-meta block and
  the body; I changed both so they still match.

## Cut

Five sentences drew scrutiny; two were slop, three were correctness fixes.

- **The philips-threat closer was a signpost with a factual slip.** "Both
  exchanges appear in a chapter Plutarch aims at chatterers, offered as proof
  that a reply can be almost wordless and still land" restated the section's own
  opener (which already names the essay and its purpose) and called two exchanges
  in two chapters "a chapter." Deleting it outright would have dropped the piece
  under the 550-word floor, so rather than leave a soft, redundant ending I
  recast it into a close reading the section had not yet earned: the wit of "If"
  is that it returns Philip his own conditional word. That reasoning is drawn
  from the two quotes already on the page (s4), not from new reporting.
- **"outside any classroom"** was an unearned flourish; nothing set up a
  classroom frame. Cut, leaving "shows the word doing exactly that work."
- **"reputation for silence"** (orientation heading) mislabeled the subject: the
  word is about few words, not none, and the piece's own thesis is "saying much
  in few words." Retitled "Sparta's reputation for terse speech."
- **"players"** named the same people the quoted sentence calls "athletes."
  Fixed to "athletes" so the noun stays constant.

No negative-parallelism closer, no manufactured debunking or contested-origin
frame; the recent-pattern risks the brief flagged (maverick's "not X, but Y"
close; the "story you know is wrong" frame) do not appear. The piece-ending
sentence — "Plutarch's record of the exchange with Philip gives that manner an
actual sound, in the Spartans' own words" — points to real quoted evidence and
lands the usefulness, so it survives the delete test. No em-dashes, no prose
semicolons, no banned-term overruns.

## Reader

Reading only what survives: I come away able to explain how a region's name
became an adjective for a manner, with the manner's actual sound (Plutarch's
"If" and "No"), the staggered English arrival (1570 / 1576 / 1589), and a
present-day instance (Filkins) — a throughline no single source supplies. That
matches the draft-handoff's original-work claim, which holds up on the page. The
prose sits closer to the voice-guide exemplars than to a median summary: it
leads with sourced quotes and specific dates and grounds present use in a real
sentence, the way the "Deadline," Dent, and Maier passages do. The headline,
"Sparta answered Philip's threat with one word," is a claim the piece defends.
