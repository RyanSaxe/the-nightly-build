# Voice guide: word-of-the-day/laconic

## How this piece should sound

At 550 to 800 words the register is compression itself: every sentence should
carry one dated fact tied to one named source, the way the two body
paragraphs of "The Bloody History of 'Deadline'" do. Nothing gets rounded off
to "long ago" or "an account" when the year and the document are known. The
definition card already states the word's meaning in one sentence; the
flexible sections exist to earn that meaning with evidence, not to restate it
more colorfully.

The "If" anecdote is what makes this word a Word of the Day pick rather than
a dictionary entry, and it survives only if Philip's threat and the Spartans'
reply arrive as something Plutarch actually wrote, not as something the piece
reports that he wrote. Eleanor Maier's virago essay never tells the reader
that Fabyan or Montagu praised a woman in masculine terms; it quotes their
sentences, original spelling intact, with the citation attached to each. The
commission already says to read Plutarch and not repeat him secondhand — this
is what that looks like on the page: the exchange itself, quoted, sourced to
the Moralia by name, not summarized as "a famous one-word reply."

The commission leaves room for one more recorded Spartan retort alongside the
"If." If one earns its place, it should behave the way Susie Dent moves from
one recovered word to the next: named, sourced, and gone in a sentence, never
gathered into a run for its own amusement. One additional retort sharpens the
pattern the piece is making; three or four turns the section into the kind of
catalogue the commission's habits-to-break note already warns against.

Ground the present-day section the way Maier grounds virago's afterlife: in
one actual use, not a claim that the word "remains useful." Virago the
publisher, its apple logo, and the reasoning behind choosing a four-century-
old word for a name are specific facts standing in for the abstraction "the
word still has currency." Laconic's closing section needs its own equivalent:
a real sentence someone wrote or said using the word in its present sense,
doing the work that a line asserting its usefulness would otherwise have to
do.

The commission rules out a "not X, but Y" closer here. Dent's paragraph on
"respair" shows a different way to land an ending: the exact citation first
(one entry, the year 1525, the dictionary named), then a single short,
declarative sentence, with nothing summarizing in between. That order of
operations — evidence, then one plain line — is available to this piece where
the negative-parallelism turn is not.

## Editors of Merriam-Webster, "The Bloody History of 'Deadline'"

Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/your-deadline-wont-kill-you

> The word began its life well before this, with our files indicating it was
> in use from the early 1860s, with the somewhat harsher definition of "a
> line drawn within or around a prison that a prisoner passes at the risk of
> being shot." Some of the earliest mentions of deadline come up in 1863,
> preserved in diaries kept by captive soldiers during the Civil War.

The sentence states the exact decade, quotes the original definition in its
own wording, and names the category of evidence ("our files," then diaries)
before naming who kept them. Nothing here is rounded off to "back then" — the
precision is what makes an old dictionary definition read as a fact rather
than a generality.

> Early in this year a Confederate prison for captured Union soldiers was
> established in Andersonville, Georgia, which would remain operational for
> slightly more than a year. The prison was notorious for poor conditions,
> harsh punishments, and for, you guessed it, having deadlines. Suddenly the
> word was being used considerably more often (in news reports, partisan
> poetry, and commission reports), often in reference to the prison at
> Andersonville.

The founding year, the location, and the length of operation are given as
plain facts before the one aside in the paragraph. "You guessed it" works
because the reader already has the specific facts needed to see the joke
coming — the wit rides on the reporting instead of replacing it.

> Although this sense of deadline saw some figurative use following the end
> of the Civil War, by the middle of the 20th century the dominant meaning of
> the word was well established as a time-related thing, rather than a
> shoot-a-person-for-crossing-a-line one.

The whole semantic shift compresses into one sentence, and the contrast is
built from the two literal definitions the piece already gave earlier, not
from a new summarizing label. It is a landing sentence that adds no fact the
article hasn't already earned.

## Susie Dent, "From respair to cacklefart – the joy of reclaiming long-lost positive words"

Source: https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2021/dec/26/respair-cacklefart-positive-words-english-language

> "Dwell on the beauty of life. Watch the stars, and see yourself running
> with them": words of positivity from the Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius. But
> how many of us really dwell on the upside of life, as opposed to its mad,
> bad, seamy side?

The essay opens on somebody else's exact words, attributed by name and title,
before Dent's own sentence turns them into a question. The quotation does the
work an introductory sentence would otherwise have to do, and Dent's own
voice only appears in the turn that follows it.

> These are the words that inexplicably lost their mojo at some point in the
> past, becoming a sorry crew of adjectives that includes unkempt, unruly,
> disgruntled, unwieldy and inept. Yet previous generations had the potential
> to be kempt, ruly, wieldy, ept and – most recently thanks to PG Wodehouse –
> gruntled.

Five words are named in a run, and the source of one of them is dropped into
the middle of the list rather than saved for its own sentence. Naming five
specific words instead of saying "several words" is what keeps the sentence a
report rather than a generalization.

> "Respair" has just one record next to it in the Oxford English Dictionary,
> from 1525, but its definition is sublime. Respair is fresh hope; a recovery
> from despair. May 2022 finally be its moment.

The dictionary citation — one record, the year — comes before the personal
claim that the definition is sublime, so the claim has evidence under it
before it lands. The closing sentence is short and plain, and it asks the
reader to feel only what the citation has already earned.

## Eleanor Maier, "'virago'"

Source: https://www.oed.com/discover/a-man-like-virago-of-a-stout-and-noble-spirit/

> Virago has at its heart the vir, the Latin word for man (compare virile and
> triumvirate) and is first recorded in English as the name Adam gave to Eve.
> As Adam explains (in the Middle English Wycliffite translation of Genesis
> 2:23): "this schal be clepid [i.e. called] virago, for she is takun of
> man".

The etymology is built from a named root, a same-root comparison the reader
already knows, and a dated primary text quoted in its original spelling
rather than modernized. Modernizing "clepid" to "called" would have made the
sentence easier to read and the history less exact; Maier keeps the original
word and glosses it in brackets instead.

> According to the chronicler Robert Fabyan, who died in 1513, Æthelflæd was
> "this noble venqueresse Uirago and made whose vertue can I nat expresse".
> The term is also used by Richard Montagu in the 17th century of Salome
> Alexandra of Judea who "ruled as Queene eight yeers and better: a man-like
> virago of a stout and noble spirit".

Two writers, four centuries apart, are quoted using the word about two
different women, in their own spelling, with a name and a date attached to
each. The pattern — a word of praise built from a masculine root, applied to
a woman with qualities coded as masculine — is shown twice instead of
asserted once, which is what makes it a demonstrated pattern rather than a
claim.

> The newly-revised OED entry, however, provides a comprehensive survey of
> the word's history and scope, providing evidence for meanings which
> Virago, the publisher, drew on: their logo of an apple, alluding to Eve's
> quest for forbidden knowledge; the evocation of strong, heroic women; and
> an ironic riposte to those who would see feminists as bad-tempered
> harpies.

The present-day relevance is traced through one real institution's actual
reasoning for its own name, down to the detail of the logo. The paragraph
never states that the word "remains relevant"; it names the publisher and
what the publisher did with the word instead, and the abstraction is left for
the reader to draw.
