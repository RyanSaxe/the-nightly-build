# Evidence record: word-of-the-day/byzantine (01)

The record supports every commissioned claim. The literal and figurative senses
are dictionary-attested: Merriam-Webster gives both the capitalized "of
Byzantium/the Byzantine Empire" senses and the lowercase figurative senses
("devious and usually surreptitious manner of operation"; "intricately
involved; labyrinthine"), a first-known-use of 1651, and dates the figurative
sense to the early 20th century; the OED's figurative sense ("reminiscent of
the manner, style, or spirit of Byzantine politics; intricate, complicated;
inflexible, rigid, unyielding") is first attested 1937. The origin story is
documented by reputable references: Byzantium as a Megarian colony under the
legendary Byzas (c. 657 BCE), Constantine's dedication of the refounded city as
New Rome / Constantinople in 330 CE, the empire's Roman self-identification, and
"Byzantine Empire" as Hieronymus Wolf's 1557 coinage in *Corpus Historiae
Byzantinae*. The pejorative sense's Enlightenment shaping is carried by a
primary Gibbon passage (Chapter 48) and by Montesquieu's 1734 *Considerations*.
The Lamport-Shostak-Pease 1982 borrowing is sourced from the paper itself.

The record is thin in two named places (both flagged below): the OED entry page
is subscription-gated and could not be read directly, so the OED wording and the
1937 date are carried by a secondary that quotes the OED verbatim, and the
attribution of that 1937 first citation to Arthur Koestler comes from a search
summary I could not confirm against the OED itself — cite the OED date, not the
Koestler name. The single modern-usage quotation (2015 Bloomberg) is quoted
through CJR; I did not resolve the Bloomberg original.

## Contradictions

- **Founding date of Byzantium.** Britannica's Istanbul entry gives "about 657
  bce" for Byzas's town; the commission and much popular reference say
  "traditionally 7th c. BCE" (some sources cite 667 BCE). These are consistent in
  century but differ by a decade; treat the exact year as traditional/legendary,
  not fixed. Byzas himself is explicitly "legendary" per Britannica ("according
  to legend"), so the eponymous-founder story is tradition, not documented fact.

- **Is the pejorative sense fair to the actual empire?** Gibbon's own Chapter 48
  ("a tedious and uniform tale of weakness and misery") is the Enlightenment view
  the modern adjective inherits, but it is contested by modern scholarship.
  Britannica frames "Byzantine" as a name that "illustrates the misconceptions to
  which the empire's history has often been subject," and a secondary source on
  Gibbon notes his continuous-decay thesis is treated as "a serious defect" of the
  later volumes given that Constantinople stood as a bulwark for roughly a
  thousand years. The word's negative charge is thus a historiographic artifact of
  the 18th century, not a neutral description of the state — a genuine tension the
  article can name.

- **"Byzantine Empire" as coinage.** Wolf (1557) is credited with first applying
  "Byzantine" to the Eastern Roman state, but standardization of the term is
  attributed to the 18th century (French authors, Montesquieu among them). So
  1557 is the coinage; the label's dominance is ~200 years later. The empire never
  used the term for itself (Britannica: its people "called themselves Romaioi, or
  Romans").

- **Attestation of the English figurative sense.** Merriam-Webster dates the
  complexity/intrigue sense to "the early 20th century" while giving 1651 as first
  known use of the word overall; the OED pins the figurative English sense to
  1937. These agree (early 20th century ≈ 1937); the 1651 date belongs to the
  literal sense, not the figurative one. Do not conflate them.
