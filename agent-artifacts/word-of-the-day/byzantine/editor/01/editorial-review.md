# Editorial review: word-of-the-day/byzantine (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: "byzantine" names one specific kind of complexity — the
deliberate-looking, opaque tangle — a charge the Enlightenment invented and
pinned to a state (the Eastern Roman Empire) that called itself Roman and never
bore the name in its own lifetime. The piece stands on four load-bearing claims,
all of which I tried to break against the evidence record and, where the record
was thin, against the cited sources directly.

1. **The empire called itself Roman, not Byzantine.** Held. Britannica (s3):
   "called themselves Romaioi, or Romans." The headline's central claim is
   sourced.
2. **"Byzantine" is a later desk-coinage (Wolf, 1557), standardized only in the
   18th century.** Held. s4 (Wikipedia, Hieronymus Wolf) carries 1557, *Corpus
   Historiae Byzantinae*, and "first to reference the Eastern Roman Empire with
   'Byzantinae.'" The contested standardization ("not standardized until the
   eighteenth century") is kept in-clause, as the evidence requires.
3. **The pejorative charge is an 18th-century historiographic artifact, not a
   fair account of the state.** Held and kept contested. Montesquieu 1734 (s5,
   quote verbatim) and Gibbon Ch. 48 (s6, both quotes verbatim) carry the
   Enlightenment charge; Britannica's "misconceptions" line (s3) supplies the
   counter-weight in the same paragraph. The literal 1651 sense (s1) and the
   figurative 1937 sense are kept distinct, as the brief demanded.
4. **The modern sense marks deliberate-seeming opacity, grounded in real use.**
   Held. The 2015 Bloomberg sentence is quoted through CJR (s8) with honest
   attribution ("Bloomberg described … as"), and the Lamport–Shostak–Pease 1982
   borrowing is the single sourced sentence the brief permits (s9). Bobby Jindal
   was Louisiana governor in 2015; title correct.

Display text, descriptor by descriptor: title, dek, all four subheads, and the
definition card check out against the record. I verified two descriptors that
sit in display/near-display text and were **not** in the evidence-record
paraphrase:

- **"German" (Wolf), dek and body.** Verified against the cited source s4
  itself, which describes Wolf as "a sixteenth-century German historian … born
  at Oettingen in Bayern, Germany." The descriptor is supported by the source
  the article already cites; the record's paraphrase merely omitted it. No change.
- **OED link (s7).** Opened as printed. It 302-redirects to an OUP subscription
  login (`oup2-idp.sams-sigma.com`); it does not land on the source. See the fix
  below.

`data-nb-kind` audit: all nine correct. s1 (MW) and s6 (Gibbon) and s9 (Lamport)
are primaries that own their claims; s2/s3 (Britannica), s4/s5 (Wikipedia), and
s8 (CJR) are correctly secondary. s7 (OED) is a legitimate primary owner of the
attestation date even though it is gated.

**The OED-vs-CJR citation question (resolved by direct edit).** The 1937
figurative attestation cited only s7 (OED). Per the citation standard, a link
must land on the source; an endpoint that fails whoever clicks it does not
qualify, and the researcher never read the OED directly (it is gated). CJR (s8)
carries the OED's exact 1937 wording verbatim, is reader-resolvable, and is
already a listed source. Rather than drop the OED — which owns the attestation
and which the writer brief directs citing "per the OED" — I dual-cited: s7
credits the primary owner, s8 gives the reader a link that lands on the verbatim
text. This satisfies both primary-source preference and reader resolvability
without orphaning a source or forcing a renumber. Source count unchanged (9).

## Cut

The piece runs lean already; the cut targeted two tells.

- **Self-grading.** "The sentence needs no gloss," planted right after the
  Bloomberg quote, tells the reader how to read the quote and grades the
  example. The voice guide bans flagging the irony and the house standard bans
  self-reference. It also contradicted itself: the next two sentences then
  glossed. Cut.
- **Contrast density.** The piece leans on the earned "not-X" contrast three
  times: "points first at a place, not a person" (the eponym correction — the
  brief's own angle), "The debate is not merely hard. It is tangled…," and the
  conclusion "Byzantine does not just mean complicated. It marks complexity that
  looks deliberate…." The standard's ceiling is two. The middle one was the most
  redundant with the conclusion, so I cut "The debate is not merely hard.,"
  leaving "It is tangled in a way that looks built to obscure" as the plain
  present-sense landing on the live example (voice-guide move 4). The eponym
  contrast and the conclusion contrast survive, both genuinely earned.

No prompt leakage: "place-adjective" is descriptive, not a planning label lifted
from the brief; no sentence claims the article fulfilled its assignment. Grammar
and punctuation are clean throughout — no run-ons, no semicolon chains, no
em-dash reflex; the dek is two independent clauses correctly joined by ", and."

**Formula / contradiction check** against the named recent library (silhouette,
bowdlerize, quisling, luddite, hysteresis, zugzwang, shibboleth, palimpsest):

- Headline avoids the flagged molds. It is not "Named for the wrong X"
  (bowdlerize) and not "coined it in YEAR for…"; it commits to a defended claim
  in subject–verb–surprise order and does not restate the etymology.
- Dek is not a stamped shape (no semicolon reversal, no suspended question, no
  three-clause comma triad). One soft note: it attributes the "byword for
  tangled, devious complexity" to Enlightenment historians, while the body more
  carefully separates the 18th-century reputation from the 1937 figurative
  attestation. It reads as a fair compression of the thesis, not a falsehood, so
  I left it; flagged below as non-blocking.
- Headings mostly fresh and in the piece's own nouns. One lands in a recurring
  series family: "What a byzantine debate is now" echoes bowdlerize's "What it
  means to bowdlerize now" and shibboleth's "What Counts as a Shibboleth Now."
  It still does real work and names its step, so I did not impose an editor
  rewrite; flagged below as non-blocking for the writer.

Furniture: the single Gibbon note-block is deliberate emphasis of the primary
sentence that seeds the whole pejorative turn — a close reading, not decoration.
It earns its place. No component reads like a stacked block. No missing furniture
opportunity; the word template leads with the card and the story is prose-shaped.

## Reader

Read straight through as the paper's declared reader, the piece gives what no
single source gives: the collision of the empire's Roman self-identity, a 1557
librarian's label, an 18th-century contempt, a 1937 first attestation, and a
2015 legislature, assembled into a claim about *which* complexity the word marks.
That synthesis matches the original-work sentence in the handoff and appears
nowhere in the sources in that form. The prose sits closer to the voice-guide
exemplars (the gerrymander/Forsyth compression, dry adjacency, time as spine)
than to a median summary: it telescopes the dead stretches, slows at the one
hinge ("Out of that reputation … English took a lowercase adjective"), and lands
the present sense on a real quoted use. The headline, reread as the largest
claim, is true and defended.
