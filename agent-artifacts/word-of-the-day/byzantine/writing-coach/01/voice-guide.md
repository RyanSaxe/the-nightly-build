# Voice guide: word-of-the-day/byzantine (01)

## How to write this piece

The house baseline is calm technical exposition that builds a concept before
the sentence that needs it. Keep the precision and the plain sentence, but
raise the tempo. This is the paper's smallest read, and it is a story with a
cast and a clock told in under 800 words: it should read the way a fluent,
well-read friend tells you where a word you use every week actually came from —
quick, exact, quietly amused, never lingering. The one thing the default
register does not give this piece is velocity across time. Supply it.

The reader is fluent and impatient. Assume they know what an empire is and skip
nothing that changed the word. Trust them to hold a date without a recap. Never
explain the joke or flag the irony: set two documented facts next to each other
and let the reader feel the distance between them.

Moves that will change sentences here:

1. Use time as the spine. Order the beats by the clock, and let each one earn
   its place only by moving the word's meaning. A beat that is merely
   interesting history is the survey the brief forbids; cut it.
2. Telescope the dead stretches. Where the years between two turning points
   carry nothing the sense needs, cross them in one controlled sentence. Spend
   full sentences only on the moments where the meaning actually turns.
3. Find the hinge and slow down there. The piece turns once, when the proper
   noun becomes the lowercase adjective. Give that turn its own clear sentence
   and let the reader feel the demotion happen.
4. Land the present sense on a concrete instance, in the present tense, on a
   real quoted use — not a gloss of how people "tend to" use it. Pin the modern
   meaning to something specific enough to picture.
5. Keep contested claims contested without slowing down. Where the record is a
   later coinage, an inferred first attestation, or a reputation historians
   built rather than a fact they found, name who and when and how firmly in a
   clause, then move on. Being exact about what is uncertain is part of the
   entertainment, not a brake on it.
6. Handle any specialist aside as a glance. If a technical second life of the
   word is worth one beat, give it one beat and return to the spine. It rewards
   the reader who catches it and costs the reader who does not nothing.

## Licenses

form: dry, situational humor — the amused undertone, never a joke laid on top
move: The exemplars raise a smile by placing documented facts in telling
      adjacency (a revered physician's name that parses as "horse-power"; an
      empire that called itself Roman, renamed centuries later by a scholar at
      his desk) and letting the reader make the connection. The comedy lives in
      which facts are chosen and the order they arrive in.
bar:  The amusement must survive deleting every adjective in the sentence — it
      has to sit inside a fact the sentence reports, so a reader who misses the
      humor still learns something true. No quip, no winking aside, no line that
      could be lifted out and reused elsewhere.

form: the telescoping sentence — one sentence that crosses a long span of time
move: Etymology writers compress centuries into a single clause so the few live
      moments get room to breathe. The long sentence stays in control: one
      subject moving through time, not a pile of clauses stacked on a comma.
bar:  It must summarize a stretch across which nothing changed the word's
      meaning, read cleanly on the first pass, and hand off to a short sentence
      at the moment the meaning does change. If a reader has to go back and
      re-read it, it packed ideas rather than time; break it or cut it.

form: the short declarative beat — a sentence of a few words set among longer ones
move: Marks a completed turn and resets the pace after a dense stretch, the way
      a held breath separates two movements. It punctuates the narrative; it
      does not comment on it.
bar:  It must carry a fact or finish the turn the prior sentences set up. It may
      never announce that something matters, grade the argument, or serve as the
      piece's closing line — the template's last sentence is the earned
      conclusion about why the word still earns its keep, not a snap. At most one
      or two in a read this short.

## Anatoly Liberman, "Words, emotions, and the story of great"
Source: https://blog.oup.com/2024/07/words-emotions-and-the-story-of-great/
Craft:
- cadence: Flat three-word verdicts ("No one knows") set against long sentences
  thick with parentheticals; the rhythm swings between the two so the reader
  never settles into a drone.
- argument: Accumulates near-relatives that look connected and are not, letting
  the pattern raise a question he then declines to over-answer.
- evidence: Historical spellings, cognates, and the OED, each named and dated
  but pressed only as far as it genuinely reaches.
- stance: A working etymologist who would rather show the seams than paper over
  them; sure about what is known, plain about what is not.
- notice: Fixes on the small anomaly others walk past (an unexplained root
  vowel, three unrelated words that all open with the same two letters) and
  makes it the hook.
- diction: Technical terms sit beside plain ones ("baby talk"); the register
  never hardens into jargon or softens into chat.
- reader: A collaborative "we"; the reader is handed the puzzle, not read a
  finding.
- The move the axes miss: he makes uncertainty the payload. The unanswered
  question is the entertainment, not a gap to be hidden.

## Merriam-Webster, "gerrymander" (Words at Play, Political Putdowns)
Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/words-at-play/political-putdowns/gerrymander
Craft:
- cadence: A dense definitional opening, then a compact narrative that varies
  sentence length to keep a two-hundred-year-old episode moving.
- argument: One claim — a word minted from a governor's name and a lizard — and
  every sentence serves it; nothing extra is admitted.
- evidence: A dated origin (1812, Governor Gerry) and a dated primary quotation
  (an 1892 newspaper) bracket the word's life with no survey in between.
- stance: Wry and unhurried; treats a political coinage as a small comedy
  without editorializing about the politics.
- notice: Seizes the one vivid physical detail (the district's salamander
  shape) and lets it carry the entire etymology.
- diction: Plain and concrete, no ornament; the wit is entirely in the facts
  selected.
- reader: Assumes an adult who wants the story fast — no throat-clearing, no
  recap of what the word means today.
- The move the axes miss: compression. A person, a year, a shape, a coined
  word, and the reader holds the whole history in four sentences.

## Mark Forsyth, "Hypocrites and Horse-Power" (Inky Fool)
Source: https://blog.inkyfool.com/2020/01/hypocrites-and-horse-power.html
Craft:
- cadence: Very short sentences dropped among longer explanatory ones; the pace
  comes from the gaps as much as the words.
- argument: Runs a small investigation — a hunch, a check, a verdict — so the
  etymology arrives as a discovery rather than a fact sheet.
- evidence: Roots given plainly, alongside a live skepticism about whether the
  tidy story is even true; he tests his own claim in front of the reader.
- stance: Delighted and mock-serious, enjoying the absurdity of the finding
  without inflating it into significance.
- notice: Hunts the juxtaposition that turns a dead root into a joke (the Father
  of Medicine reduced to "Dr Horsepower").
- diction: Learned but unbuttoned; mock-formal phrasing is the humor's delivery
  vehicle, not decoration.
- reader: Direct and intimate, brought inside the process including the false
  starts.
- The move the axes miss: the humor is adjacency, never an added joke — the
  laugh is two true facts standing next to each other.
