# Commission: word-of-the-day/serendipity

## Assignment
Cover **serendipity**: the faculty of making fortunate discoveries by accident
combined with the sagacity to recognize them. Its documented origin is a single
datable coinage with a named person and a story, it names a recognizable
pattern, and it stays useful far beyond where it began. That is the benchmark
this series wants, not the specific word sphexish.

(This replaces tonight's first word choice, "shibboleth", which the proof
correctly blocked as already published on 2026-07-31. serendipity is confirmed
unpublished.)

## The story to tell
- Horace Walpole coined the word in a letter to Horace Mann dated 28 January
  1754. He built it from "The Three Princes of Serendip", a fairy tale whose
  heroes "were always making discoveries, by accidents and sagacity, of things
  they were not in quest of." Serendip is an old name for Ceylon (Sri Lanka).
  Anchor the card's definition to an authoritative dictionary (OED, which
  credits Walpole; or Merriam-Webster) and the coinage to Walpole's own letter.
- Trace the meaning's development: rare for its first century, then taken up in
  science and popular usage in the twentieth. Robert K. Merton and Elinor Barber
  traced the word's own social history in "The Travels and Adventures of
  Serendipity" — a documented scholarly source for how the sense spread and
  drifted.
- Keep Walpole's own emphasis: the discovery takes sagacity, not mere luck. That
  is the distinction the word preserves and the one careless present use erases
  ("serendipity" flattened to "a happy accident"). Ground the present sense in
  real, cited modern usage, and show the sagacity distinction with an example.
- The last section lands why the word still earns a place: it names the prepared
  mind's part in an accidental find, which a plainer word for luck cannot.
