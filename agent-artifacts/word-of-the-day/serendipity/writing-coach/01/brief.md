# Writing-coach brief: word-of-the-day/serendipity

## What this piece has to avoid
The commission is explicit that the fairy tale and Serendip are the
documented story itself, not a myth to correct, so the draft never sets up
a "you'd think X, but actually Y" pivot around the etymology. Also ruled
out: a "coined for" opening move, a generalization-then-negation opener, a
dek in the "X, and the word that Y" mold, and a closer built on a
two-sentence, two-noun parallel-contrast aphorism with an abstract-noun
final heading.

## The spine
Walpole's own letter supplies the piece's argument, not just its origin:
"no discovery of a thing you are looking for comes under this
description." That sentence is the whole case for why "serendipity" is not
a fancy synonym for "lucky" — it structurally excludes finding what you
were already hunting. The draft is built to let that line do the
definitional work, then prove it twice: once inside the fairy tale (the
princes' clue-reading), once in a real 1928 laboratory (Fleming), before
landing on present-day usage that keeps splitting into the two halves
Walpole fused.

## Section shape agreed with the writer
1. Definition card — one sentence, Merriam-Webster.
2. The Letter That Named an Accident — the 1754 letter, quoted, plus the
   camel/mule detail and Walpole's own exclusion clause.
3. Two Centuries Before the Word Caught On — Merton & Barber on the word's
   slow adoption and its mid-20th-century "boom."
4. What Fleming Almost Threw Away — the sagacity-in-practice example the
   commission asked for.
5. 'Lucky' Doesn't Cover the Sagacity Half — present usage, landing.

Five sections total (orientation counts as the first), inside the 2-5
band, matching the shape used for the previous (blocked) shibboleth
assignment tonight.
