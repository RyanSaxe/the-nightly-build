# Evidence: word-of-the-day/serendipity

## S1 — Merriam-Webster, "serendipity"
https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/serendipity

- Definition used on the card: "The ability to find valuable or agreeable
  things not sought for," rendered as one plain sentence.
- Etymology: from "Serendip," a variant of Sarandīb, the Persian/Arabic
  name for Sri Lanka (Ceylon), plus "-ity"; Walpole is credited as coiner,
  drawing on "The Three Princes of Serendip."
- First known use: 1754 — the same year, and the same document, as
  Walpole's letter to Mann, used to support "no earlier or wider use is on
  record."
- Pronunciation: /ˌser-ən-ˈdi-pə-tē/.

## S2 — Merton & Barber, "The Origins of Serendipity" (ch. 1 of *The
Travels and Adventures of Serendipity*, Princeton University Press)
https://assets.press.princeton.edu/chapters/s7576.pdf
(fetched as a binary PDF; text extracted locally with `pypdf` since
WebFetch's summarizer could not parse the PDF stream directly)

- Reproduces Walpole's 28 January 1754 letter to Horace Mann verbatim,
  with citation "Letter to Mann, 28 January 1754, in Walpole's
  Correspondence, vol. 20, pp. 407-411." The letter recounts a "critical
  discovery" about the Capello coat of arms (occasioned by a Vasari
  portrait Mann had sent), made via a book-dipping habit Walpole calls
  "the sortes Walpolianae."
- Direct quotes used in the draft, all verbatim from the extracted text:
  "This discovery, indeed, is almost of that kind which I call
  Serendipity, a very expressive word..."; "I once read a silly fairy
  tale, called the three Princes of Serendip: as their Highnesses
  travelled, they were always making discoveries, by accidents and
  sagacity, of things which they were not in quest of"; "for you must
  observe that no discovery of a thing you are looking for comes under
  this description."
- The chapter's own commentary confirms Walpole misremembered the fairy
  tale's camel as a mule ("his memory had transformed the camel of the
  original story into a mule"), and separately notes "the boom serendipity
  was to have in the middle of the twentieth century," used to support the
  claim that wide adoption came roughly two centuries after the coinage.

## S3 — Encyclopædia Britannica, "Alexander Fleming"
https://www.britannica.com/biography/Alexander-Fleming

- Supports: Fleming returned from holiday on 3 September 1928 to find a
  culture plate contaminated by mold; the mold had cleared a ring in the
  surrounding bacterial growth; because Fleming was already investigating
  antibacterial substances, he recognized the significance instead of
  discarding the ruined plate, and went on to identify and name
  penicillin.
- Used as the modern, real-world illustration of "sagacity, not mere
  luck," as the commission asked for.
