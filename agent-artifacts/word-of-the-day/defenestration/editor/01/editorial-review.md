# Editorial review: word-of-the-day/defenestration (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: the figurative "defenestration" survives because it keeps a distinction a
plain "removal" drops, a forcible, public, resisted ousting, and the piece
grounds that in the resignation/dismissal/defenestration contrast and the 2022
Johnson use. The piece stands on four claims: the word was coined for the 1618
Prague event and first attested in English a year later; three men were thrown
and all survived, with the survival argued over on confessional lines; the act
had a local precedent in the 1419 Hussite defenestration; and the modern
figurative sense, now the more common one, is used for a violent ousting from
office.

I opened all eight citation hrefs as printed. Every one resolves to the source
itself, not to an endpoint or a search page. I checked each load-bearing claim
against the source it cites.

- Definition card and etymology (s1, Merriam-Webster, read): the two senses in
  order, "First Known Use: 1619," and "de- + Latin fenestra window" all match
  the live entry. Card holds one plain cited sentence. Good.
- 1618 event, names, the Marian rescue, the drop (s2, translated *Documenta
  Bohemica*): Martinicz, Slawata, and secretary Fabricius thrown into the moat
  "thirty cubits below," the Virgin Mary catching Martinicz in her cloak, all
  three surviving with slight injuries. All verified. The 1619 OED noun citation
  "the defenestration of the Counsailours," dated 10 Sept 1619, is present on the
  Grammarphobia page (s6) exactly as quoted; the piece correctly reaches the OED
  through Grammarphobia and never cites the OED as if opened. The "most often
  used" figurative-sense claim is supported by s7's "What it means" line
  ("Defenestration is most often used to refer to a usually swift dismissal or
  expulsion"). The Johnson quote (s8) is verbatim on the page; author, publisher,
  and 14 July 2022 date check out. The figurative political sense is left a
  twentieth-century drift with no pinned year, as the record requires.

Three breaks, all fixed directly, and one break routed:

- **Fabricated verbatim quote (s4).** The draft printed, inside quotation marks
  attributed to Quinion, "accounts differ on whether divine intervention or a
  manure heap saved them." That sentence is not on Quinion's page. The page says
  Catholic writers credited the Virgin Mary and "Protestant ones argued that they
  fortuitously landed on a heap of manure," presenting both without deciding. The
  substance the article uses is fully supported; only the quoted string was
  false. I removed the fabricated quotation and rendered Quinion's neutral
  presentation accurately, which also pays off the section's own opener ("the
  argument fell along the same line as the war"). Note for the researcher: the
  evidence record's s4 entry carries this same non-verbatim "quote," so the error
  originated upstream of the writer.
- **Height miscited to Quinion (s4).** The draft attributed "the seventy feet of
  later popular tellings" to s4, but Quinion states the fall was "thirty cubits
  (13 metres or 45 feet)," agreeing with the contemporary account, not seventy.
  The ~70 ft figure reached the record only through secondary summaries the
  researcher did not read firsthand and has no citable source. I cut the
  unsourced seventy-feet figure and stated the well-sourced drop, thirty cubits /
  about forty-five feet, citing s2 for the contemporary account and s4 for
  Quinion recording the same figure. The "the fall was long, and nobody died"
  payoff is preserved.
- **Dek undercount.** The dek read "Two imperial regents thrown from Prague
  Castle in 1618 survived the fall," but three men were thrown (two regents and
  their secretary) and all three survived, as the article's own opening states
  ("three men went out of one together"). A reader of the dek alone came away
  with the wrong count. I changed it to "Three men thrown from Prague Castle in
  1618 survived the fall" and matched the nb-meta `dek` to the rendered dekline.
- **1419 deaths unsupported by its cited source (routed).** The draft states the
  1419 Hussites "threw the councillors of the New Town Hall to their deaths" and
  that "Those men did not survive the crowd waiting below," cited to s5. The s5
  page (Britannica story) confirms the councillors were thrown from the New Town
  Hall windows and that the act sparked the Hussite Wars, but it establishes no
  deaths and gives no body count. No source read in the evidence record supports
  the 1419 killings; the "~10 killed" figure in the record comes from unread
  search summaries. The killed-vs-survived contrast is the commissioned
  1419/1618 comparison, so I did not quietly soften it. It goes to the researcher
  for a read source (below).

Display text checked descriptor by descriptor. Headline "A word coined for one
Prague window now unseats prime ministers": the coining and the figurative
present use are both defended; "prime ministers" (plural) is supported by the
cited Quinion essay, which names Thatcher and Hawke alongside the Johnson case
the body develops. Subheads each name a real step ("A window at Prague Castle,"
"Miracle or manure," "An act older than its name," "The violence the word
keeps") and skim to the argument. The `data-nb-kind` labels hold: s1/s2 primary
(a current authoritative dictionary; a translated contemporary account), s8
primary as a published instance of the usage, the encyclopedia and word-history
pages and the OED-conduit blog secondary. None hides a missing independent
source.

## Cut

The prose is already tight and I cut no slop; the piece survives the placeholder
test throughout. The Forsyth-style run in the closing section ("A resignation is
chosen. A dismissal is administrative. A defenestration is done to someone...")
depends entirely on its nouns and stays. Edge sentences all carry a fact or a
reasoning step: the survival section now ends on "the fall was long, and nobody
died," the etymology section on the first English use pointing back at the 1618
event, the final prose sentence on "the word that fit was the violent one." No
empty conclusions, no decorative-analysis trailing clauses, no self-reference,
no vague attribution.

Against the recent-pattern notes: the opener ("Most words for getting rid of a
person are polite. Defenestration is not.") is not the compressed "X did Y" lead
the desk has been repeating; the dek is not the "twist traces to a single source"
mold; the closing heading is neither "the manner outlived the place" nor the
"Never claimed, not merely departed" negative parallelism; the word is framed as
coined-for-an-event, not as an eponym. No formula carried forward.

Punctuation is plain. One em-dash (the aside on "the Prague councillors
themselves"), used as a real interruption; no semicolons or connective dashes.
My edits added none. No prompt leakage: the thesis echoes the commission's
"distinction a plainer word for removal loses," but the article earns it in its
own concrete terms (resignation/dismissal/defenestration, the Johnson use)
rather than lifting a planning label.

The furniture is the one `nb-note` carrying the 2022 quotation, a documented use
of the component. The writer flagged the ending-on-the-note as a taste call. It
lands: the voice guide explicitly steers the piece to close on a real quoted use
rather than a summarizing line, the prose conclusion ("the word that fit was the
violent one") is already stated before the note, and the quotation shows the
word doing exactly the forcible-ousting work the thesis claims. It reads as a
deliberate close, not a dropped final line. Kept.

## Reader

Read straight through, what the piece gives beyond its sources is the argument
none of them makes: that the figurative sense endures because it preserves a
forcible, public, resisted ousting that a neutral "removal" discards, shown
through the resignation/dismissal/defenestration ladder and the Johnson case.
The dictionaries give the two senses; the histories give the event; the piece
supplies the reason the modern word still earns its space. The original-work
sentence claims exactly this and the article delivers it. The prose sits with
the voice-guide exemplars (Quinion's refusal to overstate, Wilton's deadpan,
Forsyth's short-sentence turn), not a median summary. The headline holds as the
largest claim.

## Edits

- nb-meta `dek`: "Two imperial regents thrown..." changed to "Three men thrown..." to match the corrected count and the rendered dekline.
- Dekline: "Two imperial regents thrown from Prague Castle in 1618 survived the fall" changed to "Three men thrown from Prague Castle in 1618 survived the fall" (three were thrown, all survived).
- Survival paragraph: removed the fabricated Quinion quotation ("accounts differ on whether divine intervention or a manure heap saved them") and rewrote to Quinion's actual neutral presentation, attributing the manure-heap reading to Protestant writers and stating that Quinion sets the two readings side by side without settling them (s4).
- Height sentence: cut the unsourced/miscited "seventy feet of later popular tellings [s4]"; stated the drop as thirty cubits / about forty-five feet into the dry moat (s2) with Quinion recording the same figure (s4); preserved the "the fall was long, and nobody died" close.

- **writer:** After the researcher resolves the 1419 evidence, re-cite the death

## Decision

Revise: one sourcing failure remains, the 1419-deaths contrast is cited to a
source that establishes no deaths and no read source in the record supports it;
the researcher must supply that evidence or the writer cuts the claim. All other
issues were fixed directly.
