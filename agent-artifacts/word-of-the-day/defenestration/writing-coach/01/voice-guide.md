# Voice guide: word-of-the-day/defenestration

## How this piece should sound

This is a short word-history read about defenestration for a reader with a
mathematical and computing background who is widely read. The word carries a
vivid literal picture, a throwing out of a window, and a documented origin in a
specific event at Prague in 1618, with a local precedent in 1419 and a later
drift into the figurative sense of an abrupt removal from office. That reader
will enjoy the material without having its drama pointed out to them, so the
writing can stay calm and let the events carry their own weight, the way Wilton
lets a bribed bishop and a cross-country chase be funny without a line telling
the reader they are funny.

Where the record is firm, state it plainly and date it, as Quinion does with the
flat "It appears fully formed in 1577". Where a claim was argued over afterwards,
the same precision holds: set the competing accounts down in order and let a
documented detail, not an adjective, carry the judgment, the way Quinion places
Johnson's guess beside Ash's misprint. Defenestration's origin has its own
disputed edges, and the writing can report them without settling them for effect.

Tracing the development of the sense is the main work of the piece, from the
literal act named after Prague to the present use for an ousting from power.
Wilton's tawdry shows the move: each step is a thing that happened, laid down in
order, with a plain comparison to scale it. Follow the shift the way the
dictionaries record it, keeping the origin story and the current definition
apart, so the history explains the modern sense without being offered as proof
of it.

For rhythm, Forsyth is the model. A chain of plain declarative sentences can
carry an etymology, and a run of short ones can land a turn in it, as in "It is
mine no more. It is gone. The writing is done." Vary the length so the 1618
episode has room to quicken and the quieter stretches have room to slow. Keep the
humor inside the facts, so a shortened sentence is still reporting something.

When the piece shows the figurative sense, a real quoted use of defenestration in
political or corporate writing carries more than a sentence characterizing the
sense in the abstract, the way Wilton shows tawdry through a dated line from
Nehemiah Grew instead of restating the definition. A quotation of the word at
work also keeps the ending off a stamped summarizing line.

## Michael Quinion, "Curmudgeon"

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/qa-cur2.html

> "The problem, so far as your question is concerned, is that nobody has the slightest idea of where the word originated. It appears fully formed in 1577, as if out of the mists of the ages, with nothing to indicate where it comes from, or what its linguistic relatives are."

Quinion states the limit of the record directly: the word arrives on a fixed date
with nothing attached to it, and he says so rather than filling the gap with a
guess. The one ornament, "as if out of the mists of the ages", sits on a precise
year. He is visible in the refusal to invent an origin where the evidence stops.

> "The most famous is that of Dr Samuel Johnson; in his Dictionary of 1755 he quoted an unknown correspondent as suggesting that it came from the French coeur méchant (evil or malicious heart). This is now not considered at all likely. The note in Johnson's dictionary is best remembered for the howling blunder it caused John Ash to perpetrate in 1775 in his New and Complete Dictionary of the English Language in which he suggested it came from coeur, unknown, and méchant, correspondent."

He reports the false etymologies in order and lets the second one be funny on its
own: Ash misread Johnson's note and printed "coeur, unknown, and méchant,
correspondent". The joke is the documented error, quoted exactly, with no
adjective telling the reader it is absurd. Quinion's dryness is in leaving
"howling blunder" as his only comment and letting the misprint do the rest.

## Dave Wilton, "tawdry"

Source: https://www.wordorigins.org/big-list-entries/tawdry

> "Æthelthryth's tale is recounted by Bede in his eighth-century Ecclesiastical History and by Ælfric in his late tenth-century Lives of Saints. It is said that Æthelthryth took a vow of perpetual virginity and managed to get through two marriages without sleeping with either husband. Her first husband died before he could get her into the marital bed, and the second marriage was eventually annulled, much to the relief of the very frustrated young man, who had gone so far as attempting to bribe the local bishop to release her from her vow and who, when she fled his advances, had chased after her across England."

Wilton carries a saint's biography in plain sentences and gets its comedy from
what is on the record: two unconsummated marriages, a bishop offered a bribe, a
husband chasing his wife across England. The one evaluative phrase, "the very
frustrated young man", is earned by the facts just stated. Wilton is present in
the deadpan, naming Bede and Ælfric as his sources in the same breath.

> "In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, lace necklaces, known as St. Audrey's lace, were sold at the fair held each year on her feast day. And over time, the name of these lace necklaces became clipped to tawdry lace. The merchandise at this fair was the type of stuff that you would find at any tourist trap, cheap and gaudy, hence the fashion eventually became associated with finery of inferior quality."

He traces the sense change one documented step at a time: necklaces sold at the
fair, the name clipped to tawdry lace, the goods cheap enough that the word
turned pejorative. The modern comparison, "tourist trap", scales the point
without straining for it. Each link is a thing that happened, so the drift reads
as history rather than assertion.

## Mark Forsyth, "Releasing, Relaxing and Relishing Rhyme and Reason"

Source: https://blog.inkyfool.com/2025/10/releasing-relaxing-and-relishing-rhyme.html

> "The English word release comes from the French relasser, which meant something like relinquish, abandon, leave behind. This seems strangely appropriate, as it really is what we do with tigers and orcas and books about the history of English poetry. It is mine no more. It is gone. The writing is done."

Forsyth moves from the French source to the English word in one plain sentence,
then drops into three short ones: "It is mine no more. It is gone. The writing is
done." The change of length is the whole effect. He is visible in the
willingness to let a serious rhythm follow a joke about releasing orcas.

> "And relaxare gave us a word for that too. The French took the word twice, and the second time they made it into relais, which is in the name of various restaurants like Le Relais de Venise, which means the taste of Venice. But relais sounded too French, so we changed it to relish. Then we made it a verb."

He walks a real chain, relaxare to relais to relish, in short declaratives, and
lands the turn on a documented fact stated flatly: "relais sounded too French, so
we changed it to relish. Then we made it a verb." The humor is in the plainness,
not in a punchline hung off the end of it.
