# Commission: word-of-the-day/defenestration

## The word
Defenestration: the act of throwing someone or something out of a window, and
by extension the abrupt, forcible removal of a person from office or power. The
word is unusual, its literal sense is vivid, and its figurative sense is in
working use in political and corporate writing. It clears the series bar because
its documented origin is a real historical episode with named people and a
consequence out of proportion to the act.

## The story to reconstruct
The word was coined for a specific event: the throwing of the Catholic imperial
regents from a window of Prague Castle on 23 May 1618, the spark historians
attach to the Thirty Years' War. Name the men thrown and the detail that they
survived the fall, which is part of why the episode was remembered and argued
over. Set that 1618 event against the earlier Hussite defenestration of 1419,
so the reader sees the act had a local precedent before it had a Latin-derived
English name. Then trace the sense development the dictionaries record: from the
literal act, named after Prague, to the present figurative use for an abrupt
ousting from power.

The article's own work is to keep the figurative sense honest against the
literal one: what distinction the word still preserves that a plainer word for
"removal" loses. That is the payoff the last section should land, grounded in a
real modern use, not asserted.

## Boundaries
- Etymology is history, not proof of the present meaning. The definition is what
  authoritative dictionaries record now; the origin story explains how it got
  there. Keep the two separate, as the series prompt requires.
- The definition card carries one plain cited sentence. The word, not the
  experience of looking it up, is the subject.

## Sources
Template floor is 4 sources. Cite an authoritative dictionary (the OED for the
etymology, first attestation, and the order the senses arrived; a second current
dictionary for the working definition) and reputable historical accounts of the
Prague defenestrations that name the participants and the survival. Ground at
least one present-day figurative use in a real published example. Prefer the
record that owns each claim over a retelling.

## Habits to break (recent word desk)
The recent words are eponyms (maverick, boondoggle, gerrymander, quisling,
bowdlerize); defenestration is not, so it should not be framed as one. Two
recent closers are worth not copying: the negative-parallelism landing ("Never
claimed, not merely departed") and the "the manner outlived the place" turn.
Recent deks lean on a "the twist traces to a single source" reveal; find this
piece's own dek. Do not open with the recurring "X answered Y with one Z" shape.
