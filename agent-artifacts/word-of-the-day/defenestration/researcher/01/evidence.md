# Evidence record: word-of-the-day/defenestration (01)

The record supports every commissioned claim with a source that was opened and
read. The word's current definition and two senses (literal act; figurative
removal from office) are firmly held by a current authoritative dictionary
(Merriam-Webster, read directly) and corroborated for the OED's data through a
source that quotes the OED at length. The 1618 Prague event, its named victims
and their titles, the survival, and the disputed reason for it are held by a
contemporary primary account (a translated eyewitness/Catholic narrative in
*Documenta Bohemica*) plus reputable secondary histories. The 1419 precedent and
a real, dated present-day figurative use (Boris Johnson, 2022) are both sourced.

The record is thin in two places, both recorded honestly below. First, the OED
entry itself is paywalled and could not be read firsthand; the OED's first
attestation (1619) and sense order reach this record through Merriam-Webster
(which gives the same 1619 first-use date directly) and through the Grammarphobia
blog, which quotes the OED's dated citations. Second, sources disagree on the
height of the fall and on when the *figurative political* sense hardened, and one
word-historian (Quinion) makes a first-attestation claim that contradicts the
OED. These are laid out under Contradictions. Nothing found undermines the
commissioned angle; the survival dispute and the 1419-vs-1618 contrast both
strengthen it.

## Contradictions

- **Height of the fall.** The primary account says "thirty cubits" (~45 ft / ~14
  m); Quinion says "about 45 feet," agreeing. Popular secondary accounts (e.g.
  Wikipedia and All That's Interesting, seen only in search summaries, not read)
  give ~21 m / ~70 ft. Report the drop as roughly 45–70 feet into the castle moat
  and attribute the "thirty cubits" figure to the contemporary account rather than
  asserting a single precise height.

- **Why they survived.** This is the live dispute the commission wants. The
  contemporary Catholic account (*Documenta Bohemica*, primary) credits the Virgin
  Mary and divine grace. The naturalistic reading, stated as the competing account
  by Quinion and echoed by etymonline, is that they landed on a manure/refuse
  ("trash") heap in the moat that broke the fall; a Protestant variant (in
  secondary summaries not read firsthand) attributes it to a soft "mist." The
  honest framing: contemporaries and later writers split between a miracle and a
  dung heap. Do not state either as settled fact.

- **First attestation of the word.** The OED (via MW's 1619 first-use date and
  Grammarphobia's quoted 10 Sept 1619 citation) places the earliest English use in
  1619, one year after the event. Quinion instead claims the first English use of
  the term is a 1743 account, with the "defenestration of Prague" label appearing
  only from the 1850s. These cannot both be the first use of the word. The OED is
  the authority for first attestation; Quinion is likely describing the naming of
  the *event* as a set phrase, not the word's first appearance. Prefer 1619, and do
  not repeat Quinion's 1743 as the word's origin.

- **When the figurative political sense arrived.** Sources vary: the OED marks a
  colloquial "remove from power" sense without a single clean date (an early
  figurative verb use is 1917, about discarding a theory, not ousting a person);
  Quinion dates the "sacking from office" sense to the early 1990s; other usage
  writers cite ~1955. Treat the figurative political sense as a 20th-century
  development without pinning it to one year the sources do not agree on.

- **Event date.** OED, MW, Britannica, and the primary account all give 23 May
  1618. Only etymonline gives "May 21, 1618." Use 23 May 1618.
