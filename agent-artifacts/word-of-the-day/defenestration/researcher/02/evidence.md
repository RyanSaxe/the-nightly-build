# Evidence record: word-of-the-day/defenestration (02)

This record preserves the full researcher/01 evidence and resolves the one
sourcing failure the editor/01 review routed back: the commissioned
1419-killed / 1618-survived contrast now carries a source that was actually
opened and read. A curated secondary encyclopedia (New World Encyclopedia, read
directly) establishes firsthand that the 1419 First Defenestration killed seven
members of the city council, and states the mechanism — the councillors were
thrown "onto the spears of the armed congregation below," i.e. finished by the
crowd, not by the fall. A scholarly essay (Public Domain Review, read directly)
independently establishes the count of those thrown (three municipal consuls and
seven citizens = ten) but does not state deaths. The 1419-deaths claim is now
sourced; the exact victim count varies between sources and is recorded as a
range under Contradictions.

Two corrections to the researcher/01 record are folded in. First, the editor/01
review found that the s4 (Quinion / World Wide Words) entry carried a
non-verbatim string presented as a quotation ("accounts differ on whether divine
intervention or a manure heap saved them"). That string does not appear on
Quinion's page — reconfirmed by reopening the page for this invocation — so it is
removed and replaced with the exact sentence that is on the page. Second, the
1419 figure in Numbers, previously flagged as unverified against any read source,
is now grounded.

Everything else in the researcher/01 record was rechecked against the review and
is preserved. Nothing found undermines the commissioned angle; the now-sourced
1419 deaths strengthen the killed-vs-survived contrast the piece turns on.

## Contradictions

- **1419 victim count.** Sources agree the councillors were thrown and killed but
  differ on the number. New World Encyclopedia (read) says the event "involved the
  killing of seven members of the city council." The Public Domain Review essay
  (read) counts ten thrown — "three municipal consuls and seven citizens." Popular
  tellings seen only in search summaries add a fuller list (a mayor, a judge's
  aide, elders, a serf) also totalling about ten thrown. The honest framing: about
  ten people were thrown from the New Town Hall and killed by the crowd below, with
  the council members among them; roughly seven of them councillors. Attribute the
  "seven councillors killed" figure to New World Encyclopedia and the "three
  consuls and seven citizens thrown" figure to the Public Domain Review; do not
  assert a single precise death toll as settled. What is firmly sourced and is the
  load-bearing point for the article is qualitative: in 1419 those thrown were
  killed (finished by the armed crowd below); in 1618 all three lived.

- **Who led the 1419 crowd.** New World Encyclopedia, Britannica (story page), and
  the weight of sources name the Hussite priest Jan Želivský. One weaker local
  source (Prague Morning, see Discarded) misattributes leadership to the military
  commander Jan Žižka. Use Jan Želivský.

- **Height of the 1618 fall.** The primary account says "thirty cubits" (~45 ft /
  ~14 m); Quinion agrees at "thirty cubits (13 metres or 45 feet)." The ~70 ft /
  ~21 m figure appears only in secondary summaries not read firsthand and has no
  citable source; the editor/01 cut it from the draft. Report the drop as thirty
  cubits / about forty-five feet into the moat, attributed to the contemporary
  account (and to Quinion recording the same figure).

- **Why the 1618 men survived.** The live dispute the commission wants. The
  contemporary Catholic account (*Documenta Bohemica*, primary) credits the Virgin
  Mary and divine grace. Quinion sets that beside the Protestant reading that the
  men "fortuitously landed on a heap of manure," without deciding. Frame it as
  contemporaries split between a miracle and a dung heap; do not state either as
  settled fact. Do NOT reuse the researcher/01 fabricated quotation — see the s4
  Note.

- **First attestation of the word.** The OED (via MW's 1619 first-use date and
  Grammarphobia's quoted 10 Sept 1619 citation) places the earliest English use in
  1619, one year after the event. Quinion instead claims the first English use is a
  1743 account, with the "defenestration of Prague" label appearing only from the
  1850s. These cannot both be first use. The OED is the authority for first
  attestation; Quinion is likely describing the naming of the *event* as a set
  phrase. Prefer 1619; do not repeat 1743 as the word's origin.

- **When the figurative political sense arrived.** Sources vary: the OED marks a
  colloquial "remove from power" sense without a single clean date (an early
  figurative verb use is 1917, about discarding a theory, not ousting a person);
  Quinion dates the "sacking from office" sense to the early 1990s; other usage
  writers cite ~1955. Treat it as a 20th-century development without pinning one
  year.

- **1618 event date.** OED, MW, Britannica, and the primary account all give 23 May
  1618. Only etymonline gives "May 21, 1618." Use 23 May 1618.
