# Evidence: word-of-the-day/hysteresis (researcher/01)

The coinage is now pinned to a primary source I read in full: J. A. Ewing's own
paper, "On the Production of Transient Electric Currents in Iron and Steel
Conductors by Twisting them when Magnetised or by Magnetising them when
Twisted," received by the Royal Society September 7, 1881, and printed in
*Proceedings of the Royal Society of London*, vol. 33, pp. 21-23. On page 22,
Ewing writes: "the author now gives the name Hysterésis (ὑστέρησις, from
ὑστερέω, to be behind)." This is stronger than the brief's working assumption
in one respect and complicates it in another. Stronger: the person, year, and
journal are now confirmed against the actual page, not a retelling. Complicating:
the coining sentence sits inside a paper about *twisted, magnetised wires*
(a magnetostriction/Wiedemann-effect experiment on "polarisation"), not the
classic iron-magnetization B-H loop the popular story (and the brief) leans
on — Ewing explicitly says he is reusing a name he'd already floated for a lag
he'd described in an earlier paper on thermoelectricity under stress. The
B-H hysteresis loop that everyone now pictures is the subject of his *later*
papers (1883, 1885), not the coining paper itself. Both the Greek etymology
and the standing definition are covered by dictionaries and by Ewing's own
1893 textbook. The economics generalization is anchored directly to the
Blanchard & Summers NBER working paper. The OED itself — the source the brief
names first — could not be opened (403, twice); Merriam-Webster stands in as
the authoritative-dictionary source actually read, and it disagrees with the
primary paper on the exact year (1882 vs. 1881), which is recorded below as
the piece's central factual tension. Coverage is thin only on the space between
the 1881 coining paper and the 1885 paper that made "magnetic hysteresis" the
famous sense — I have the endpoints and Ewing's own back-reference linking them,
not a blow-by-blow of the intervening two years.

## Contradictions

- **The exact year of coinage is genuinely contested across source types.**
  The primary paper I read in full (Proc. Roy. Soc. vol. 33) was received
  September 7, 1881, and names the term on p. 22 within a volume dated
  November 17, 1881-March 30, 1882 — so 1881 is what the coining document
  itself supports. Merriam-Webster, an authoritative modern dictionary,
  dates "First Known Use" to 1882. I could not open the OED directly (see
  Discarded) to see whether it agrees with Merriam-Webster or with the
  primary text; secondhand paraphrases of the OED entry surfaced in search
  results also said 1882, but I am not treating that as read evidence.
  The likely explanation is a cataloguing convention — the Royal Society
  volume spans two calendar years and dictionaries may index by the
  volume's later year or by a different, later paper of Ewing's — but the
  piece should say 1881 on the strength of the primary source and flag that
  standard dictionaries index the word to 1882.
- **"Coined while studying magnetization of iron" is an oversimplification.**
  The word's actual first appearance, in Ewing's own hand, is inside a paper
  about *twisted, magnetised wires* — a lag in something Ewing calls
  "polarisation," related to but distinct from the classic B-H
  magnetization loop most retellings picture. Ewing says explicitly he is
  reapplying a name from an even earlier paper on the thermoelectric
  behavior of stressed metals. The famous "magnetic hysteresis" sense — the
  loop, the iron, the energy lost as heat — is worked out fully in Ewing's
  1885 Philosophical Transactions paper (vol. 176, p. 524), confirmed
  independently by the 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica and by Ewing's own
  1893 textbook footnote, which cites both papers together. The angle
  should distinguish "coined the word" (1881, torsion/polarisation) from
  "fixed the word's now-standard magnetic sense" (1885, the iron
  magnetization loop) rather than collapsing them into one moment.
- **Priority for discovering the phenomenon (as opposed to naming it) is
  disputed.** Bhargava's history credits Emil Warburg with associating
  hysteresis with magnetic materials "simultaneously" with Ewing, "about
  the year 1879" — two years before Ewing's coining paper. The DSB
  biography confirms that Ewing's own 1885 paper acknowledged Warburg's
  independent discovery. Wikipedia (not used as a source in this record,
  but consulted while orienting the search) cites a 1946 biography of Ewing
  by Bates making the same point. This is a discovery-priority dispute, not
  a naming dispute: no source disputes that Ewing coined the *word*.
- No disagreement was found on the Greek root chain itself (ὑστέρησις from
  ὑστερέω from ὕστερος) — Ewing's own gloss ("to be behind"/"to lag
  behind") and the dictionaries' gloss ("shortcoming"/"to be late, fall
  short") differ only in translation emphasis, not in the underlying Greek.

## Answers to the brief's specific questions

- **Who coined "hysteresis," when, in what context:** James Alfred Ewing,
  in a paper received by the Royal Society September 7, 1881, and printed
  in *Proceedings of the Royal Society of London*, vol. 33, pp. 21-23. The
  context was a lag he observed in "polarisation" when a magnetised iron or
  steel wire is twisted — not, at the moment of coining, the classic iron
  B-H magnetization loop, though he explicitly reused a name he had already
  been using informally for a similar lag in stressed metals'
  thermoelectric behavior. The word's now-standard "magnetic hysteresis"
  (iron-magnetization loop) sense was fixed in his 1885 Philosophical
  Transactions paper, vol. 176, p. 524.
- **Greek root and literal sense:** ὑστέρησις (hysterésis), from ὑστερέω
  (hystereō), "to be behind" / "to lag behind" (Ewing's own gloss);
  dictionaries render the same root chain as "a coming short, a
  deficiency" / "to be late, fall short," ultimately from ὕστερος
  (hysteros), "later."
- **Standing definition to quote:** Merriam-Webster: "a slowing of an
  effect when the forces acting upon a body are changed (as if from
  viscosity or internal friction)."
- **Physics meaning, one sentence:** Britannica: "Lagging of the
  magnetization of a ferromagnetic material, such as iron, behind
  variations of the magnetizing field" — because the material's internal
  realignment does not track the applied field instantaneously, tracing
  different curves as the field rises and falls and enclosing a loop that
  represents energy lost as heat.
- **Modern generalization, precisely sourced:** Olivier Blanchard and
  Lawrence Summers, "Hysteresis and the European Unemployment Problem,"
  NBER Working Paper No. 1950 (June 1986): persistently elevated European
  unemployment reflects shocks that shift the equilibrium unemployment rate
  itself, via insider/outsider wage bargaining, rather than fading as
  standard theory would predict.

## Limitation to report

The single most important limitation: the OED, the brief's first-named
authority, was inaccessible (403 both times), so the coinage year rests on
a primary source (1881) that disagrees with the one authoritative
dictionary I could actually verify (Merriam-Webster, 1882) — a genuine,
unresolved discrepancy the piece should state rather than silently pick a
side on. A second, related limitation: I could not open Ewing's 1883
"preliminary notice" (Royal Society and JSTOR both blocked it), so the
throughline from the 1881 coining paper to the 1885 defining paper rests on
Ewing's own back-citation and on secondary accounts (DSB, 1911 Britannica),
not on reading the middle document myself.
