# Voice guide: word-of-the-day/hysteresis

Register: the house default (Hashimoto calm-and-precise, Olah/Weng patience),
compressed to the paper's smallest form. In 550-800 words there is no runway —
open on the physical fact of Ewing's coil and iron, not on the idea of
etymology. The reader relationship is a colleague being told a good, true
story at speed: you respect their time enough to cut the wind-up, and their
intelligence enough to give the real apparatus, the real date, the real
quote, instead of a smoothed-over paraphrase. Every sentence must survive
the question "does the next sentence need this one," because at this length
a sentence that only sets a mood is a sentence that pushed out a fact.

Two specific muscles this article needs that a longer article can afford to
warm up into: a definition line the reader could lift out of the piece and
hand to someone else, complete and unhedged; and a hinge sentence, short and
plain, that closes the origin story and opens the present-day sense without
letting the etymology pose as proof of the modern meaning.

## Licenses

```text
form: quoted diction from the coiner
move: Craddock, the Merriam-Webster desk, and Bernstein all let the
      historical actor's own word choice carry the surprise, instead of
      narrating it in adjectives. Deploys once, at the moment the coinage
      itself is the news — Ewing choosing "hysteresis" over a plainer
      English word, or explaining why the Greek fit.
bar:  the quoted phrase must be something you read in Ewing's own writing
      (or a source that quotes him directly), and it must do definitional
      or motivational work — show why he reached for that word — not
      decorate a sentence that already made its point.

form: one earned ironic turn on the word's afterlife
move: the Merriam-Webster "quark" piece closes its origin story on a single
      dry line ("Quark stuck apparently in part because it didn't sound
      scientific") that the preceding facts already built. It is observation,
      not a bolted-on kicker.
bar:  usable once, and only if the irony is a fact you can point to (a
      contrast the sourced history itself contains), not a line added for
      lift. If you have to invent the contrast, cut the sentence instead.

form: a short declarative hinge between story and present sense
move: Bernstein and Craddock both mark the turn from narrating the coinage
      to stating what the word now does with one plain, short sentence,
      set off by surrounding longer ones. It functions as connective
      tissue the reader can lean on, not as a punchline.
bar:  it must state a fact or a distinction the piece has already earned,
      not announce that a distinction is coming. If it could be cut without
      losing information, it is decoration wearing the shape of a hinge.
```

An empty license keeps the house default. Do not use a form this list
doesn't name — in particular, no first-person narrator's aside (Craddock's
closing "I wonder what changed" and Bernstein's memoir opening are moves
their outlets allow and this paper's self-reference ban does not).

## Recently used, do not reuse

- The "X means/names Y" headline claim (zugzwang, kayfabe). Find hysteresis's
  own headline shape; it does not have to state the payoff as an equation.
- The antedating / "coined by someone else" / "named for the wrong person"
  reversal that led several recent word-desk pieces. Hysteresis's real arc —
  a named physicist's deliberate, well-documented coinage that outgrew its
  field — does not need a myth to correct. Do not force one.
- The comma-triad and semicolon-reversal dek molds, and hedged contrast past
  one earned use in the body (house limit, worth restating at this length
  where the temptation to hedge the lag/delay distinction is real).

## Paul Craddock, "The word 'scientist' was coined in 1834, as a joke"
Source: https://paulcraddock.substack.com/p/the-word-scientist-was-coined-in
Craft:
- cadence: opens on one flat, surprising declarative sentence, then lets
  longer sentences carry the social history that explains it. Short
  sentences mark turns, not just endings.
- argument: the coinage is the whole spine — Whewell's joke, its slow,
  contested adoption, Faraday's and Huxley's resistance — argued as a
  professionalization story, not a list of trivia.
- evidence: named people, a specific review, a specific society meeting,
  quoted resistance from Faraday. Every claim has an actor and a date
  attached to it.
- stance: wry but not performing wit — the humor is in the documented facts
  (a joke word scientists refused for a century), not in narrator jokes.
- notice: notices the gap between a word's origin and its reception, and
  spends real space on the decades the word was disliked, which is the part
  a lesser piece would cut.
- diction: plain, concrete nouns for institutions and people. No hedging
  qualifiers around what is and isn't documented.
- reader: assumes curiosity, not expertise; never explains what "coined"
  means, explains instead why this particular coining was contested.
- the move the axes miss: keeps the origin story and the word's present
  standing as two visibly separate movements, connected but not conflated —
  exactly the discipline this brief needs between Ewing's coinage and the
  word's economics sense.

## Merriam-Webster Words at Play, "What Does 'Quark' Have to Do with
Finnegans Wake?"
Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/quark
Craft:
- cadence: alternates a short decisive sentence at each turning point
  ("The line struck him as appropriate...") with a longer sentence that
  supplies the mechanism.
- argument: builds toward a single point — a scientist's private, playful
  reading choice became permanent technical vocabulary — and stops once
  that point is made.
- evidence: quotes Gell-Mann's own account of finding the word and his own
  reasoning about pronunciation, sourced to his book, not paraphrased into
  vaguer prose.
- stance: treats the physics as settled and uninteresting next to the
  naming; doesn't re-derive quark theory, doesn't apologize for that choice.
- notice: notices that the word's technical definition and its origin story
  are doing different jobs, and gives each exactly the space it needs — the
  definition in one sentence, the story in the rest.
- diction: no filler transitions; sentences start on nouns and verbs, not
  on throat-clearing ("In the world of particle physics...").
- reader: assumes no physics background and no Joyce background, and
  defines each on first use in one clause, then moves on without returning
  to re-explain either.
- the move the axes miss: closes by naming one more scientist-coined word
  (googol) that took on a life of its own, widening the frame in a single
  sentence instead of a new section — a way to gesture at a word's larger
  pattern without writing the survey the brief warns against.

## Jeremy Bernstein, "An Entropic Story"
Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.3448 (PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1109.3448)
Craft:
- cadence: a physicist's sentences, built to carry one clean idea each, with
  the technical content never allowed to make a sentence do two jobs.
- argument: treats entropy as something made — a specific, deliberate
  linguistic choice by a working scientist solving a naming problem — the
  same posture this article needs toward "hysteresis."
- evidence: grounds the coinage in Clausius's own stated reasoning for
  wanting a word that echoed "energy," not in a generic "scientists began
  calling it..."
- stance: unhurried but never padded; a working physicist's confidence that
  the reader can follow the real mechanism if it's built in order.
- notice: notices that a coined technical word carries its coiner's reasons
  inside it, and treats recovering those reasons as the interesting part,
  ahead of the physics itself.
- diction: exact terms, no synonym reached for variety once a term is set.
- reader: assumes patience and some technical background, and rewards it by
  not re-simplifying material already established.
- the move the axes miss: narrates the coining as a problem the scientist
  had to solve (needing a name that signaled a precise kinship with an
  existing concept), which is the transferable model for narrating why
  Ewing needed a Greek word for magnetic lag rather than an English one.

## Self-test

A writer following only the house default would already write cleanly about
Ewing and the coil. What this guide adds: pull the coinage's own vocabulary
into the piece as evidence (the quoted-diction license), let one earned irony
close the origin movement instead of a stated moral, and mark the turn from
story to present sense with a short sentence that does real work rather than
a section break. Those three moves, plus refusing this desk's two recent
molds, are what should make this piece read as this piece and not as the
next word-of-the-day filling the same slot.
