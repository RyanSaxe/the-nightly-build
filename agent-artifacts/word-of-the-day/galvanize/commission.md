## The word and why it qualifies

"Galvanize": in its live figurative sense, to shock someone or something into
sudden action. It clears the desk's bar the way the sphexish benchmark does: its
origin is a specific, documented scientific episode with named people and a real
dispute, and the word stays useful far beyond the laboratory that produced it. The
eponym is Luigi Galvani, whose 1780s Bologna experiments made a dead frog's legs
twitch when touched by metal, which he read as evidence of an intrinsic "animal
electricity." Alessandro Volta disputed the explanation, arguing the electricity
came from the dissimilar metals, and that dispute led Volta to the electric pile
(the battery). Galvani's nephew Giovanni Aldini then staged public demonstrations
applying current to animal and human corpses, including the 1803 Newgate
demonstration on the body of executed prisoner George Forster, a spectacle that
fed the culture that produced Frankenstein. From that episode the verb passed into
electroplating ("galvanized" iron) and into the figurative sense of a sudden jolt
into motion.

## What the article must do

Tell the documented story of the eponym and trace how the meaning developed from
the science to the present figurative sense: Galvani's experiment and his claim;
Volta's counter-explanation and where it led; the word's coinage from Galvani's
name; and the path to today's "shock into action." Ground the present sense in
real, cited examples of use. Keep etymology as history: it explains where the
sense came from, and the present meaning is established from actual usage and an
authoritative dictionary definition, not from the origin story. Distinguish the
literal electroplating sense ("galvanized steel") from the live figurative one,
and note that the figurative "rouse to action" sense is what the piece is about.
Do not pad a short lexical read into a general history of electricity.
