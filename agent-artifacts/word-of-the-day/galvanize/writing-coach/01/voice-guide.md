# Voice guide: word-of-the-day/galvanize

## How this piece should sound

The story has two rival explanations in the room at once: Galvani reading the
twitching frog leg as evidence of animal electricity, Volta reading the same
leg as evidence of nothing more than two dissimilar metals in contact. Zimmer's
opening on "drone" is built the same way, naming the easy guess and then
splitting it into the two distinct mechanisms it could be, each stated as a
real candidate before either is judged. The frog leg deserves the same
treatment: both readings named and held as live positions before the piece
sides with one.

Popular tellings of the dispute tend to garble what Volta actually objected to.
Forsyth's fluke post has the matching move: he doesn't announce that the
dictionary's account is wrong, he sets the OED's own uncertain date next to an
earlier one and lets the gap make the correction. Wherever this piece
straightens out what the casual version gets backwards about the Galvani-Volta
quarrel, the correction should work the same way: the specific fact set beside
the specific misreading, not a sentence announcing that the truth is more
complicated.

Aldini's demonstrations at Newgate, on the body of a hanged man, are the
strongest material in the piece, and they don't need help. The Reagan-and-
Monroe turn in the Zimmer passage shows what flat, sequential reporting does
for a fact that's already strange enough: no adjective stands in front of it,
the sentences just proceed and the surprise arrives on its own. Aldini's stage
earns the same restraint, including whatever it lent the culture that produced
Frankenstein, which lands harder for being stated once, plainly, after the
demonstration itself is on the page.

Wherever a number survives into the piece, Liberman's habit in the swing post
is worth having: he states the big figure and then tells the reader in the next
breath exactly how much of it to trust, rather than either dropping the caveat
or hedging the whole sentence into mush. A decade of experiments, a year of
publication, an execution date, a first-attestation year for the figurative
sense — whichever of these the piece is confident about can be stated flatly,
and whichever it isn't can say so in the same specific way, without either
fact softening the other.

At the length this template allows, the close has room for one earned claim
about the word's present use, not a second pass through the history. Forsyth
ends "Fluke" on a first-person verdict sized to what his own digging supports.
Liberman ends the swing post on the two words from his own title doing their
ordinary, current work, with no sentence framing them as a close. Either shape
argues against a generic send-off: land on "galvanize" doing what it actually
does in a real sentence today, sized to what the piece has shown about why a
sudden external jolt is the right reading and a slow build isn't.

## Ben Zimmer, "How 'Drone' Got Off the Ground"

Source: https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wordroutes/how-drone-got-off-the-ground/

> "You might be able to guess that drone is derived from its namesake in the
> beehive: those stingless male bees that make no honey but fertilize the eggs
> of the queen. But how did the apiarist's drone find its way into
> aeronautics? Did it have to do with the buzz of the plane's engine, since
> drone has long been used to describe buzzy bee noises? Or was there another
> analogy at work, comparing a bee at the behest of its queen to an unmanned
> aircraft at the behest of its controller?"

Zimmer gives the easy guess first and then splits it into two distinct,
named mechanisms before answering either. The three questions in a row each
name a specific candidate rather than asking a vague "how did this happen,"
which is why the paragraph reads as a real puzzle rather than a rhetorical
one.

> "Denny's friend Ronald Reagan, then an Army publicist, assigned a
> photographer to take pictures of the women working on the assembly line.
> One of those women was named Norma Jeane Dougherty, and the photo shoot
> eventually led to a Hollywood career — under the name Marilyn Monroe."

The most quotable fact in the piece arrives in two flat sentences with no
comment attached to it. Zimmer never tells the reader this is a good story;
the surprise sits entirely in the sequence of facts, and the writing gets out
of the way of it.

> "Much like surge in 2006, it's not the word itself that is new and notable
> but the move from noun to transitive verb. The part-of-speech switch
> indicates that drone has entered a new stage in its long life cycle."

Closing on the word's present life, Zimmer reaches for one specific prior
case, sourced to a named linguist's post, instead of a general statement
about how language changes. The analysis is a comparison the reader could go
check, not a claim asking to be taken on faith.

## Mark Forsyth, "Fluke"

Source: https://blog.inkyfool.com/2011/02/fluke.html

> "The OED is a little more helpful. It says that fluke was originally a
> lucky shot in billiards, and suggests that it might have something to do
> with the Whitby dialect word fluke, meaning guess, although that wasn't
> recorded until 1876, so it's probably the other way round."

Forsyth reports the dictionary's own hedge and then quietly overturns it with
a single date. He never says the OED is wrong; he puts two years next to each
other and lets the reader do the subtraction himself.

> "Now, there are two possibilities here: the first is that the slang word
> fluke was already around in 1853 and the author wanted to give his
> character a funny name. This seems unlikely. It's hardly a good joke to use
> a word so obscure that it had never appeared in print before."

He states a hypothesis, rejects it, and gives the one-sentence reason before
moving to the alternative. The rejection is a judgment call about how jokes
work, made in his own voice, not something he outsources to a citation.

> "All of which convinces me that fluke, the chance, comes from Mr Fluke of
> Christ Church."

The piece ends on a first-person verdict instead of a recap of what was
found. "Convinces me" keeps the claim sized to what one blog post of amateur
research can actually support.

## Anatoly Liberman, "In full swing"

Source: https://blog.oup.com/2025/07/in-full-swing/

> "It is now believed that the human language emerged about 230,000 years
> ago. I am rather suspicious of exact numbers in this matter, but let us
> agree that the date is in principle realistic. As soon as people learned to
> produce the sounds needed for expressing their emotions, they began to say
> something like swap and swag."

Liberman states a huge figure and immediately tells the reader how much of it
to trust, before going on to use it for what it's good for. The doubt
attaches to the number specifically, not to the sentence as a whole, so the
claim that follows can still commit to something.

> "The OED quotes a 1598 statement that swagger was "created as it were by a
> natural Prosopopeia [here: without any known source], without etimologie or
> deriuation (sic)." I am inclined to say that that's all there is to it."

He keeps the source's own spelling rather than modernizing it, then answers
several centuries of etymological argument in one dry sentence. The person
is visible in how little space he gives himself to agree with the OED.

> "Take your swag and go home with the swagger of a winner."

The column's last line uses both words from its own subject in their
ordinary present-day sense, with no sentence framing it as a conclusion. It
closes on the words at work rather than on a statement about the essay
itself.
