# Editorial review: word-of-the-day/galvanize (editor/01)

## Skeptic

The thesis: "galvanize" carries a jolt that was once a literal electric current
and is now a metaphor, and the metaphor still demands an external shock delivered
all at once rather than a slow internal build. The piece stands on four claims.

Claim one, the eponym and his experiment. Galvani made a severed frog's leg twitch
in 1786 (scissors during a storm; a metal hook on an iron railing) and concluded
in 1791, in *De Viribus Electricitatis*, that living tissue held its own "animal
electricity." Britannica (s2) confirms the 1786 experiments, the 1791 treatise, and
the animal-electricity conclusion word for word. Held.

Claim two, the dispute as two real readings. Galvani read the leg as a source of
charge; Volta read it as a mere detector, the current arising from two dissimilar
metals in contact. Britannica and Whipple (s2, s3) both carry Volta's exact
objection, and Whipple carries Galvani's Leyden-jar analogy for his own side. The
piece resists the popular "Galvani wrong, Volta right" flattening: Volta won the
narrow point and still named the effect after his rival. I pushed on "Galvani never
conceded" as possibly unsupported; Britannica settles it, recording Galvani's 1794
anonymous defense and his metal-free frog experiment. Held.

Claim three, Aldini at Newgate. Linda Hall (s4) confirms Aldini was Galvani's
nephew, succeeded him at Bologna in 1798, ran current through animal and human
corpses, and on 18 January 1803 galvanized the executed George Forster: jaws
contracted, muscles convulsed, one eye opened, right hand raised, legs moved. Date,
name, and effects all match. The Frankenstein link is reported as speculation and
then narrowed to what the record actually holds (Shelley was five; the Shelleys are
only documented as having discussed galvanism), which is the honest reading. Held.
Two dramatizations rode along that the record does not support: "hours after" the
hanging and "in front of a crowd." I cut both to the flat report the source and the
voice guide both call for.

Claim four, the two senses and their dates. MW (s1) dates the verb's first use to
1802 (sense 1a, physiological); Etymonline (s5) dates the entry to 1801, the
electroplating branch to 1839, and the figurative "excite as if by electricity" to
1853. The article keeps 1802/1801 on the literal sense and 1853 on the figurative
one, never crossing them, and the 1853 *Villette* usage corroborates the figurative
date. I opened MW and confirmed the figurative sense is "to stimulate or excite as
if by an electric shock" with no "sudden" in it; the definition card had smuggled
the thesis word "sudden" into a definition cited to the dictionary, so I removed it.
Held.

Display text: headline "The jolt in 'galvanize' used to be literal" is a claim the
piece defends and dodges the desk's proper-noun-does-something reflex. The dek states
a claim about the world, not about the article's method. All four subheads are the
piece's own nouns. The Villette quotation in the note is verbatim against the primary
text.

Every citation href was opened as printed. All six resolve to the source's own page
(MW, Etymonline, Britannica, Whipple, Linda Hall, Standard Ebooks *Villette*). The
one source discrepancy is the hook's metal: Britannica (s2) says copper, Whipple (s3)
says brass. The article named the same hook both ways in consecutive sections; I made
it copper throughout, the name established first and cited to s2, since the s3
citation supports the dissimilar-metals mechanism regardless of the metal's name.

## Cut

The piece is lean and mostly clean of slop. The sentence-by-sentence pass, the edge
pass, and the delete test turned up no empty conclusion, no unearned punchline, no
decorative-analysis verb, and no self-reference. The negative constructions that
appear ("The electricity was not in the frog," "not the one people reach for today")
each correct a real, named position and survive the earned-contrast test. The dek is
two clauses joined by "and," not a comma triad or a semicolon reversal. No em-dashes,
no banned-term pressure.

Three sentences failed on grounds other than pure slop and were fixed rather than
routed. The Newgate sentence carried "hours after" and "in front of a crowd," both
unsupported by the evidence record and both exactly the adjectival dramatization the
voice guide warns off the Aldini material; cut. The Foster-spelling sentence claimed
the name is spelled Foster "as often as" Forster, a frequency the record does not
establish; trimmed to the noted variant. The definition card's "sudden" was the
thesis pre-loaded into a dictionary-cited line; cut. No repeated pattern across the
piece beyond the tendency to reach for a vivid specific the record had not supplied,
which the three trims resolve.

The close ("why a slow build never galvanizes, and why the word still asks for a
jolt") is a word-sized verdict the argument earned, not a generic send-off, and it
is not built to a prior article's mold. It stands.

## Reader

Read straight through, the piece gives what the six sources do not assemble on their
own: one twitching leg read two ways, each partly right, the loser's name winning the
language, and a working rule for the live verb, that it needs an external jolt all at
once because that is what the current literally did to Aldini's corpse. That reading
is the article's own synthesis, and it matches the draft-handoff's original-work
sentence. The prose sits closer to the voice-guide exemplars (Zimmer's and Liberman's
flat, sequential handling of a strange fact) than to a median summary. The headline
holds as the largest claim.

## Edits

- Definition card: removed "sudden" so the MW-cited line reads "to rouse someone or something to action, as if by an electric shock," matching the dictionary rather than the thesis.
- Volta section: changed "brass hook" to "copper hook" so the same hook is named one way; copper is established and cited to s2, and the s3 dissimilar-metals point is unaffected.
- Newgate section: cut "hours" and "in front of a crowd" from the Forster sentence; the record supports neither and the voice guide asks for flat reporting there.
- Newgate section: trimmed "The record spells the name Foster as often as Forster" to "The name is also spelled Foster," dropping the unsupported frequency claim.

## Required work

None. All findings were editor-fixable; no evidence gap, broken central claim, or
source-policy failure remains for the researcher or writer. The writer still runs the
proof and the orchestrator stamps after these edits.

## Decision

approve — the load-bearing facts, dates, quotation, and citation targets all verify
against the owning sources, and the four remaining fidelity and flat-reporting trims
were made directly.
