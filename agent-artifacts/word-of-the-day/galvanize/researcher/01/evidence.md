# Evidence: word-of-the-day/galvanize (01)

The record firmly supports the article's spine. The dictionary definition and the
overall first-use year of the verb are owned by Merriam-Webster; the figurative
"rouse to action" sense and its 1853 first-attestation are owned by Etymonline,
which also dates the electroplating sense. The Galvani experiments, the "animal
electricity" claim, and the substance of Volta's counter-explanation (that the
current came from the contact of two dissimilar metals, not from the animal) are
documented by two independent history-of-science sources that agree on the
mechanism, including the Leyden-jar analogy Galvani used for his own side. The
January 1803 Newgate demonstration by Giovanni Aldini on George Forster's corpse
is documented by a reputable library source. A real figurative usage is confirmed
verbatim from the primary literary text, Charlotte Bronte's *Villette* (1853),
which also happens to sit at the figurative first-attestation year.

Three things are thin or need care. First, no source I read gives a *separate*
dictionary date for the figurative sense: Merriam-Webster folds the verb into a
single 1802 first-use tied to the physiological sense, and only Etymonline pins
the figurative sense to 1853. I did not read the OED entry (paywalled), so the
1853 figurative date rests on one reference source plus the corroborating *Villette*
usage. Second, the two etymology sources disagree on the year the verb entered
English (1801 vs 1802). Third, the Frankenstein link is explicitly speculative in
the sources, and the spelling of the prisoner's name varies (Forster / Foster).

```text
URL:         https://www.britannica.com/biography/Luigi-Galvani
Kind:        secondary — an encyclopedia reporting the history of science; it
             recounts Galvani's and Volta's work from outside either party.
Establishes: Galvani's experiments and dates, his "animal electricity" claim, the
             title and date of his treatise, and Volta's counter-explanation.
Paraphrase:  In 1786 Galvani obtained muscular contractions by touching a frog's
             nerves with scissors during an electrical storm, and further observed
             twitching when a copper hook pressed into the spinal cord was hung on
             an iron railing — contraction occurring with only a metallic contact
             between muscle and nerve. He published in 1791 in "De Viribus
             Electricitatis in Motu Musculari Commentarius" (Commentary on the
             Effect of Electricity on Muscular Motion), concluding that animal
             tissue held an innate vital force he called "animal electricity."
             Volta rejected this, holding that the contact of dissimilar metals was
             the true source of the stimulation and the frog's leg only an
             indicator. The dispute prompted Volta to build the voltaic pile, a
             battery producing current without biological tissue. Volta coined the
             term "galvanism" and still praised the work as one of the most
             beautiful and surprising discoveries.
Locators:    Body sections on the frog experiments, the 1791 Commentarius, and the
             Volta controversy.
Quote:       "the contact of dissimilar metals was the true source of stimulation";
             treatise title "De Viribus Electricitatis in Motu Musculari
             Commentarius".
```

```text
             demonstrate contact electricity without tissue, Volta built the pile

Quote:       Galvani's analogy, "a kind of circuit of a delicate nerve fluid is

             circuit which is completed in a Leyden jar."
```

## Contradictions

- **Which first-use year, and for which sense.** Merriam-Webster dates the verb's
  first known use to 1802 and ties that date to sense 1a (physiological
  stimulation), giving no separate date for the figurative sense 1b. Etymonline
  dates the verb's entry into English to 1801 and separately dates the figurative
  sense to 1853. The two disagree on the entry year (1801 vs 1802) and differ in
  what they date: MW dates the literal verb, Etymonline additionally dates the
  figurative sense. The article should say the verb is attested from about
  1801-1802 in the literal electrical sense and that the figurative "rouse to
  action" sense is first recorded in 1853 (Etymonline), not present the 1802 MW
  date as the figurative date. The 1853 *Villette* usage independently corroborates
  the figurative date.

- **Frankenstein influence: documented vs asserted.** Popular retellings assert
  Aldini's Forster demonstration inspired *Frankenstein* (1818). The Linda Hall
  source treats this as often suggested but speculative: Mary Shelley was five in
  1803, and what is actually documented is only that the Shelleys discussed
  galvanism before the novel was conceived. Keep this as culture/context, not
  causation.

- **Both men were partly right.** Popular versions frame the dispute as Galvani
  wrong, Volta right. The sources are more careful: Volta correctly identified
  bimetallic (dissimilar-metal) contact as a source of current, which led to the
  battery, while Galvani's core claim that living tissue generates electricity was
  later vindicated as bioelectricity. The article's "true scientific quarrel"
  should note that the disagreement was real and that each side owned part of the
  answer, without flattening Volta's objection to a footnote.

- **Name spelling.** Sources render the executed prisoner as both "George Forster"
  and "George Foster." The commission uses "Forster." Note the variance; do not
  treat the two spellings as two people.
