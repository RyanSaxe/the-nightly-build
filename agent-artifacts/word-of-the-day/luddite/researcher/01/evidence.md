# Evidence: word-of-the-day/luddite (researcher 01)

The evidence strongly supports the commissioned article. The definition and the
1811 first attestation are secured from an authoritative dictionary
(Merriam-Webster, opened in full). The Ned Ludd origin is documented *as
tradition, not fact*: the founding anecdote was first printed decades after the
fact and the scholarship treats the man as "almost certainly fictional." The
1811-1816 movement, its specific machines and grievances (wide frames, cheap
"inferior" hosiery, unapprenticed labour; croppers versus gig mills and
shearing frames), the Frame Breaking Act (royal assent 20 March 1812, a capital
statute set to lapse 1 March 1814), and Byron's maiden Lords speech of 27
February 1812 are all grounded in opened primary or authoritative sources, with
Byron quoted from Hansard's own text. The commissioned angle — that the Luddites
opposed the *use* of machinery to deskill and underpay, not machinery as such —
is directly supported by a labour history and by Brian Merchant, and the present
"reclaimed" sense is grounded in a named 2022 New York Times report.

The record is thin or contested in three places, each flagged below: (1) the
OED entry itself is behind a login wall and could not be opened, so the
dictionary attestation rests on Merriam-Webster, with the OED's "1811 Nottingham
Review" claim recorded only from secondary reporting; (2) the popular
"more troops than Wellington had in the Peninsula" comparison is, on the
evidence, **inaccurate** and should not be repeated as fact — see Contradictions;
(3) the York execution total (17) is firm, but many retellings cite "14,"
counting only the second hanging.

## Contradictions

- **"More troops than Wellington had in the Peninsula" is not supported and
  should be cut or heavily qualified.** The commission's phrasing ("troop
  deployments larger than some of Wellington's Peninsular forces") repeats a
  popular claim that the opened economic-history source rejects. The 12,000
  figure originates with F. O. Darvall, *Popular Disturbances and Public Order
  in Regency England* (1934), p. 260, whose own wording was vague ("larger than
  many actual armies with which British Generals had... won important foreign
  campaigns") and named neither Wellington nor the Peninsula. Eric Hobsbawm
  (*Labouring Men*, p. 8) added the Wellington comparison. Anton Howes
  ("Were more troops sent to quash the Luddites than to fight Napoleon?",
  medium.com/@antonhowes) concludes the comparison is inaccurate: Wellington's
  initial 1808 force was ~30,000, and the Peninsula peak (Oct 1813) ~73,000 —
  each far above 12,000 — and the Luddite deployment was largely home-service
  militia, a structural mismatch. Safe to state: roughly 12,000 troops were
  deployed against the Luddites (Darvall's figure). Not safe to state: that this
  exceeded Wellington's Peninsular army.
  Source: https://medium.com/@antonhowes/were-more-troops-sent-to-quash-the-luddites-than-to-fight-napoleon-233c802c216d

- **Did Ned Ludd exist?** The dictionary tradition (Merriam-Webster) preserves
  the founding anecdote — a Leicestershire youth who smashed a frame c.1779 —
  while flagging it as possibly mythical. The scholarship (wordorigins; Britannica
  "probably mythical") treats him as almost certainly fictional, noting the story
  was first printed decades later (Merriam-Webster's word-history attributes the
  anecdote to George Pellew's *Life of Sidmouth*, 1847). Present both: the name
  is documented, the man is not.

- **York execution count: 17 vs 14.** The firm total is 17 in January 1813
  (3 on 8 Jan for Horsfall's murder + 14 on 16 Jan for the Rawfolds attack and
  burglaries). Many secondary retellings say "14," counting only the 16 January
  hanging. Britannica declines a number ("many hangings"). Use 17 with the
  breakdown, or specify "fourteen hanged on 16 January."

- **"What the Luddites really wanted" is itself a scholarly argument, not a
  neutral fact.** The reading advanced by E. P. Thompson (*The Making of the
  English Working Class*) and by Merchant — that the target was the free-market
  destruction of customary wages and the deskilling of trades, not machinery per
  se — is the interpretation the commission adopts and the evidence supports. It
  is a historiographical position (the older popular reading was simple
  machine-hatred), so the article should present it as the better-supported
  reading, not as the only thing ever said. Thompson's book is cited here from
  secondary reporting; it was not read firsthand.
