## Word and angle
**Luddite** (noun; also adjective). Chosen because its documented origin has
real events and people for an entertaining short read, it names a recognizable
pattern, and it remains sharply useful far outside its origin — the benchmark
the series sets with "sphexish."

The story to tell (documented): the 1811-1816 machine-breaking movement among
English textile workers (framework knitters, croppers, weavers) in
Nottinghamshire, Yorkshire, and Lancashire, acting under the name of the
probably-mythical "Ned Ludd" / "General Ludd"; the specific grievance (skilled
workers displaced by wide frames and shearing machines used to undercut wages
and quality, not machinery as such); the state response (the Frame Breaking Act
of 1812 making machine-breaking a capital crime; troop deployments larger than
some of Wellington's Peninsular forces; the 1813 York trials and executions);
and Lord Byron's February 1812 maiden speech in the House of Lords opposing the
death penalty for frame-breaking.

Trace the development of the sense: from the specific historical actors to the
modern "Luddite" as a general term for someone opposed to new technology, and
the more careful "neo-Luddite" usage. Ground the present sense in real,
citable modern usage. The article's useful turn: the common present meaning
("anyone who hates technology") flattens what the original Luddites actually
opposed (the use of machinery to displace and deskill labor on the owners'
terms) — a distinction that stays useful whenever the word is reached for in a
debate about automation and AI.

Etymology is history, not proof of a present meaning: cite an authoritative
dictionary (OED / Merriam-Webster) for the definition and first-attestation,
and original/primary sources for quoted historical usage.
