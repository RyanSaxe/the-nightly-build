# Voice guide: the short lexical-history essay

## Directive

Write this as a told story with a scalpel at the end, at column tempo. The
house register is a patient first-principles explainer that builds each concept
before the sentence that needs it. That patience is wrong for the paper's
smallest read. This genre moves: it drops into a scene, telescopes decades into
a clause, and spends its economy on getting to the turn while the reader still
has momentum. Report the history; do not teach it in graded steps.

Keep the house reader relationship exactly. The best word writers earn their
charm partly by addressing the reader ("dear drowsy reader") and narrating
their own digressions. Do not. Direct address, "a reader will notice," and any
gesture at the act of looking the word up are cut by the house standard and stay
cut here. Transfer the wit, never the buttonholing. The prose is one confident
voice talking about the word, not to the reader about the word.

The exactness is the point of the piece, not its garnish. The whole read exists
to hand back a distinction the common usage has flattened. Land that distinction
plainly, in the story's own nouns, and let the history that preceded it do the
arguing. Do not announce that a correction is coming, do not label it a myth or
a lesson, and do not reach for the automation parallel — the reader supplies it.

Three moves change sentences here that the default will not produce on its own:
open inside the history rather than framing it, allow the sentence a dry
understatement when the documented fact is already funny, and let the closing
distinction arrive as a flat statement of what was actually true.

## Licenses

form: scene-first compression
move: The strong short essays open on a dated, located fact and trust it to
  carry the reader in, then telescope large spans into a single clause once the
  scene is set. Merriam-Webster opens "toward the end of 1811, in the vicinity
  of Nottingham" with no throat-clearing; Wilton lets one newspaper line stand
  for a decade of usage. Use for the opening and for every transition between
  eras.
bar: A compressed passage must still carry a specific, sourced fact the reader
  could not have supplied. Compression that drops detail to move faster is just
  a summary; cut it instead. The opening sentence names a real thing (a date, a
  place, a machine, a number) before it names an idea.

form: dry understatement on a documented fact
move: Forsyth gets his laugh by stating a true, well-sourced absurdity flat and
  declining to explain the joke — a name honoring a man who likely never
  existed, a state deploying more troops than it sent to a war. The fact does
  the work; the sentence just refuses to flinch.
bar: The wit rides on a cited fact and the sentence still delivers that fact if
  the reader misses the joke. No aside exists only to be funny. Ceiling: at most
  two such turns in the piece, and none in the definition card or the closing
  distinction. If removing the drollery loses information, it stays; if it loses
  only the drollery, it goes.

form: documented uncertainty kept in view
move: The exemplars treat "probably mythical," "almost certainly fictional,"
  "no strong evidence" as part of the interest rather than a hedge to bury. The
  gap between the name and the man is itself a fact worth stating cleanly.
bar: State the uncertainty as a plain finding with its source, not as a writerly
  wink and not as a qualifier smuggled mid-sentence. It earns its place only
  where the uncertainty is genuinely part of the story, not sprinkled for
  texture.

form: the flat landing
move: Wilton and Merriam-Webster close on what the word now does or what it
  originally meant, stated once, and stop. No moral, no "the real point is," no
  restatement of the thesis. The restraint is what makes the correction land.
bar: The closing distinction is a declarative sentence about the word or the
  people who made it, built entirely on facts already reported above. It names
  no lesson and grades nothing. If it announces its own significance, rewrite it
  until it merely states the true thing.

## Mark Forsyth, "Fainéant" (Inky Fool)
Source: https://blog.inkyfool.com/2010/10/faineant.html
Craft:
- cadence: Short declaratives and one-word questions set against longer
  explanatory sentences; the rhythm speeds and slows to keep a small subject
  lively.
- argument: Sets up the intuitive folk reading, then overturns it with the older
  documented form. The reversal is the structure.
- evidence: Old French forms, historical epithets (the rois fainéants, Louis the
  Sluggard), a classical quotation — real sources, lightly worn.
- stance: Delighted authority. Certain of the facts, amused by them.
- notice: Fixes on the sound of the word and how the sound misleads, then uses
  documentation to correct the ear.
- diction: Plain Anglo-Saxon verbs around the technical etymological terms;
  never inflates.
- reader: Chummy and direct ("dear drowsy reader") — the one move to leave
  behind; the house voice does not address the reader.
- important move: Wit is anchored in the word's own material (its letters, its
  history), never imported. That anchoring is what makes it transferable while
  the persona is not.

## Dave Wilton, "Luddite" (Wordorigins.org)
Source: https://www.wordorigins.org/big-list-entries/luddite
Craft:
- cadence: Short factual sentences ("Being a textile worker was a good living")
  alternating with longer contextual passages, pacing the historical narrative.
- argument: Lets a chronological chain of dated citations carry the meaning's
  drift; the shift from labor grievance to anti-technology insult emerges from
  the evidence rather than being asserted.
- evidence: Dated newspaper quotations anchor each stage; explicit caveats about
  weak evidence and the limits of usage data show the sourcing honestly.
- stance: Scrupulous and restrained. Corrects the record without scolding.
- notice: Catches that the modern sense inverts the original grievance, and that
  a 1947 usage rhymes with a present one.
- diction: Concrete and unshowy; the history supplies the interest.
- reader: Treated as intelligent and uninstructed; no hand-holding, no address.
- important move: Ends by letting the strongest quotation stand rather than
  drawing the moral. The withheld conclusion makes the present relevance land
  harder — the model for this article's close.

## Merriam-Webster, Word of the Day: "Luddite"
Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-day/luddite-2022-07-26
Craft:
- cadence: Tight and even; every sentence advances the history, none lingers.
  The whole thing is shorter than most single sections of a house article.
- argument: Origin to proverb to modern sense in three clean moves, strictly
  chronological.
- evidence: A cited 1847 biography for the Ned Ludd legend; the "probably
  mythical" qualifier does real work in one word.
- stance: Neutral reference-desk authority, faintly warm.
- notice: Marks the exact hinge — "with the onset of the information age" — where
  the sense broadened.
- diction: Register-perfect for compression: no clause spent on transition.
- reader: Assumed curious, never lectured; the example sentence models present
  use instead of describing it.
- important move: Demonstrates how much documented history fits in 550-800 words
  when nothing is framed and nothing is summarized twice — the geometry this
  article lives in.
