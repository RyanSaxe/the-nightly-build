# Voice guide: word-of-the-day (clinical-origin lexical history)

## Directive

Write with the house calm, but pitched to a quick read that pays the reader
back in scene and distinction rather than exposition. The house default
explains; this piece shows a dated episode and then draws one clean line. Trust
a well-read reader to feel a recognition without being told it is coming; state
the pattern once, precisely, and let the recognition happen off the page.

Three moves change sentences here. First, render the origin as an observed
scene built on a single exact detail, and deliver that detail flatly: the cooler
the sentence, the harder the strange fact lands. Second, when the word must be
separated from a look-alike term, draw the difference as a bright line from a
sourced property of each, not as a rhetorical reversal. Third, allow one dry
note of ordinary recognition to land why the word earns its keep, aimed at the
human universality of the pattern and never at the patients who first showed it.

Keep the clinical register when the material is clinical: name the physician,
the year, the deficit, the exact behavior. Precision is the warmth here.

## Licenses

form: the compressed clinical scene, one or two sentences, built on a single observed detail
move: as Sacks does, present the deficit through one exact thing a named clinician recorded seeing, and report it in a flat clinical register that adds no adjective of astonishment; use it once, where the origin episode first enters.
bar:  the sentence names a real sourced particular (who, when, what was observed), and it would lose its force if a word like "remarkable" or "astonishing" were added; if it tells the reader the case is strange instead of letting the fact be strange, cut it.

form: the bright-line distinction from a near-neighbor term
move: as Wilton and Stamper do, name the confusable term plainly and separate it from the subject by a real cited property of each, so the correction reads as reporting; deploy it where a reader would otherwise carry the wrong near-synonym forward.
bar:  the confusion being corrected must be one a well-read reader actually holds (named, real, not invented for the sentence), and the split must rest on a sourced property rather than a "not X but Y" cadence; at most one such distinction carries weight in the piece.

form: the dry understatement that lands present use
move: as Stamper does with self-aimed wit, make one plain, lightly wry observation about how the pattern shows up outside the clinic, pointed at its human universality; use it once, near the close, to earn the word's place in a working vocabulary.
bar:  it states something true and specific about present usage, stays sayable aloud in a serious paper, and points away from the clinical subjects; if the wit is doing the work instead of the observation, or if it hardens into a reusable line, cut it.

## Kory Stamper, "Falling in Love with Words" (Word by Word excerpt)
Source: https://longreads.com/2017/03/14/falling-in-love-with-words-the-secret-life-of-a-lexicographer/
Craft:
- cadence: short declaratives broken by one longer comic sentence; rhythm comes from constraint then release, not from uniform length.
- argument: builds a claim about a word's texture by accumulating precise near-synonyms and rejecting the loose one, so the point is demonstrated rather than asserted.
- evidence: the sense-distinction itself is the evidence ("measly" carries a miserly, grudging meagerness that "teeny" does not); she shows the gap instead of naming it.
- stance: affectionate expert who finds the fine grain delightful and never superior about holding it.
- notice: the self-implicating physical detail (sweating through the dress in a cold room) that fixes an abstract scene in the body.
- diction: mostly plain words with one vivid figure per stretch ("lit up like a penny arcade"), never a figure in every sentence.
- reader: treated as a fellow word-lover who will enjoy a distinction, not a student who needs it justified.
- important move: she aims the humor at herself, never at the words or the people, so the precision stays respectful and the wit never curdles.

## Ben Zimmer, "Medical Misapprehensions" (Word Routes)
Source: https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wordroutes/medical-misapprehensions/
Craft:
- cadence: level and journalistic; opens colloquial, then tightens onto the documented stakes without a hinge sentence announcing the turn.
- argument: a light observation becomes a consequential point (amusing mishearings become deadly drug errors) by accumulation, not by a stated pivot.
- evidence: specific named cases with a parenthetical gloss (fentanyl mistaken for sufentanil, about ten times as potent; Celexa the antidepressant against Celebrex the anti-inflammatory).
- stance: accessible authority worn lightly; expertise shown through the exact example, not claimed.
- notice: the precise near-homophone pair that collides, chosen because the collision is the whole point.
- diction: plain, always the specific term over the vague magnitude, a parenthetical clarification instead of a digression.
- reader: general but respected, handed the real name and the real number rather than a gesture at scale.
- important move: he crosses from amusement to consequence silently, so the reader arrives at the stakes without being told a turn is happening.

## Anatoly Liberman, "The level of our expertise: the perplexing verb baffle" (Oxford Etymologist)
Source: https://blog.oup.com/2026/07/the-level-of-our-expertise-the-perplexing-verb-baffle/
Craft:
- cadence: conversational and essayistic, willing to stop on a question before answering it.
- argument: reframes "origin unknown" as a claim with levels, turning an admission of ignorance into structure the reader can follow.
- evidence: names where his knowledge rests (the amassed database) as carefully as what it establishes, and marks the point where it runs out.
- stance: the humble expert whose credibility is built by naming the limit rather than papering it.
- notice: the seam where certainty stops and inference begins.
- diction: technical terms set beside colloquial phrasing, so the scholarship never becomes a jargon wall.
- reader: an intelligent adult trusted to hold an open question without needing it forced shut.
- important move: uncertainty framed as an invitation, not a failure; an honest seam is left more interesting than a false resolution.

## Dave Wilton, "sabotage" (Wordorigins.org)
Source: https://www.wordorigins.org/big-list-entries/sabotage
Craft:
- cadence: methodical and date-anchored, one claim per step, no step skipped.
- argument: the documented origin displaces the charming folk myth and is shown to be the better story for being true.
- evidence: primary sources with dates (newspaper uses of 1906 and 1914), dictionary entries, and the point where the myth got amplified (a 1991 film) tracked as carefully as the truth.
- stance: the debunker who prefers the real history and says plainly why it is the richer one.
- notice: the exact moment a "bungled job" sense turned deliberate, which is the pivot the whole entry rests on.
- diction: precise and unshowy, sources named inline in the sentence that uses them.
- reader: someone who would rather hold the sourced truth than the fable, and is shown the fable is the poorer of the two.
- important move: he names the myth plainly and dates its spread, so the correction reads as reporting rather than contrarian point-scoring.

## Oliver Sacks, "The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat" (LRB)
Source: https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v05/n09/oliver-sacks/the-man-who-mistook-his-wife-for-a-hat
Craft:
- cadence: cool observation first, then the one detail that unsettles; sentences shorten at the moment of strangeness rather than swelling.
- argument: he presents and withholds interpretation, letting the concrete symptom carry the weight and admitting where conventional neurology fails him.
- evidence: the observed behavior itself, rendered exactly (the patient reaching for his wife's head as if for a hat), with nothing added.
- stance: the physician who reports a deficit precisely while keeping the patient's dignity, never sensational.
- notice: the single telling act delivered flatly, which is exactly what makes it land.
- diction: clinical register held even at the vivid moment, no adjective piled onto the strange fact.
- reader: trusted to feel the strangeness unprompted, never cued to be amazed.
- important move: restraint; the flatter the delivery of an extraordinary fact, the harder it hits, and any melodrama would defuse it.
