# Evidence: word-of-the-day/anosognosia (02)

This record carries forward the full, still-valid 01 evidence for the article's
spine and adds one new verified finding requested by editor/01: the exact
Merriam-Webster pronunciation respelling for "anosognosia." The word card
currently prints `\ˌa-nə-ˌsäg-ˈnō-zh(ē-)ə\` (schwa `nə` in the second syllable),
but Merriam-Webster prints the second syllable as `nō` (macron). I opened the
M-W entry firsthand and captured the printed line verbatim: M-W prints
`ˌa-nō-ˌsäg-ˈnō-zh(ē-)ə`. The general-dictionary lookup
(/dictionary/anosognosia) resolves to the same medical-dictionary entry and
carries the identical pronunciation; the medical and main forms do not differ
for this word. The card should be corrected from `nə` to `nō` to match its
source. Nothing else in the 01 record changed.

The 01 spine still holds. The word's definition is carried by an authoritative
dictionary (Merriam-Webster) and its Greek roots (a- "without" + nosos "disease"
+ gnosis "knowledge") are uncontested. The origin is well documented and dated:
Joseph Babinski coined "anosognosie" in 1914, presenting to the Société de
Neurologie de Paris, to name the unawareness of paralysis he observed in
patients with organic hemiplegia; his own defining sentence survives in the
original French and in a published English translation. The near-neighbor
history (Gabriel Anton's 1890s cases of unawareness of cortical blindness and
deafness, and the composite eponym "Anton-Babinski syndrome") is sound. The
widening of sense into general "unawareness of illness" and its modern
psychiatric use (impaired insight as a driver of treatment non-adherence in
schizophrenia) is carried by a current clinical reference and the well-known
Amador line of work. The single Dunning-Kruger contrast is defensible and
sourced.

Where the record is thin (unchanged from 01): (1) the precise meeting date
"11 June 1914" appears only in gated/secondary sources I could not open in full,
though the year, venue, and journal citation are solid from opened sources; use
"June 1914" or "presented to the Société de Neurologie de Paris in 1914" to stay
on firmly sourced ground. (2) The OED first-attestation (1915, Medical Record,
New York) comes from an OED search snippet; the OED entry itself is login-gated
and I did not read it directly. (3) The Amador prevalence figure (57% of 412
patients) comes from secondary summaries of his study, not the primary paper.
Each is flagged in Sources/Numbers below.

## Contradictions

- **Word-card pronunciation vs. its source (the 02 finding).** The word card
  prints the schwa form `\ˌa-nə-ˌsäg-ˈnō-zh(ē-)ə\`; Merriam-Webster's own entry,
  opened firsthand this round, prints `ˌa-nō-ˌsäg-ˈnō-zh(ē-)ə` (second syllable
  `nō`, macron). This is a divergence between the card and the M-W notation it
  imitates, resolved in M-W's favor: the card is wrong and should read `nō`.
  Only the second syllable differs; every other character matches.
- "Coined" vs. "named/first described": Sources are consistent that Babinski
  introduced the neologism "anosognosie" in 1914 (Langer 2009; LITFL; the
  translated primary paper carries the term in its title). Anton described the
  *phenomenon* of unawareness earlier (1890s) but did not use this word. So
  Babinski coined the *term*; Anton has priority on the *observation* for
  blindness/deafness. State it that way to avoid the false impression that
  Babinski first observed unawareness of any deficit.
- Anosognosia vs. denial: A genuine, live conceptual boundary rather than a
  factual dispute. StatPearls and Wikipedia both treat them as
  phenomenologically similar but distinct (organic unawareness vs. psychological
  defense); Wikipedia notes "attempts have been made at a unified explanation."
  The writer should present the distinction as the standard clinical position
  while acknowledging the boundary is debated, not settled.
- First-attestation date: The 1914 coinage is in French. The OED's earliest
  English evidence is reported as 1915 (Medical Record, New York) - a different
  claim (first English-language attestation) from the coinage date. These do not
  conflict once labeled correctly. See Numbers.
- Anton's dates: Sources variously cite 1895 (cortical deafness case) and 1899
  (cortical blindness) for Anton's descriptions; both appear consistently. Use
  "the 1890s" if a single year is not needed.
