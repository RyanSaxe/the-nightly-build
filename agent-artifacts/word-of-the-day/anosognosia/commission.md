# Commission: word-of-the-day/anosognosia

## Subject
The word **anosognosia**: a person's unawareness of their own deficit or
illness — the stroke patient who cannot see that their left arm is paralyzed and
insists it works. Trace the documented clinical origin, the widening of the
sense, and the word's present life as a precise name for a pattern of
non-awareness.

## Why this word
It clears the sphexish bar. Its origin is a memorable, dated clinical episode
(Joseph Babinski's 1914 report to the Société de Neurologie in Paris, naming the
denial of hemiplegia), it names a recognizable and still-useful pattern
(unawareness *of the deficit itself*, distinct from denial or mere ignorance),
and it stays useful far beyond neurology — in psychiatry (used for lack of
insight in psychosis) and as a sharp everyday term for the specific failure of
not knowing what one lacks. For this paper's reader it is a cleaner name than
the overworked "Dunning-Kruger" for a related idea, and the piece can make that
distinction in one honest, sourced line without becoming about Dunning-Kruger.

## The documented story to tell (researcher verifies exact facts/dates)
- The Greek roots (a- + nosos "disease" + gnosis "knowledge") and Babinski's
  1914 coinage for patients unaware of their hemiplegia. Get the exact venue,
  year, and what he described.
- The near neighbor: Gabriel Anton's earlier (1890s) accounts of patients
  unaware of blindness/deafness, and the composite eponym "Anton-Babinski
  syndrome." Keep this to the role it plays in the word's history.
- The widening of sense: from a specific right-hemisphere stroke sign to a
  general term for unawareness of illness, including its modern use in
  psychiatry for impaired insight (anosognosia as a reason patients stop
  treatment). Ground the present sense in real, cited modern usage.

## Definition sourcing
Cite an authoritative dictionary (Merriam-Webster and/or a medical dictionary,
plus OED if it carries a datable first attestation) for the definition and, if
available, the first-attestation date. Etymology is history, not proof of
present meaning; the present sense is grounded in real modern uses.
