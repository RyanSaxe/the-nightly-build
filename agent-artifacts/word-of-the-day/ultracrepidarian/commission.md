## The word
**ultracrepidarian** (adj./n.): giving opinions on matters beyond one's
knowledge or competence. Chosen against the series benchmark (sphexish): it has a
memorable, documented origin episode, it names a recognizable pattern, and it
stays useful far beyond the setting that produced it. Its story has two
documented layers worth telling:
1. The ancient anecdote behind the root: Pliny the Elder's account (Natural
   History, Book 35) of the painter **Apelles** and the cobbler who, having
   rightly corrected a sandal, presumed to criticize the leg — prompting the
   rebuke recorded as *"ne supra crepidam sutor iudicaret"* ("let the cobbler not
   judge above the sandal"), i.e. *sutor, ne ultra crepidam*.
2. The actual coinage of the English word, much later: **William Hazlitt**'s use
   of "ultra-crepidarian" against the critic **William Gifford** (c. 1819), which
   is where the adjective enters English.
The article must keep these distinct: the Latin proverb is the root's history;
the English word is Hazlitt's coinage. Etymology is history, not proof of the
present meaning — ground the present sense in real modern usage.
