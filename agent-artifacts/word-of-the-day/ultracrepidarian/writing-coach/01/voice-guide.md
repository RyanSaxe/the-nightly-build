# Voice guide: word-of-the-day/ultracrepidarian

## Directive

Write this as a small, exact story told by someone who trusts the material,
not a lecture about it. Register: dry, precise, unhurried — closer to a good
footnote than a column. Reader relationship: no confiding, no coaching, no
"you've probably said this yourself." State things and let the two documented
scenes (the cobbler's line, Hazlitt's insult) carry the interest; do not
narrate your own amusement at them.

Three moves will actually change sentences here:

1. **Structure the piece as two acts, not one continuous etymology.** The
   Latin proverb and the English coinage are 1,700-odd years and one
   deliberate borrowing apart. Give the pivot from "root" to "word" its own
   beat — a sentence that marks the jump, not a subordinate clause that
   smooths over it.
2. **Let a quoted primary line do the work a summary would flatten.** Pliny's
   Latin and Hazlitt's own sentence against Gifford are both quotable and
   both do more than paraphrase can. Quote them and move on; don't explain
   why they're sharp after showing that they are.
3. **Compress background instead of narrating it.** Any dictionary fact
   (first attestation, current headword status) gets one clause where it's
   needed, never its own throat-clearing sentence ("It's worth noting that
   ...").

## Licenses

```text
form: the pivot sentence
move: marks the jump from the Latin anecdote to the English coinage as a
      turn, not a transition — a short, declarative beat that names the gap
      (centuries, language, genre) the word crossed. Sanguine's word-history
      piece does this with the humors framework: it states the medieval
      belief plainly, then turns on one line to "in time, the physiological
      theory ... dissipated, but the word is still used" — the turn is a
      sentence, not a paragraph.
bar:  one such pivot per piece, placed where the Pliny anecdote ends and the
      Hazlitt-Gifford story begins. It must name what actually separates the
      two layers (time, language, or genre), not just say "later."

form: the corrected assumption
move: state a plausible misreading of the word in one clause, then close it
      with the evidence in the next. Liberman's restive essay does this by
      flatly stating the word "once meant the opposite" of its current sense
      before showing the record. Use it once, for the real confusion this
      word invites: that giving an opinion beyond one's competence is the
      same as being a dilettante or merely presumptuous, when the word's
      own history (a cobbler judging past the sandal) draws a narrower line.
bar:  the misreading must be one a reader would actually make, named
      exactly, and closed by a specific fact from the sourcing, not by
      assertion.

form: the dry closing turn
move: land the present-day relevance with one line of earned, quiet irony
      drawn from the piece's own facts — never a general moral. M-W's
      sanguine piece closes on "a label we'll gladly accept, no matter where
      it comes from": small, specific to what came before it, not
      transferable to another word.
bar:  the line must depend on a fact stated earlier in this piece. If it
      would read the same bolted onto a different word-of-the-day, cut it.
```

## Recently used, do not reuse

The eponym opener — leading with "here is the person behind the word" — has
run across several recent pieces in this series and is the default entry
point for exactly this kind of two-layer, name-attached word. Find another
way in for ultracrepidarian. The commission's own material suggests one: the
word's two-layer structure (proverb, then coinage) means the piece can open
on the corrected cobbler or on the act of correction itself — someone
speaking past their competence — rather than opening on Apelles, Hazlitt, or
Gifford by name. Save the names for where the story earns them.

## Sanguine: word history, Merriam-Webster
Source: https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/sanguine-word-history
Craft:
- cadence: short declaratives that open on the reader's own vocabulary
  ("If you're an optimist, you may be called sanguine"), then a compact
  swerve into the etymology before the paragraph is over.
- argument: the word's oddity (blood -> optimism) is the whole engine; the
  humors framework exists only to resolve that oddity, not as scholarship
  for its own sake.
- evidence: names the mechanism precisely (the four humors, which liquid
  produced which temperament) instead of gesturing at "old medical belief."
- stance: amused but not performing amusement — the humor sits in the facts,
  not in adjectives layered onto them.
- notice: it treats the etymological pivot as the whole story and stops
  there; no coda, no extra history past the point that resolves the puzzle.
- diction: plain register words carrying the technical content ("bile,"
  "ruddy," "confident") rather than dressing the medical theory in modern
  hedge language.
- reader: assumed to already use the word; the piece explains where it came
  from, not what it means.
- the move the axes miss: it resolves a genuine oddity (why does a blood
  word mean optimism?) rather than just reporting a fact chain — the piece
  is built as an answer to a question the reader didn't know they had.

## Restive people never rest, Anatoly Liberman (Oxford Etymologist)
Source: https://blog.oup.com/2026/06/restive-people-never-rest/
Craft:
- cadence: longer sentences that hold a qualification inside them
  ("Some parts of the story ... can be found in most dictionaries, but it
  is the attempts to connect a few distant dots that may be interesting"),
  earned by genuine uncertainty rather than hedging reflex.
- argument: builds by stating a record, then testing a hypothesis against
  it, so the reader watches a case get made instead of receiving a verdict.
- evidence: distinguishes what "can be found in most dictionaries" from
  what the writer is proposing — flags which claims are settled and which
  are the writer's own connection.
- stance: guide, not authority; states disagreement and uncertainty by name
  ("I tend to agree," "I would therefore be happy if") without weakening
  the claims that are actually solid.
- notice: catches the word meaning the opposite of its current sense at an
  earlier stage, and uses that reversal as the spine of the piece rather
  than a trivia aside.
- diction: technical terms (cognate, onomatopoeia) get defined by use in
  the next clause, never left to float.
- reader: trusted to follow a real argument, not just a result.
- the move the axes miss: it names the misconception before it corrects it,
  so the correction has something to push against instead of just adding
  information.

## Sesquipedalian, A.Word.A.Day (Anu Garg)
Source: https://wordsmith.org/words/sesquipedalian.html
Craft:
- cadence: extreme compression — definition, origin, one-line note, one
  real usage citation, done. Nothing repeats across the fields.
- argument: the note earns its single sentence by being about the word
  itself, not the topic the word describes ("a sesquipedalian word is one
  and a half feet long" — the etymology is played straight as a fact, not
  as a joke, and the literalism does the amusing work on its own).
- evidence: the usage line is a citation from a published book, not an
  invented example sentence — the word is shown doing real work.
- stance: no persona, no address to the reader; the entry trusts the facts
  arranged in order to be interesting without a narrator vouching for them.
- notice: pairs the word with a related word (sesquicentennial) sharing the
  root, giving the reader a second handle in one clause instead of a
  digression.
- diction: defines with a string of plain synonyms ("polysyllabic ...
  long-winded") rather than a single technical gloss.
- reader: assumed to want the shortest path to competent use, not a story
  for its own sake.
- the move the axes miss: economy is the craft. Every field does exactly
  one job and stops; nothing is restated for emphasis.

## Self-test

A default house piece on ultracrepidarian would open by introducing Hazlitt
or Apelles by name, walk chronologically from Pliny to Hazlitt to modern
usage, and close by restating the definition in other words. That would be
competent and indistinguishable from any other word-of-the-day in the
series. What this piece should sound like beyond that: two short, separate
scenes joined by one visible pivot, each resolved by a quoted line rather
than a summary of one — the corrected cobbler's proverb standing on its own,
then the pivot naming the seventeen-odd centuries and the language it
crossed, then Hazlitt's own jab standing on its own. The economy is the
wit: showing two good quotes doing their own work, back to back, without a
narrator explaining why they're sharp.
