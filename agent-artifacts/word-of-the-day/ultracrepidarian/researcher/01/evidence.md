# Evidence: word-of-the-day/ultracrepidarian (01)

The evidence supports the article's spine firmly. The definition (giving opinions
beyond one's knowledge or competence, adj. and n.), the ancient anecdote and its
exact Latin, the ~1819 English coinage against William Gifford, and real modern
usage are all confirmed from primaries or firsthand-read authoritative pages.
Two things are genuinely contested and must not be smoothed: (1) whether Hazlitt
*coined* the word or merely *first recorded* it — his own phrase "well called"
presents the epithet as one others had already applied, and an editor of Hazlitt
suggests Charles Lamb coined it; (2) the Latin form, since Pliny actually wrote
*supra crepidam* ("above the sandal"), while the familiar proverb and the English
prefix carry the later *ultra crepidam* ("beyond the sandal"). The record is
thin in exactly one place: the OED entry — the standard authority for the precise
first-attestation wording and date — is login-gated, so its 1819 dating is
corroborated by multiple independent sources and by Hazlitt's own text, but not
read firsthand from OED itself.

## Contradictions

- **Coined vs. first recorded (the central caution).** Most sources, including
  the OED, credit William Hazlitt's 1819 "A Letter to William Gifford, Esq." as
  the earliest evidence of "ultra-crepidarian." But Hazlitt writes "You have been
  *well called* an Ultra-Crepidarian critic," presenting the label as one already
  in circulation against Gifford, not as his own invention. Wikipedia
  (Ultracrepidarianism), citing an editor of Hazlitt's works, records that the
  word "might have been coined by Charles Lamb instead." The honest statement:
  Hazlitt gives the first *recorded* use; who actually *coined* it is unsettled.
  The article should say "first recorded in" or "the word enters the printed
  record with Hazlitt," not "Hazlitt coined it," unless it explicitly flags the
  dispute.

- **Latin form: supra vs. ultra.** Pliny's actual text reads *ne supra crepidam
  sutor iudicaret* ("above the sandal"). The more familiar proverb form
  *sutor, ne ultra crepidam* / *ne sutor ultra crepidam* ("beyond the sandal")
  is a later, post-classical reshaping, and it is the *ultra* form — not Pliny's
  *supra* — that the English prefix in "ultra-crepidarian" echoes. Any claim that
  the English word comes straight from Pliny's wording should note this: the sense
  is Pliny's, the *ultra-* is the proverb's later cast. Dictionary.com even renders
  the Latin loosely as "ne supra crepidam sutor judicare" (wrong verb form);
  trust the LacusCurtius primary text for the exact Latin.

- **Exact wording of Hazlitt's line.** The accurate wording is "You have been well
  called an Ultra-Crepidarian critic" (confirmed in the reprinted primary and by
  four dictionaries/etymology pages). Language Log renders it loosely as "You are
  an Ultra-Crepidarian critic"; that is a paraphrase and drops the load-bearing
  "well called." Use the "well called" form.

- **Dictionary coverage/dating minor disagreement.** Merriam-Webster's free
  dictionary does not carry the word at all (its URL 404s), so it cannot be the
  cited authority the commission's "e.g. OED/Merriam-Webster" implies — use OED
  and Dictionary.com. Dictionary.com dates the word to the band "1800–20," while
  OED pins the first evidence precisely to 1819; these are compatible, not
  conflicting, but do not present "1800–20" as a first-use date.
