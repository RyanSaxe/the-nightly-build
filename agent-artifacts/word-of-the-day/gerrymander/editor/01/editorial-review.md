# Editorial review: word-of-the-day/gerrymander (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: a reprinted newspaper cartoon, not the governor's own hand, fixed
"gerrymander" onto Elbridge Gerry, who signed the 1812 map without authoring it,
and the word then broadened from one Essex district to the general practice.

The claims it rests on, and how each held:

- Headline, "a governor who barely touched the map." MHS (s2) states there is
  little evidence Gerry authored or strongly backed the redistricting law he
  signed. "Barely touched" is a fair rendering of "signed but did not draw or
  support." Held.
- Dek, a Boston newspaper "welded his surname to salamander" in 1812. The cartoon
  titled "The Gerry-Mander" carried the portmanteau; the dek attributes the
  welding to the newspaper, not to a named coiner, so it does not overclaim the
  unsettled coinage. Held.
- The map was drawn to hold the senate: 11 Feb 1812 signing (s2), ~49% of the
  vote against 29 of 40 seats (s3, verified on History.com), Essex South's long
  jointed shape (s2). Held; the figures match the owning sources.
- The cartoon ran in the Boston Gazette on 26 March 1812 (s4). The date rests on
  a secondary account and is cited to one; the prose claims no primary newspaper
  copy, so the limit the evidence record flags is carried honestly.
- First known use 1812, noun and verb, attributed to Merriam-Webster alone (s1,
  verified). No OED attribution appears. Held.
- Authorship: Tisdale documented via the Dean memorandum and the February 1812
  Thorndike dinner (s2, verified); Stuart and Allston only "attributed," Stuart
  found less credible (s5, s4). The documented/asserted line the commission asks
  for is drawn correctly.
- Coiner left unsettled: "several Federalist opponents... among them Richard
  Alsop" (s2, s4). No single coiner asserted. Held.
- Pronunciation: hard g like "Gary," historically correct (s6, s2); soft-j drift
  argued on the record in 1850 (s7, quote verified); still "sub judice" per a
  Supreme Court public information officer (s6, verified). Held.
- Rucho (2019) cited for present usage, quoting "political gerrymandering has gone
  too far" (s8, verified against the Cornell mirror), then corrected where the
  opinion calls the 1812 lines "congressional" against the state-senate record
  (s2). The piece never leans on Rucho for origin facts. Held.

Two breaks, both mine to resolve without routing:

- "The Boston editor Benjamin Russell supplying the name" is a named-person claim
  no cited source supports. I re-opened MHS (s2), which does not mention Russell,
  and the evidence record's Smithsonian paraphrase names Alsop, not Russell.
  Russell appears only inside the evidence record's own account of the Stuart
  legend, which the record says it does not support. Nonessential, so cut rather
  than routed; the surviving sentence credits the Stuart attribution the sources
  do document.
- Every href opened as printed. s1, s2, s3, s5, s6, s7, s8 resolve to their own
  pages; s5 (MHS catalog) and s8 (supremecourt.gov PDF) return 200. Smithsonian
  (s4) returns 403 to automated fetchers, but the printed URL is the article's
  own page, which a reader reaches in a browser and which the researcher read
  there; not a broken link. The asset crop retains the whole winged, clawed
  district-monster and the title text, and its caption is a factual cited label
  that keeps the Salem broadside distinct from the Boston Gazette cartoon.

`data-nb-kind` labels audited: the dictionary (s1), the MHS catalog record for
the physical object (s5), and the Rucho opinion (s8) as primary; the narrative
and reporting accounts as secondary. Each fits the primary/secondary test.

## Cut

Two sentences failed the slop test as edge signposts and were deleted, not
repaired:

- "The arithmetic showed the map's purpose." An empty frame ahead of the vote
  and seat figures. The voice guide asks for facts stacked without a sentence
  that comments on them; the numbers carry the purpose on their own.
- "The question has never fully closed." A signpost that pre-states what the
  "sub judice" quotation then proves. Cutting it lets the 1850 and modern facts
  sit in the gap the reader assembles.

I kept "What is certain is how it spread" and "The history blurs even there":
each carries a real epistemic turn central to the commission (origin uncertain
but diffusion documented; the misremembering reaches the Supreme Court), not a
grade of the argument.

No borrowed phrasing from the voice-guide exemplars (Forsyth, Zimmer, Liberman)
survives in the draft, and no lifted clause from the commission or briefs; "blur"
is a single common word, not a carried clause. Punctuation is clean: no
em-dashes, no semicolons, colons used to introduce what their clause promises.
Headings read as the argument's own steps. The headline and closer avoid this
desk's recent molds: the headline leads with "A cartoon," not a proper noun
doing or not doing something, and the closer is prose landing the definition, not
an nb-note repeating "the word keeps X."

## Reader

Reading only the article, I leave with the record pulled apart from the legend
and from the court's own error: Tisdale documented while Stuart stays asserted,
three 1812 dates kept distinct, the districts state-senate and not congressional,
and the hard-g to soft-j drift traced from 1850 to a sitting court. No single
source assembles that with the errors flagged. The draft-handoff's original-work
sentence claims exactly this separation, and the article delivers it. After the
cuts the prose sits with the Forsyth and Liberman exemplars, dated facts placed
one after another, rather than a median summary. The headline holds as the
piece's largest claim.

## Edits

- Cut "The arithmetic showed the map's purpose." (slop signpost).
- Cut "The question has never fully closed." (slop signpost).
- Cut "with the Boston editor Benjamin Russell supplying the name" (named-person
  claim no cited source supports; nonessential).
