## The word and why it qualifies

"Gerrymander": to manipulate the boundaries of an electoral district to favor one
party or group. It clears the desk's bar the way the sphexish benchmark does. Its
origin is a specific, documented episode with named people and a real artifact: in
1812 the Massachusetts legislature, under Governor Elbridge Gerry, redrew state
senate districts to favor the Democratic-Republicans, and one Essex County district
was drawn in a long, contorted shape. A Boston newspaper ran a cartoon that added a
head, wings, and claws to the district's outline and labeled the creature the
"Gerry-mander", fusing the governor's surname with "salamander". The word stays
useful far beyond that election, now naming the practice wherever district lines
are drawn to rig an outcome. It carries a documented wrinkle worth telling: Gerry
pronounced his surname with a hard "g", yet the word settled into a soft "j"
sound, so the eponym is mispronounced relative to the man.

## What the article must do

Tell the documented story: the 1812 redistricting under Gerry, the salamander-
shaped Essex district, the newspaper cartoon that coined the term and roughly when
and where it ran, and how the word spread from that cartoon into general use. Trace
the development of meaning from the specific 1812 coinage to the present general
sense, and settle what is documented versus commonly asserted (the popular
attribution of the cartoon's drawing to the painter Gilbert Stuart, and the exact
newspaper and date, should be stated only as firmly as the record supports).
Ground the present sense in a real, cited example of modern use. Note the hard-g /
soft-j pronunciation history as the documented curiosity it is. Cite an
authoritative dictionary for the definition and first attestation. Keep etymology
as history, not proof of the present meaning, and do not pad a short lexical read
into a general history of American redistricting.

## Sources

## Required contribution

drawing district lines to rig a result), the true 1812 episode and cartoon behind
the word, what in the popular story is documented and what is merely repeated, and
the pronunciation wrinkle that the eponym has drifted from the man. The article
gets the coinage details right where popular versions blur them.
