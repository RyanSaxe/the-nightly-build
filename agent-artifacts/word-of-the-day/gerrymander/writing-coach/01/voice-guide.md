# Voice guide: word-of-the-day/gerrymander (01)

## How this piece should sound

At 550-800 words, the piece has room for the documented episode and the drift
in its meaning, not a general history of American redistricting or a survey
of every retelling. Forsyth's Seychelles and Colombia passages below show
what fits inside that room: a short run of dated, named facts, placed one
after another, with the irony sitting in the gap between two facts rather
than in a sentence that comments on them. Gerry's short time as governor, the
shape of the Essex County district, the newspaper, and the year can run the
same way. State them and leave them alone.

The commission's central task is separating what the record supports from
what popular retellings assert about the cartoon's authorship and origin,
and that is exactly Zimmer's material in the Upset piece. His opening names
the shape of the argument before making it: a story worth testing, not
retelling. The Thompson passage lets a named researcher and a quoted 1877
newspaper line carry the dating, instead of the writer asserting a date
outright. Where a popular attribution turns out to hold some truth, the way
Zimmer's closing credits the Upset legend, a specific and bounded credit tied
to a dated citation is available in place of either endorsing the popular
version whole or flatly rejecting it. That move may suit whatever the record
turns out to say about who drew the cartoon and where it ran.

The word's own history of meaning can sit in the piece the way Liberman lets
restive's reversed sense sit next to its dating: an exact bracket of years,
attached directly to the semantic fact it dates, with a concrete image doing
the proving rather than an abstract claim standing alone ("a restive horse
refuses to move" is the model). Gerrymander's own path, from one 1812
district to a general practice for rigging district lines, can be dated the
same way, a claim at a time, each one pinned to a year or a named source
rather than asserted loose.

The hard-g, soft-j pronunciation history is one checkable fact, not an
argument. Liberman's habit of stacking dated facts with nothing added between
them, visible in the Nares passage below, where the definition, the source,
and the year all arrive without commentary, fits a detail like that well. The
brief already rules out this desk's recent closer molds. Ending on one plain,
checkable fact about the word is an option the exemplars below support,
without needing a line that tells the reader what to make of it.

## Mark Forsyth, "Countries Named After People"

Source: https://blog.inkyfool.com/2025/10/countries-named-after-people.html

> "I recently discovered that the Seychelles were named after Jean Moreau de
> Séchelles, a Frenchman, who never set his gallic, garlicky, eyes upon the
> islands that bear his name."
>
> "The Seychelles were named in his honour because he happened to be the
> minister of finance when the islands were acquired by France in 1756. He
> had been in the post for a whole two years, and then he had a stroke and
> was replaced. His life was not extraordinary or eminent and yet he is on
> the map. He is a sovereign state. He has a seat at the UN."

The two paragraphs run as one build: first the single absurd fact (a man who
never saw the islands named after him), then four short declaratives that
climb from "a whole two years" to "a sovereign state" to "a seat at the UN"
without a single word of comment attached. The joke is entirely in the
sequence of facts and their escalating scale. Forsyth is visible in the
choice to end on the flattest possible sentence ("He has a seat at the UN")
rather than a bigger one. The writer trusts the fact to land on its own.

> "The Philippines were named in 1542 after Philip II of Spain. He enjoyed a
> bloody mary, who was Queen of England at the time. But they didn't have any
> children."

The pun works only because two specific, dated facts are already sitting in
the sentence: Philip II's marriage and his wife's nickname. Take out the
names and the joke disappears, which is the test for whether a joke belongs
in careful writing. The person shows in how little setup the joke needs.
Forsyth doesn't explain the pun, he just states it and moves to the next
country.

> "It didn't get that name till the C19th, and Christopher Columbus never
> visited the place. So the namer of so many islands, has become the name of
> the mainland."

Two facts (the naming came centuries after Columbus's life, and he'd named
many islands but never the continent that eventually took his name) are set
next to each other with a plain "So" between them, and the irony is left for
the reader to assemble. Nothing in the sentence tells the reader that this
is ironic.

## Ben Zimmer, "Debunking the Legend of 'Upset'"

Source: https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wordroutes/debunking-the-legend-of-upset/

> "Some stories about word origins recall the old Italian saying, se è non
> vero, è ben trovato: even if it is not true, it is well invented. One such
> too-good-to-check story involves the sporting usage of upset, which, it is
> said, came to be because an unfavored horse named Upset beat the great
> thoroughbred Man o' War."

The opening states the piece's stance before it argues for it: this is a
story the writer is about to test, not one he is about to retell as fact.
"It is said" is doing real work. It marks the claim as attributed to popular
tradition rather than to the writer, before a word of evidence has appeared.
The person is visible in the choice of an old proverb about invented-but-good
stories rather than a flat "this is a myth."

> "Thompson found unequivocal use of upset as a noun in horse-racing as early
> as 1877. In that year, The New York Times reported that a program at New
> Jersey's Monmouth Park racetrack 'indicates a victory for the favorite in
> each of the four events, but racing is so uncertain that there may be a
> startling upset.'"

A named researcher, a specific year, and the actual newspaper sentence do the
work here, not the writer's summary of what they show. Quoting the 1877 line
instead of paraphrasing it lets the reader check the claim directly against
the same evidence Zimmer used.

> "I did find a weaker version of the legend in a 1962 column by Washington
> Post sports columnist Bob Addie: 'The term "upset" in sports gained
> considerable stature back in 1919 when a horse actually named Upset beat
> the wonder horse, Man o' War.' That may in fact be true: certainly upset
> gained traction in sports reporting starting in the '20s, and Upset may
> have had something to do with that. So let's give some credit to the
> scrappy colt, even if he didn't achieve full-fledged eponym status."

Having shown the strong version of the legend is false, Zimmer doesn't close
by simply declaring the word a myth. He finds a weaker, dated, named claim
that survives scrutiny and grants it exactly that much credit, no more. The
verdict is sized to what the citation supports.

## Anatoly Liberman, "Restive people never rest"

Source: https://blog.oup.com/2026/06/restive-people-never-rest/

> "Restive, though it now means 'restless, fidgety,' once meant the opposite,
> namely, 'inactive, inert'! A restive horse refuses to move. The word goes
> back to the root of the Romance verb having the sense 'to remain in the
> same position,' from Latin restāre 'to rest.'"

The reversal in meaning is stated as a plain fact, and the very next sentence
gives a concrete picture that proves it before the paragraph moves on to the
word's root. "A restive horse refuses to move" pins an abstract claim about
semantic change to something a reader can see, in six words.

> "The most common sense today (that is, 'restless') surfaced in books only
> in the middle of the nineteenth century, that is, in Dickens's days! And we
> show surprise when unexpected semantic leaps are said to have occurred in
> the remotest past."

An exact bracket of centuries is attached directly to the meaning it dates,
not floated as a vague "eventually." The second sentence is a single aside
about how surprising the lateness of the shift is, attached right where the
fact sits rather than saved for a separate paragraph of reflection.

> "To set up one's rest 'to make up one's mind, to pause for rest, to halt.'
> Robert Nares explained this phrase in his indispensable 1822 book A
> Glossary; or, Collection of Words, Phrases, Names, and Allusions to
> Customs, Proverbs, &c. Thought to Require Illustration… From the game of
> primero, meaning 'to stand upon the cards you have in your hand, in the
> hope that they may win.'"

Three separate facts — the definition, the named source and its date, and
the phrase's origin in a card game — arrive one after another with no
sentence of commentary between them. The writer is visible in the choice to
name Nares and give the book's full, unwieldy title rather than write
"an early dictionary."
