# Evidence: word-of-the-day/gerrymander (01)

This record supports every fact the commission asks for: Merriam-Webster's
definition and 1812 first-known-use date; the 1812 Massachusetts redistricting
under Governor Elbridge Gerry, with Gerry documented as the signer, not the
author, of the map; the salamander-shaped Essex South senate district; the
cartoon that fused Gerry and salamander, its newspaper and date, and the
Tisdale-versus-Stuart attribution question; the hard-g / soft-j pronunciation
history, including a dated primary quotation; and a primary example of present
use. It is strong on the pronunciation history, on Gerry's limited role, and on
the fact that Gilbert Stuart's authorship is asserted rather than documented. It
is thin in two places, both recorded below: I did not read a primary newspaper
copy of the March 26, 1812 Boston Gazette issue (the date rests on consistent
secondary accounts and on the museum that holds the surviving broadside), and I
could not reach the Oxford English Dictionary entry (paywalled), so the
first-attestation year rests on Merriam-Webster alone. Two authoritative pages
I tried, the Library of Congress law blog and the Smithsonian NMAH object
record, returned 403 to every attempt and are listed under Discarded.

One caution the writer must not miss: the strongest primary example of modern
use, the Supreme Court's opinion in Rucho v. Common Cause, itself misstates the
1812 facts (it calls the districts "congressional" when they were state senate
districts). The word is used correctly there; the history it recites is not. Do
not let a modern citation launder the origin details this record settles.

## Contradictions

- **Who drew the cartoon.** Documented: Elkanah Tisdale, on the strength of a
  nineteenth-century memorandum John Ward Dean printed in the New England
  Historic and Genealogical Register (per MHS). Asserted but not confirmed:
  Gilbert Stuart and Washington Allston; MHS lists the cartoon as merely
  "attributed to" them, and Smithsonian's account finds the Stuart version less
  credible. The Stuart story (Stuart adds head, wings, and claws to a map on a
  newspaper office wall; editor Benjamin Russell answers "Better say a
  Gerrymander") is the popular retelling the record does not support. History.com
  even captions its image a "Gilbert Stuart cartoon," showing how routinely the
  attribution is repeated. The writer should credit Tisdale as the documented
  illustrator and flag Stuart as a common but unconfirmed attribution.

- **Who coined the word.** No single coiner is established. MHS says "several
  different Federalist opponents" are credited. Smithsonian's account names the
  poet Richard Alsop; the Stuart legend credits editor Benjamin Russell. Treat
  the coiner as unsettled; the portmanteau (Gerry + salamander) and its Federalist
  origin are what the record supports.

- **The newspaper and date.** Consistently reported as the Boston Gazette,
  March 26, 1812 (Smithsonian, quoting a historian; History.com confirms Boston
  Gazette, March 1812). This is well attested in secondary accounts but I did not
  read a primary copy of that newspaper issue. Note the surviving artifact MHS
  holds is a separate broadside imprinted "[Salem, Mass: 1812]," and MHS's own
  catalog does not tie the object to a specific newspaper issue. So "Boston
  Gazette, March 26, 1812" is the documented publication of the cartoon; the
  Salem broadside is a distinct printing.

- **Word first-attestation versus episode date.** These are different dates and
  should not be blurred. The redistricting law was signed February 11, 1812
  (MHS). The cartoon and coinage appeared March 26, 1812 (Smithsonian). The
  dictionary first-known-use year is 1812 (Merriam-Webster). The episode and the
  word's first attestation fall in the same year but are not the same event.

- **Rucho's history is wrong.** The Supreme Court opinion (a primary modern-use
  source) calls the 1812 districts "congressional." They were Massachusetts
  state senate districts (Essex South). Every historical source here agrees on
  state senate districts. Cite Rucho for present use, not for the 1812 facts.

- **Pronunciation.** Uncontested that Gerry used a hard g and that the word
  settled into a soft j. The only wrinkle is that this drift was already argued
  in 1850 (Indiana proceedings), so the soft-j form is not a recent corruption
  but a long-running shift.
