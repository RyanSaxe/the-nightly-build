# Voice guide — word-of-the-day/bowdlerize

## Directive

Write as a serious paper's short word essay: calm, exact, first-principles, and
fast. The reader is well-read and does not need to be told a word is interesting;
show it by choosing the concrete detail that makes it so. You have 550–800 words and
a word card doing the defining work up front, so the prose owes the reader story and
distinction, not a second definition.

Moves that will change sentences here:

- **Let one vivid documented fact carry each turn.** A retired physician, an
  uncredited sister, a title page's exact promise: name the specific thing and let it
  do the persuading. Do not gloss a fact you could quote.
- **Keep the eras clean.** Origin is history; present sense is a separate claim. Move
  from 1807 to now on a hinge the reader can see, never by blurring them into "over
  time."
- **Quote small and exactly.** A five-word phrase from a title page beats a sentence
  of paraphrase. Set the quotation, cite it, and get out.
- **Spend the ending on the distinction, not a moral.** The last section earns its
  place by drawing the line between this word and its neighbours, then stops. No
  summary, no lesson, no pointer onward.
- **Prefer the plain verb.** "Removed," "kept his name on it," "sold it as the play":
  the drama is in the acts, so report them and skip the adjectives that grade them.
- **One earned contrast is the ceiling.** The piece's whole job is a distinction, so
  spend the contrast where it counts (the neighbouring words) and nowhere decorative.

Compression rule: if a sentence restates the card, the last sentence, or a fact
already established, cut it whole rather than trim it.

`Recently used, do not reuse:` colon-subtitle headlines ("X: How Y…"); the "not X but
Y" / "X is not Y; it is Z" reflex; the disgrace-eponym framing used for *quisling*
(this is a censor's earnestness, not treachery); the recent word-of-the-day openers
and closers (kayfabe, quisling, limerence, mondegreen, solastalgia, petrichor,
apophenia, umwelt). Do not open on a definition the card already gives.

## Exemplars

## Mark Forsyth, "Releasing, Relaxing and Relishing Rhyme and Reason" (Inky Fool)
Source: https://blog.inkyfool.com/2025/10/releasing-relaxing-and-relishing-rhyme.html
Craft:
- cadence: short declarative opener, then a swerve; sentences stay light even when the
  root is technical.
- argument: one etymological thread pulled until it surprises, no throat-clearing.
- evidence: names the source language and form ("comes from the French relasser"),
  then tests it against ordinary use.
- stance: amused but exact; the humour rides on a real fact, never replaces it.
- notice: points at the everyday word the reader never questioned.
- diction: plain Anglo-Saxon verbs; the concrete example ("tigers and orcas and
  books") does the work.
- reader: treated as a curious equal, addressed through the material not at them.
- the move the axes miss: he earns delight by withholding the payoff one clause, so
  the origin lands as a small reveal rather than a lecture.
Calibration: "This seems strangely appropriate, as it really is what we do with
tigers and orcas and books."

## Kory Stamper, "The Voice of Authority: Morality and Dictionaries" (Harmless Drudgery)
Source: https://korystamper.com/2013/01/25/the-voice-of-authority-morality-and-dictionaries/
Craft:
- cadence: builds through worked examples, then drops to a flat declarative to land
  the claim ("It's a book that tells you how people use words").
- argument: descriptivist backbone; what a word does in use governs what it means.
- evidence: real public misuses of the dictionary, handled as cases not anecdotes.
- stance: sympathetic and skeptical at once toward how people wield word-authority.
- notice: catches the moment a reference tool gets mistaken for a moral verdict.
- diction: conversational verbs ("gets dragged") inside a rigorous frame.
- reader: trusted to follow a long setup to a short conclusion.
- the move the axes miss: she separates what a source records from what people wish it
  proved — exactly the etymology-vs-present-sense discipline this piece needs.
Calibration: "The dictionary is an authority, and so gets dragged into all manner of
arguments."

## Ben Zimmer, "The Hidden History of 'Glitch'" (Word Routes, Visual Thesaurus)
Source: https://www.visualthesaurus.com/cm/wordroutes/the-hidden-history-of-glitch/
Craft:
- cadence: opens on the word in today's news, then reaches back for the origin.
- argument: present usage first, history as the explanation, uncertainty flagged.
- evidence: cites the research and dates the trail; qualifies with "most likely"
  where the record is thin rather than faking certainty.
- stance: a reporter of words; the finding is the story, not his cleverness.
- notice: the gap between how confidently people use a word and how little they know
  its source.
- diction: precise, unshowy, the odd texture word ("glitchiness") used once.
- reader: assumed smart and busy; no filler before the substance.
- the move the axes miss: he binds a live contemporary hook to a documented past, the
  hinge this piece needs between the 1800s and now.
Calibration: "As it happens, some new research on the word brings its origin, most
likely from Yiddish, into a sharper perspective."
