# Editorial review: word-of-the-day/boondoggle (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: *boondoggle* reached national use not through the scoutmaster who is
usually credited with it, but through a 1935 relief-hearing witness and the
newspaper headline his testimony produced, and the word has named wasteful
public spending ever since while its cozy origin story fails against the print
record.

Claims it stands on, and how each held:

1. On April 3, 1935 a witness named Robert Marshall used "boondoggling" at a New
   York City Aldermanic hearing and could give the committee no clean
   definition. Held. Opened `#s2` (Zimmer/vocabulary.com): it carries the
   "Boon doggles is simply a term applied back in pioneer days to..." answer and
   the "Named for Daniel Boone?" / "No...It is boon doggles. It is spelled
   differently." exchange verbatim, both cited to `#s2`. The shorter "what we
   call gadgets today" rendering is cited to `#s3` (Quinion), which carries it.
   The article is honest that no stenographic transcript survives and presents
   the two renderings as two papers' paraphrases, not one fixed quote — exactly
   what the brief required.

2. The next day's New York Times headline put the `$3,187,000` figure into
   national circulation. Held for `$3,187,000`: `#s3` (Quinion) carries the
   headline verbatim, and the citation is correct. **Broke** on the very next
   figure: see Cut/Required work below. The `$19,658,512` denominator and the
   "about 16 percent" derivation are cited to `#s3`, which does not contain that
   figure.

3. The "coined by scoutmaster Robert Link" story is antedated by a July 1927
   Rochester clipping (in an unrelated sense) and a December 1927 troop
   newsletter already named *Boondoggle*, both predating the 1929 Jamboree, and
   Wilton judges Link's claim to "lack supporting evidence" but be "plausible."
   Held. Opened `#s4` (Wilton): all four elements are present verbatim,
   including the Rochester dating and Wilton's verdict, and the section presents
   the Link/Jamboree account as "the story usually told" (cited to `#s1`
   Merriam-Webster) before weighing it against the antedating. This is the
   round's main sourcing risk and the draft handles it honestly.

4. The word was contested from the start (an RNC pamphlet weaponizing it, FDR's
   Newark reclaim) and still names wasteful spending in 2026. Held. `#s2`
   carries the RNC "Frankly Destructive" pamphlet line and the FDR Newark quote;
   `#s5` (Daily Signal) resolves to the article whose headline and lede the piece
   quotes verbatim, dated August 12, 2026.

Display text: headline, dek, and all three subheads name actors and the
piece's own nouns; none is a scaffolding slot, none follows the recent word
pieces' origin-finding headline mold or "What the word names now" closer mold.
Dek states a claim about the world (the word launched into politics; critics
and FDR fought over it), supported by `#s2`. No colon-subtitle, no Betteridge
question. Witness named only "Robert Marshall," correctly dropping the
single-source "director of the practicum shop" title.

Every `data-nb-kind` audited against the primary/secondary test: `#s1`
Merriam-Webster primary (owns its definition/pronunciation/etymology note),
`#s2`/`#s3`/`#s4` secondary (etymology researchers reporting on the 1935 events
from outside), `#s5` primary (the artifact of present-day usage the article
documents). All five labels are correct. No OED citation slipped in; the
definition card cites Merriam-Webster only, as the brief required. All five
hrefs were opened as printed and each lands on the source itself.

## Cut

Ran the slop pass sentence by sentence and walked every edge alone. The prose
is clean. Openers are dated and named ("On April 3, 1935, a New York City
Aldermanic committee...heard a witness named Robert Marshall use a word none of
the aldermen recognized: boondoggling"), the reduce-to-placeholder test kills
nothing, and the wit sits in precise, checkable detail as the voice guide asked
("The next morning, the New York Times put a number on the room's laughter";
the blunt one-word "It wasn't." answering FDR's hope for the word). No negative
parallelism, no empty conclusions, no self-reference, no prompt leakage, no
borrowed phrasing from the voice-guide exemplars. Zero sentences failed the
slop test; no cuts were needed.

The closer ("The relief hearing's ax handles and belts, and the unfinished rail
line, are the same complaint at different scales: money visibly spent and
nothing delivered worth the cost") survives the delete test: it carries the
cross-era synthesis the two documented instances built, which is a reasoning
step, not a signpost. Its second half leans close to the definition card's
wording, but the "different scales" claim is the piece's own and earned.

One direct edit (see Edits): the newborn-son sentence attributed the quote to
two papers but cited only the source carrying one of them.

## Reader

Read straight through as the declared reader, the piece delivers a single short
narrative no one cited source gives whole: the word's national breakthrough came
from a hapless hearing witness and a headline, the tidy scoutmaster-origin story
is antedated two years by a clipping and a troop newsletter, and the word's
political life runs unbroken from 1935 to a 2026 rail line. That is synthesis
across Zimmer, Quinion, and Wilton, not restatement. The prose sits closer to
the voice-guide exemplars (Quinion, Zarevich) than to a median summary.

The catch: the draft handoff names the "about 16 percent" computation as the
piece's original work, and that is precisely the part that is unsourced (see
Required work). The article still earns its keep without it, but its *declared*
original contribution rests on a figure no cited source carries, so it cannot
publish as written.

## Edits

- Added `#s2` (Zimmer) alongside the existing `#s4` on the newborn-son sentence
  ("the Buffalo Evening News and the New York Herald Tribune both had him say
  ..."). `#s4` (Wilton) attributes that quote only to the Buffalo Evening News;
  the New York Herald Tribune telling is carried by `#s2`, which I confirmed on
  the page. The sentence now cites both papers it names.

## Required work

- **researcher.** The sentence "That figure was about 16 percent of the
  $19,658,512 New York City had budgeted for relief that month" cites `#s3`
  (worldwidewords.org), but that page carries only `$3,187,000` — the
  `$19,658,512` figure is absent there and also absent from `#s2` and `#s4`, the
  other prose sources. The evidence record's Numbers section wrongly lists
  `$19,658,512` as owned by "New York Times, 4 April 1935 headline (as read via
  worldwidewords.org)"; it is not on that page. The figure is genuinely part of
  the full NYT headline (the discarded NYT URL slug ends "...-play-19658512-
  voted-for"), but no accessible cited source reproduces that portion of the
  headline. Needed finding: an accessible, citable source that carries the
  `$19,658,512` total-April-relief figure, or an explicit confirmation it cannot
  be reached this run. Correct the evidence record either way.

- **writer.** If the researcher cannot source `$19,658,512`, cut the comparison
  and revise the sentence to stand on the sourced `$3,187,000` figure alone
  (this removes the flagged "original work" 16% computation; the piece survives
  on the rest). If the researcher sources it, re-cite the sentence to the source
  that actually carries the figure rather than `#s3`.

- **writer.** Quotation fidelity: the article prints "the faintly squirming
  wrinkly infant"; both source papers (`#s4` Buffalo Evening News, `#s2` Herald
  Tribune) print "the faintly squirming, wrinkly infant" with a comma. Restore
  the comma inside the quotation, or drop the quotation marks around that phrase.

## Decision

revise — an inline figure (`$19,658,512`) and the 16% computation built on it,
the piece's declared original work, are cited to a source that does not contain
the figure and are unsupported by any accessible cited source; the researcher
must source or confirm it and the writer must re-cite or cut.
