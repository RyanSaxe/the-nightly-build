# Evidence: word-of-the-day/boondoggle (researcher/01)

The record firmly establishes the definition, pronunciation, and the 1935 New
Deal episode that carried *boondoggle* into national use, with the NYT headline
and hearing testimony corroborated across two independently-researched
etymological accounts (Ben Zimmer's and David Wilton's) that draw on different
contemporaneous newspapers. It does **not** firmly establish the 1920s scoutcraft
coinage story as told: Robert H. Link's personal claim to have invented the word
surfaces only in April 1935 — six to eight years after the word's earliest print
appearances — in two mutually inconsistent versions, and the newest archival
research (2024) antedates the word's first print use to 1927 Rochester, before
Link says he coined it, in a sense (a "daunting task") unrelated to any leather
object. Present-day figurative use is solidly grounded in three independently
verified, dated 2025–2026 sources, one of them the letter of a private citizen
using the word exactly as the commission's angle requires. The OED could not be
read directly — it is subscription-gated and the login wall was confirmed, not
merely a transport failure — so OED-specific etymological dating in this record
comes only where a secondary source that itself consulted the print OED reports
it, always labeled as such.

## Contradictions

- **Exact wording of the April 3, 1935 testimony is not stable across
  contemporaneous papers.** Worldwidewords.org's version, attributed to the
  New York Times: "simply a term applied back in the pioneer days to what we
  call gadgets today." Vocabulary.com's version of what appears to be the same
  exchange: "Boon doggles is simply a term applied back in pioneer days
  to...things men and boys do that are useful in their everyday operations or
  recreations or about their home." Both agree on the pioneer-days/gadget
  framing and the same witness, but neither source's writer had access to a
  stenographic transcript — 1935 papers paraphrased hearing dialogue rather
  than quoting verbatim, so this should be reported as "papers at the time
  quoted him saying, in substance," not as a single fixed quotation.

- **The witness's exact title is confirmed in only one paper.** The Jersey
  Journal (5 April 1935, per Wilton) names him "Robert C. Marshall, director of
  the practicum shop." No other source consulted here independently confirms
  this title; Zimmer's account calls him only "a witness named Robert
  Marshall." Use the fuller title only if a second source can confirm it.

- **Two incompatible tellings of how Link says he coined the word, days
  apart.** Nevada State Journal (6 April 1935, syndicated "Hastings" column):
  Link coined it "out of the blue sky," with "absolutely no significance"
  beyond being "a good-looking addition to the uniform" — no baby story.
  Buffalo Evening News and NY Herald Tribune (8 April 1935): Link says the word
  came to him at his son's birth in 1926 and became the boy's nickname before
  he later applied it to the 1929 Jamboree lanyards. A third telling reportedly
  ran in a magazine called Word Study later in 1935 (noted by Quinion, not
  independently read here). These are not independent corroborations of one
  fact; they are three retellings from the same man within the same year, and
  Wilton's phrase — the claim "lacks supporting evidence" but is "plausible" —
  is the fairest summary the record currently supports.

- **The print record now antedates the Jamboree origin story by two years.**
  Quinion's account (last updated 2013) states flatly that no use of
  "boondoggle" is known "before 1929" and ties the word's birth to the
  Rochester scouts' presentation at that year's World Jamboree. Wilton's 2024
  research overturns this: a Rochester Democrat and Chronicle piece from 23
  July 1927 already uses "boondoggle" (for a daunting, unrelated task, not an
  object), and the word was already the name of a Rochester scout troop's own
  newsletter by 4 December 1927 — both roughly two years before the Jamboree
  and, in the newsletter case, a year before Link's son was even born under the
  telling that dates the coinage to 1926. This is the strongest evidence that
  the popular "coined by Link for the Jamboree lanyard" origin story, as
  usually told (including by Merriam-Webster's own "Did You Know?" note), is
  cleaner than what the documentary record actually shows. Merriam-Webster
  states the Link/Jamboree theory is the one "supported by evidence"; the
  primary-source antedating available since 2024 complicates that confidence
  more than M-W's public-facing note lets on.

- **A possible British precursor is unresolved.** Wilton notes the OED's own
  citation (which this research could not independently verify — see
  Discarded) dates the British scouting term "woggle" (a related object, a
  neckerchief slide) to a 9 June 1923 issue of Scout magazine, credited to
  Scoutmaster William Shankley — which, if genuinely a precursor, would predate
  every confirmed American use of "boondoggle" by four years. Wilton is
  explicit that this connection is unproven and the American term "could have
  developed independently." Recorded here as an open question, not a claim to
  put in the article as settled.
