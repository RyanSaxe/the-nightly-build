# Voice guide: word-of-the-day/boondoggle (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is the paper's shortest form, and every one of its 550-800 words has to
carry the story rather than introduce it. Quinion's "Boycott" gets to the
named land agent in its second sentence and never steps back to explain that
a word history is coming; this piece can open on the word's documented
episode the same way, trusting the card above it to have already done the
defining.

Let the humor come from precision, not from an inserted line. Quinion's "wrong
period and wrong country, I'm afraid" is funny because it is simply the
correct, blunt answer to a wrong guess — the wit sits in how exactly he
corrects the record, not in a separate joke bolted onto it. Where this
article's own documented episode has a similarly blunt, checkable detail, let
that detail do the work rather than commentary on top of it.

Zarevich's "serendipity" piece spends its last real paragraph on a dated,
named instance of the word in use — a film release, a director, a plot — before
one closing sentence contrasts it with how the word actually behaves. The
brief here asks for the present political sense to be shown in real, dated
usage rather than asserted from the etymology; this piece can close the same
way its own research allows, on a specific instance rather than a summary of
one.

Kelly's "rogue" piece treats a documented sense-shift as a sequence of dated
stages and anchors the abstract stages with one well-chosen period quotation
rather than several. This word has two separate documented settings to move
between rather than one continuous drift; each transition is a place for a
comparable single, specific citation to carry the turn, instead of a
transitional sentence asserting that the meaning changed.

## Michael Quinion, "Boycott"

Source: https://www.worldwidewords.org/qa-boy1.html

> "Wrong period and wrong country, I'm afraid. No one who organised a boycott
> at that time could have used the word, because it only appeared in the
> language in 1880. It's an excellent example of an eponym, a word based on a
> proper name, like wellington boots, garibaldi biscuits or the mackintosh."

This is the opening answer to a reader's mistaken guess, and it corrects the
record before it does anything else. The comparison to wellington boots and
garibaldi biscuits places the pattern in front of the reader immediately,
using things they already know rather than defining "eponym" in the abstract.
Quinion is visible in the flat "I'm afraid" — he treats a wrong guess as
worth a real answer, not a throat-clearing dismissal.

> "Labourers would not work for him, local shops stopped serving him (food had
> to be brought in from elsewhere for him and his family), and he even had
> great trouble getting his letters delivered. In the end, his crops were
> harvested that autumn through the help of fifty volunteers from the north of
> the country, who worked under the protection of nine hundred soldiers."

Every clause here is a separate, checkable fact — no shops, no post, no
harvest without armed help — and the two numbers at the end (fifty, nine
hundred) do the work that an adjective like "extreme" would have done more
weakly. Quinion never tells the reader the situation was severe; the count of
soldiers says it.

> "The events aroused so much passion that his name became an instant byword.
> It was first used — in our modern sense of collective and organised
> ostracism — in the *Times* of London in November 1880, even while his crops
> were still being belatedly harvested; within weeks it was everywhere."

The date and the source of first use sit right next to the claim they
support, so the reader can see exactly how "instant" the byword was. Quinion's
hand shows in the aside "even while his crops were still being belatedly
harvested" — he keeps two timelines (the man's ordeal, the word's spread) in
view at once instead of finishing one before starting the other.

## John Kelly, "An etymology goes 'rogue'"

Source: https://mashedradish.com/2016/12/16/an-etymology-goes-rogue/

> "The Oxford English Dictionary (OED) first documents rogue, as roge, in
> 1489, when it referred to a 'vagrant.' Over the course of the 16th century,
> the sense of rogue shifted. By the 1560s, it referred to a 'scoundrel,' by
> the 1590s an 'endearingly mischievous, rascally person,' and by the early
> 1600s an abusive term for a 'servant.'"

Kelly moves the word through four senses in four decades without ever writing
a sentence that just says "the meaning changed" — each decade gets its own
attested sense in its own words, so the drift is shown rather than announced.
The precision is in refusing to average the four senses into one description.

> "Rogue was a favorite of Shakespeare: He used rogue, to various degrees of
> insult and endearment, over 100 times in his plays. Perhaps the most famous
> example comes from Prince Hamlet: 'O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I!'"

One citation carries the claim instead of several — Kelly picks the single
most recognizable instance rather than piling up examples to prove the word
was common. The count ("over 100 times") establishes scale before the one
quotation makes it concrete.

> "Rogue elephant, as the OED observes, may have been influenced by a phrase
> in Sinhalese, spoken in Sri Lanka: hora aliyā, 'thievish or restive
> elephant.' Rogue, originally, indeed decried thieves. And in the beginning
> of the 1800s, rogue was also referring to wayward horses. Underlying these
> senses of rogue, then, is an idea of 'trickiness' and 'unruliness,' whether
> of petty criminals or strong-willed beasts."

Kelly names a specific competing derivation and attributes it to the OED
rather than presenting it as his own guess, then draws the connecting idea
("trickiness," "unruliness") out of the specific senses he's just given
instead of asserting it first. The reasoning is visible in the order: facts,
then the idea they support.

## Emily Zarevich, "What Is Serendipity?"

Source: https://daily.jstor.org/what-is-serendipity/

> "'I define serendipity as the art of making an unsought finding,' writes Pek
> Van Andel in *The British Journal for the Philosophy of Science.* There
> appear to be endless scenarios to which this unusual word can be applied:
> getting lost on the highway, which leads to a new friendship made at a pit
> stop; mixing up ingredients in a recipe and discovering a surprisingly
> delightful new flavor combination; setting out to invent one thing but
> ending up with the invention of another; falling in love not with your
> blind date but with the server at the restaurant instead."

Zarevich opens on a sourced, attributed definition rather than her own
paraphrase, then makes the abstract definition concrete with a run of small,
ordinary examples instead of one grand one. The examples get more specific as
the sentence goes, ending on the most particular of the four.

> "On January 28, 1754, Walpole wrote a letter to his good friend Horace Mann.
> Mann, using his wealth and connections, had secured a portrait of Bianca
> Cappello, a late Grand Duchess of Tuscany, as a gift for Walpole. Walpole,
> who loved everything to do with Italian history, cherished the painting. He
> went on a research spree one day and unexpectedly found a Capello family
> crest that incorporated the fleur-de-lis of the Medici family into which
> Bianca had married."

The exact date opens the paragraph before anything else, and every person in
it is named and given a reason to be there — the gift, the crest, the
marriage. Zarevich is visible in how much ordinary domestic detail she keeps
(a friend, a gift, a hobby) around a moment that could have been reported as
just "a coinage."

> "The word gained popularity after the release of the 2001 romantic comedy
> film *Serendipity,* directed by Peter Chelsom. The film follows a couple who
> meet by chance and feel an instant attraction to one another. Though they're
> both already in relationships, they conduct a series of small
> chance-reliant tests to determine if they're meant to be together. This
> premise is, of course, scripted. In real life, serendipity only happens when
> you're not waiting for it or trying to force it."

This is the piece's closing move: a dated, named, real instance of the word
in circulation, not a restatement of its origin. The last sentence draws a
contrast, but it's earned by the four sentences of plot summary just given —
the reader has just watched the scripted version and can check the contrast
against it.
