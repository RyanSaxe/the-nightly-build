Paper of the Day

Goodfellow's GAN Paper Named Its Own Failure Mode and Never Fixed It

A fair-budget study three years later found that none of the fixes proposed since consistently outperforms the non-saturating loss the paper itself patched in when its proven objective wouldn't train.

11 min read2026-08-02

Generative Adversarial Nets

Ian J. Goodfellow, Jean Pouget-Abadie, Mehdi Mirza, Bing Xu, David Warde-Farley, Sherjil Ozair, Aaron Courville, Yoshua Bengio · arXiv:1406.2661 (NIPS 2014) · 2014

Read the paper
We propose a new framework for estimating generative models via an adversarial process, in which we simultaneously train two models: a generative model G that captures the data distribution, and a discriminative model D that estimates the probability that a sample came from the training data rather than G. The training procedure for G is to maximize the probability of D making a mistake. This framework corresponds to a minimax two-player game. In the space of arbitrary functions G and D, a unique solution exists, with G recovering the training data distribution and D equal to 1/2 everywhere. In the case where G and D are defined by multilayer perceptrons, the entire system can be trained with backpropagation. There is no need for any Markov chains or unrolled approximate inference networks during either training or generation of samples. Experiments demonstrate the potential of the framework through qualitative and quantitative evaluation of the generated samples.1

A Two-Model Alternative to Markov Chains

Before 2014, training a deep generative model meant a Markov chain that mixed slowly or an approximate-inference network that traded accuracy for tractability. Goodfellow and seven coauthors at the Université de Montréal proposed a different route: pit two multilayer perceptrons against each other and train both with backpropagation alone, no Markov chain and no unrolled inference network anywhere in the loop.1 A generator G maps a noise vector to a sample. A discriminator D looks at a sample and estimates the probability it came from the training data rather than from G. G's only goal is to make D wrong as often as possible. The paper's claim is that this contest has one answer: at the game's equilibrium, G's output distribution equals the data distribution exactly, and D can do no better than a coin flip.

The Value Function and the Rule for Training It

A contest needs one shared score, a single quantity D pushes up and G pushes down. The paper calls it the value function V(D,G) and sets the game up as a minimax problem over it:

G wants to shrink the second term, since that is the only piece it can touch; D wants to grow both. Training alternates between the two: k steps of gradient ascent on D holding G fixed, then one step of gradient descent on G holding D fixed. The paper treats k as a hyperparameter and reports using the cheapest setting in its own runs, k=1, one discriminator update per generator update.1

The Optimal Discriminator

Before asking what G should do, the paper first asks a narrower question: for a fixed G, what is the best D can do? Call that discriminator D*_G. Proposition 1 gives it exactly:

D*_G(x) is a probability: the share of the probability mass at x that comes from real data rather than from the generator, out of the mass the two together put there. Where p_g already matches p_data closely at x, that share sits near one half, and D cannot do better than guessing. Where the two diverge, D*_G(x) drifts toward 0 or 1 in the direction of whichever distribution actually put mass there.

The Unique Equilibrium

Substitute D*_G back into V and only G is left. Call that C(G) = V(D*_G, G): what G actually minimizes once D has already found its best response. Theorem 1 states its minimum plainly: "the global minimum of the virtual training criterion C(G) is achieved if and only if p_g = p_data. At that point, C(G) achieves the value −log 4."1 Algebraically, the criterion reduces to a known distance between the two distributions:

G and D here range "in the space of arbitrary functions," not the two multilayer perceptrons with a fixed depth and a finite set of weights that any actual training run uses.1 The proof is about functions; the code that runs it optimizes parameters.

The Fix Baked Into the Paper Itself

The proof above describes where the objective bottoms out. It says nothing about how quickly G gets there, and the paper admits the practical problem directly: "equation [1] may not provide sufficient gradient for G to learn well."1 Early in training, G's samples are easy to reject. D assigns them a low probability of being real, D(G(z)) sits near 0, and the term G is minimizing, log(1−D(G(z))), sits on a plateau close to zero gradient. The paper's fix keeps the same two players and the same D but swaps what G optimizes: "rather than training G to minimize log(1−D(G(z))) we can train G to maximize log D(G(z))."1 Near the point where the old loss saturates, log D(G(z)) still has a steep slope, and the paper reports the new objective provides "much stronger gradients early in learning."1 Theorem 1 says nothing about this new objective. The paper adopted it as a practical substitute and never re-derived an equivalent guarantee for it.

The Failure the Paper Already Named

Section 6 of the paper, "Advantages and disadvantages," lists what adversarial nets cannot yet do. Two sentences name a specific risk:

Section 6, in the paper's words
there is no explicit representation of p_g(x), and... D must be synchronized well with G during training (in particular, G must not be trained too much without updating D, in order to avoid "the Helvetica scenario" in which G collapses too many values of z to the same value of x to have enough diversity to model p_data).1 Goodfellow et al., "Generative Adversarial Nets," 2014

The field's later name for this is mode collapse: the generator settles on a small number of outputs regardless of its input. The paper coined its own name first, in 2014, and offered one countermeasure: keep D and G synchronized, without saying how. Two years later, Goodfellow's own NIPS tutorial keeps the same name for the same failure: "Mode collapse, also known as the Helvetica scenario, is a problem that occurs when the generator learns to map several different input z values to the same output point."2 Independent of both papers, an outside explainer of the same period uses the same word for the same behavior: "the generator may collapse to a setting where it always produces same outputs. This is a common failure case for GANs, commonly referred to as Mode Collapse."3 What later work added was not the discovery of a failure mode the 2014 paper missed. It explained why the paper's one countermeasure was the only lever available. Salimans and coauthors, including Goodfellow himself, spell it out. "Because the discriminator processes each example independently, there is no coordination between its gradients, and thus no mechanism to tell the outputs of the generator to become more dissimilar to each other," they write.4 Instead, "all outputs race toward a single point that the discriminator currently believes is highly realistic."4

Where the Proof's Assumptions Broke in Practice

Theorem 1 needs an optimal discriminator evaluated over the full space of functions, and it says nothing about disjoint supports. Real image data does not fill its ambient pixel space; it concentrates on a low-dimensional manifold, and so does whatever a generator with far fewer parameters than pixels can produce. Arjovsky and Bottou's companion paper works out what the proof leaves unaddressed: when p_data and p_g sit on manifolds that do not align, "there exists an optimal discriminator D* that has accuracy 1 and ∇_x D* = 0 for almost any x" on both.5 A discriminator can be perfect and useless in the same breath: right about every sample, with a gradient of zero almost everywhere it looks. Their Theorem 2.4 turns that into a bound on training itself. As D approaches optimal, the gradient the generator receives from the original objective shrinks toward zero. The bound is exact: \|\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p(z)}[\log(1-D(g_\theta(z)))] \|_2 < \dfrac{M\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}. Here ε measures how close D is to optimal, and M is a constant.5 The better D gets, which is exactly the direction training pushes it, the tighter that bound squeezes G's gradient toward zero. The same point, worked from the objective rather than the theorem: "when the discriminator is perfect, we are guaranteed with D(x) = 1... therefore the loss function L falls to zero and we end up with no gradient to update the loss during learning iterations."3

Arjovsky, Chintala, and Bottou's WGAN paper, published two weeks later by two of the same authors, replaces the objective rather than patching around this bound. It substitutes the Earth-Mover distance for the Jensen-Shannon divergence Theorem 1 minimizes: W(\mathbb{P}_r,\mathbb{P}_g) = \inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mathbb{P}_r,\mathbb{P}_g)} \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\gamma}[\|x-y\|]. The paper proves this distance converges under sequences where JS, KL, and reverse KL do not.6 On mode collapse, the paper reports one direct result: "in no experiment did we see evidence of mode collapse for the WGAN algorithm."6 That is a claim about the experiments WGAN's authors ran, not a proof that the Earth-Mover objective forecloses mode collapse everywhere. The paper does not claim the second.

What the Record Shows After

WGAN changed what training measures and how reliably a practitioner can watch it. It did not, by itself, settle how good the finished samples look under a fair test. Lucic, Kurach, Michalski, Gelly, and Bousquet ran that test directly: the same compute budget and the same hyperparameter search, across models and datasets. They scored every run by Fréchet Inception Distance, the distance between the statistics of real and generated image embeddings, where lower is better.7

NS-GAN is the non-saturating fix, not a later variant. It wins MNIST outright while losing CIFAR-10 to WGAN-GP by three points, and no row wins all four columns. The paper's own conclusion is exact: "we did not find evidence that any of the tested algorithms consistently outperforms the non-saturating GAN introduced in [9]." Reference 9 is Goodfellow's 2014 paper. The same paper frames the result against a compute budget: "most models can reach similar scores with enough hyperparameter optimization and random restarts... improvements can arise from a higher computational budget and tuning more than fundamental algorithmic changes."7 This does not undo what WGAN changed. WGAN's contribution was a gradient signal that survives near convergence and a distance estimate that tracks sample quality where the original discriminator's loss does not.6 Lucic's finding is about the sample quality a well-tuned model reaches at the end, not about how reliably training gets there. The two results answer different questions, and neither rebuts the other.

Seven years after the paper, a different objective displaced GANs for much of image generation. Dhariwal and Nichol's guided diffusion model, ADM-G, beat the strongest published GAN, BigGAN-deep, on the same benchmark at every resolution they tested:8

Its best configuration, adding classifier guidance and upsampling, reaches FID 3.94 at 256×256 and 3.85 at 512×512, different numbers from the plain comparison above, using machinery the ADM-G row does not.8 The same paper reports matching BigGAN-deep with as few as 25 sampling passes, while covering the data distribution more completely.8

Verdict

The 2014 paper proved exactly what it claimed: for a fixed generator, an optimal discriminator exists in closed form, and substituting it turns the game into a Jensen-Shannon divergence with a single, provable minimum at p_g = p_data.1 That proof holds without qualification inside the space of arbitrary functions the abstract names. It assumed an optimal discriminator reachable by training, sufficient capacity in both networks, and a game played in that function space rather than the finite-dimensional parameter space every real network occupies. Practice supplied none of the three by default. Arjovsky and Bottou's Theorem 2.4 shows why a near-optimal discriminator on real data starves the generator of gradient.5 The paper's own Section 6 admits mode collapse as a known risk with no fix beyond hand-tuned synchronization.1 Lucic et al.'s fair-budget study found no successor consistently better once compute is held constant.7 What the field added was not a repair of the original proof. It was a new objective that survives where the JS-divergence proof does not, and eventually a different generative family that left the adversarial game behind for the tasks that mattered most.68
