Opinion

The childhood vaccine schedule runs through a committee. The order goes around it.

The 10 August order cuts the diseases recommended for every child from 18 to 11, reserving the rest for high-risk groups or a doctor's case-by-case call.

8 min read2026-08-12
This paper's position Position

Cutting the childhood vaccine schedule by executive order is wrong on the two grounds that decide such a policy. It goes around the statutory body the schedule is set through, and the vaccine-autism claim its signers gave for it is one the evidence has closed.

The American Academy of Pediatrics says the science of vaccines has not changed.1 Bill Cassidy, a Republican physician and Senate committee chair, calls the order wrong.2 And a federal court has already blocked the administration's earlier attempt to rewrite this schedule outside that body.3

Who actually sets the schedule

The childhood immunization schedule is not the President's to write, and it is not really the CDC director's either. It is built by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, a panel established in 1964 under Section 222 of the Public Health Service Act and governed by the Federal Advisory Committee Act.45 The committee reviews the evidence for each vaccine, votes, and forwards its recommendations to the CDC director, who decides whether to adopt them. Only then are they published in the agency's Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report.5

That review is not a formality, because federal law ties coverage to its output by name. The Affordable Care Act requires private health plans to cover the committee's recommended immunizations with no cost sharing, under Section 2713 of the Public Health Service Act.65 The Vaccines for Children program, which supplies shots to families who cannot afford them, is bound to the committee by Section 1928 of the Social Security Act, and the committee itself sets that program's covered list.758

So the panel that weighs the evidence is the same panel the money follows. A shot moved off the recommended list can lose its no-cost guarantee and its place in the Vaccines for Children supply, even when no evidence review found it unsafe.8

How the order goes around it

On 10 August the President signed "Delivering Gold Standard Childhood Vaccine Recommendations for Americans."9 It sorts childhood vaccines into three categories: recommended for all children, recommended for certain high-risk groups, and left to shared clinical decision-making.9 The universal list falls to eleven diseases, which the White House fact sheet describes as "a decrease from the 18 diseases recommended by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in 2024."10 The order also directs that the combined MMR shot be split into three separate single-disease injections once such products are made domestically, and that vaccines be given at separate visits.910

The text is careful about its own reach. It tells agencies to act "to the fullest extent allowable by law," tasks an HHS panel with a 90-day report, and stops short of purporting to amend the committee's schedule itself.9 A separate section instructs the Attorney General to bring "meritorious legal actions" against state vaccine laws.11 Read narrowly, the order asks agencies to do something. It does not, by its own words, make the schedule become something.

The administration has already tried the direct version, and a court stopped it.

The categories and the labels are the committee's own. The defect is the route. A policy the statutes send through evidence review and CDC adoption was instead set by assignment from the top, months after a court found the agency version of it likely unlawful.

Measles cases in the United States in 2026 already exceed the annual total of any of the past 35 years, and the country is close to losing its measles elimination status.2 Two healthy American children died of measles in the past year, according to the pediatrician Aaron Milstone.1 Spreading the same shots across more visits, the academy's president Andrew Racine warned, raises the barriers that keep children from finishing a series.2

The claim its signers made off the page

The order's text does not mention autism. Neither does the White House fact sheet. Both argue peer-nation alignment and parental choice.910 The autism claim was made out loud at the signing. The President tied the action to "many subjects, but including autism in particular," and Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the health secretary, put autism prevalence at one child in 31 and said, "It has to be an environmental exposure, because genes don't cause epidemics."12

The record on MMR and autism
  • The largest test followed 657,461 Danish children for more than a decade. It found a fully adjusted autism hazard ratio of 0.93 (95% CI, 0.85 to 1.02) between MMR-vaccinated and unvaccinated children, with no raised risk even among children whose siblings were autistic.13
  • Reviewing more than a thousand studies in 2011, the Institute of Medicine concluded that the evidence "favors rejection of a causal relationship" between MMR and autism.14
  • The 1998 paper that began the scare, a case series of twelve children,15 was fully retracted by The Lancet in 2010, after a UK medical panel found its author had acted dishonestly.16

A careful defender will answer that the President's ad-libs are not the policy, and that the written order rests on peer-nation numbers instead. The order's own instructions answer back. Splitting MMR into three shots, and adding alternative adjuvants to the task force's charge, are not peer-nation practices. They are the specific preoccupations the secretary and the President voiced at the signing.912 Spacing shots across separate visits tracks the same complaint, aired at the signing, about "72 jabs" and "five separate visits" in a child's first year.1012 The document was written to carry the program of the remarks, even where the rationale behind that program stayed off the page.

The case for a leaner, consent-first schedule

The strongest case for the order needs no autism claim at all. It runs like this. The United States really did recommend against more diseases than its peers. Across 20 peer countries the average was 13.6 diseases; the American list stood at 18.17 A list that long invites a fair question: does every shot on it clear a favorable risk-benefit bar for every child? "Shared clinical decision-making" is not a euphemism coined for this order. It is a category the committee created and uses, for vaccines a given person may benefit from when the population-level benefit is uncertain.18 And the ethics literature treats informed consent as foundational: parents have a genuine claim to weigh a particular vaccine with their own physician.19 On this reading, the order returns judgment to the exam room and trims a schedule that had grown longer than anyone else's.

Each of those points is true, and none of them reaches the order's method. The peer comparison is real, and it also cuts the other way. The honest version of the same count puts the pared-down American list at 11 diseases, below all but one of those 20 peers. Peer schedules differ because disease burden and health systems differ. The epidemiologist who ran the Danish autism study has said exactly that of this comparison.1317 "More than peers" is not a finding that any particular vaccine fails its risk-benefit test, which is the judgment the committee exists to make.18

Shared clinical decision-making cuts hardest against the order, precisely because it is the committee's category. The way to move a vaccine into it is an evidence review and a vote, not a line in an executive order.18 Grant that centralized rule-setting has real failure modes. Routing around the committee is still worse than any failure its own process has produced, because that process is what confers the coverage and the evidence base in the first place.518 Informed consent argues for good information from a trusted physician. It does not argue for changing what the schedule recommends by fiat, and the same ethicists who ground the consent claim hold that parental autonomy weakens as the duty to sustain herd immunity goes unmet.19

What an ACIP vote would settle

There is a clean version of nearly everything the order says it wants. If the committee took up the schedule's length, reviewed the evidence for each shot, and voted some vaccines into the high-risk or shared-decision categories, that would be the schedule doing its job, and this paper's objection would fall.18 If a court upheld the January change as consistent with the Administrative Procedure Act and the Federal Advisory Committee Act, the process ground would go with it.5

On the autism ground the bar is just as concrete: a study of the size and rigor of the Danish cohort, finding the association that review after review has failed to find.13 Nothing said at the signing came close.

Verdict

Judged by the two tests that decide a schedule change, the order fails both. It advances the causal reason its signers gave, one the evidence has closed, and it sets the schedule by an authority the statutes never handed it. The repair runs through the committee the schedule already uses: an evidence review and a vote.518
