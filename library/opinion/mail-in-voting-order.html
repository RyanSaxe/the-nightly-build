The Administration Skips the Merits in Its Own Supreme Court Filing

On the emergency docket, the Solicitor General asks the justices to let the blocked provisions of Executive Order 14399 take effect for the November midterms without defending their constitutionality.

8 min read2026-08-01
This paper's position Position

The Elections Clause puts authority over who may vote and how mail ballots move in a federal election with the states, not the President, so the courts were right to block Executive Order 14399 and the Supreme Court should leave it blocked.

Twenty-three states and the District of Columbia sued to stop the order.1 Judge Indira Talwani found its citizenship-list and mail-ballot sections “legally void.”1 The First Circuit's majority, Judges Gelpí and Rikelman, agreed: no statute the government cited lets the President compel a state citizenship list or set mail-ballot rules for a federal election.2 The plaintiffs' counsel of record on that order include Attorneys General Rob Bonta of California, Letitia James of New York, and Kwame Raoul of Illinois.2

The Constitution names who decides this

Article I gives Congress and the states power over federal elections, not the President. “The times, places and manner of holding elections for Senators and Representatives, shall be prescribed in each state by the legislature thereof; but the Congress may at any time by law make or alter such regulations, except as to the places of choosing Senators.”3 The presidency does not appear in that sentence. Judge Talwani read the omission as controlling: Executive Order 14399's citizenship-list and mail-ballot provisions are “legally void” because they regulate exactly what the clause commits to state legislatures and Congress, and the order comes from neither.1 Her order also invoked the Electors Clause, which gives state legislatures, not the President, the power to direct how a state chooses its presidential electors, closing off the other half of a federal election the same way.1

The First Circuit did not need to look past that gap. Denying a stay of Talwani's injunction on July 25, 2026, Judges Gelpí and Rikelman held that the federal defendants had failed to identify any statute authorizing the President to compel a state citizenship list or dictate mail-ballot handling rules for a federal election.2 That is the same failure that had already sunk the government's request for a stay in the district court on July 7, when Talwani applied the same four-factor standard and found no likelihood of success on the merits.4 Without a clause or a statute behind it, the President's signature is not enough.

The order gives two different commands

Executive Order 14399, signed March 31, 2026, does two different things.5 Section 2 directs the Social Security Administration and U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services to compile a state-by-state citizenship list for state election officials and tells the Justice Department to prioritize noncitizen-voting prosecutions. Section 3 is a separate command: it directs the U.S. Postal Service to write new rules for mail-ballot envelope markings, list requirements, and delivery and return conditions in federal elections.5 A third piece, Section 5, adds enforcement and record-retention language, but Talwani found it “merely precatory,” a command with no independent force, so there was nothing there to enjoin.1

Talwani permanently enjoined the federal defendants, not the President personally, from enforcing Sections 2 and 3 against the 23 plaintiff states, the District of Columbia, and Pennsylvania's governor, for the November 3, 2026 election “or any earlier federal election.”1 A separate ripeness ruling on June 18 drew that boundary: claims about elections after November 3, 2026 were dismissed without prejudice as too speculative to decide now, while the claim about the coming midterms stayed live.6 Twenty-seven states are not covered, and neither are elections after that date.

The block covers 23 states, not the country

Twenty-three states and the District of Columbia sued; twelve states, led by Missouri, intervened on the government's side and separately asked the Supreme Court to let the order take effect.7 The First Circuit split 2-1 to keep the injunction in place.2

That head count does not decide a constitutional question. It does show that the states themselves, the officials the Elections Clause put in charge of federal elections, disagree sharply about how far federal power should reach into that job.3

The order's defenders don't agree on why it should survive

The administration's actual position at the Supreme Court is narrower than the order itself. Solicitor General D. John Sauer's application does not defend Executive Order 14399's constitutionality. His own filing states that the government “does not defend the legality of the EO” on the merits.8 Instead, it argues Talwani and the First Circuit should never have reached the merits at all. Under Trump v. New York and Clapper v. Amnesty International USA, a federal court cannot rule on a policy's legality before it causes a concrete, non-speculative injury.8 Sauer argues the plaintiff states have not shown one yet. Article III's injury requirement exists precisely to keep courts out of disputes that might resolve themselves once a policy is actually applied.

The merits defense that Sauer's application skips does exist in the record, just not from the administration itself. Missouri Attorney General Catherine Hanaway and eleven co-intervenor states, in their own July 28 stay application, frame Executive Order 14399 as “promoting election integrity” and argue the federal government has a legitimate interest in accurate citizenship data for federal elections.7 They separately argue the question is “certworthy” because of a split with the D.C. Circuit's handling of a related Postal Service rulemaking dispute.7 Judge Dunlap, concurring in part and dissenting in part from the First Circuit's order, goes further. Verifying citizenship through Social Security and immigration records is, in his view, a longstanding federal function distinguishable from regulating ballot mechanics, and the balance of harms favors letting that narrower data-sharing piece proceed while the merits are litigated.2 Of everyone defending the order, Dunlap is the only one who answers the Elections Clause question on its own terms instead of arguing around it.

Both defenses have real gaps. Sauer's standing argument runs into Talwani's own June 18 ripeness ruling, which already separated the speculative claims from the live ones. It dismissed challenges to elections after November 3, 2026 as too remote to decide, and kept the challenge to the coming midterms alive because that harm is no longer speculative.6 A district court and a circuit majority both found it concrete enough to reach the merits, under the same injury standard Sauer now invokes.1 He is asking the Supreme Court to relitigate a question the lower courts already answered.

Dunlap's narrower defense fails for a different reason. Distinguishing citizenship verification from ballot mechanics does not change who decides whether that list becomes part of a federal election in the first place. The Elections Clause commits that choice to state legislatures and Congress, not to an executive order directing federal agencies to hand states a file and expect them to use it.3 Nothing in the record identifies a statute authorizing what Section 2 actually does: direct federal agencies to compile a citizenship list for state election officials.5 A federal interest in accurate data is not federal authority to dictate how states act on it.

What would flip this judgment is specific. A Supreme Court holding that Sections 2 and 3 fall within power Congress already delegated to the executive branch, through a statute this litigation has not surfaced, would do it. So would Congress passing one: a law authorizing federal agencies to compile a citizenship list for state election officials, or authorizing the Postal Service to set mail-ballot handling rules. Either would move the question from the Elections Clause to ordinary statutory interpretation, where the executive branch's hand is far stronger.3 Nothing before the Supreme Court right now does either. The Solicitor General's application asks the Court to postpone the question, not answer it, and postponing it does not make the President's authority any more real.
