The Fed Held Rates 9-3 in July. Three of Its Own Presidents Wanted to Hike.

Lorie Logan and two fellow regional Fed presidents dissented from Kevin Warsh's 9-3 hold, arguing that five years of above-target inflation demands a rate increase, while economists such as Mark Zandi and Janet Yellen call the inflation a tariff-and-energy supply shock no hike can fix.

10 min read2026-07-31

A 9-3 Vote to Hold, After Five Years Above Target

On July 29, 2026, the Federal Open Market Committee voted 9 to 3 to hold the federal funds rate at its target range of 3-1/2 to 3-3/4 percent, "in support of the Federal Reserve's dual mandate."1 The dual mandate is the Fed's twin statutory goals, stable prices and maximum employment, and the two sometimes pull policy in opposite directions. Kevin Warsh had taken the oath of office as Fed Chairman on May 22, 2026, and was unanimously selected FOMC chairman the same day.2 He chaired this meeting and its press conference.3 It was the Committee's fifth consecutive meeting holding the rate.4 Three regional bank presidents dissented: Beth Hammack of Cleveland, Neel Kashkari of Minneapolis, and Lorie Logan of Dallas, each preferring to raise the target range by a quarter point.1 Independent analysis called it the most fractured hawkish dissent the Committee has produced since September 2016: the first time since then that three policymakers dissented with a unified view on which direction rates should move.5

The dissent split over a fact both sides accept. Inflation has stayed above the Fed's 2 percent target for what Logan calls "more than five years."6 Warsh, recalling a figure from six weeks earlier, put it at 63 months: "I said something like 63 months of inflation above target. I didn't say 64, though the final calculation might be a close one."3 Consumer prices rose 3.5 percent over the twelve months through June 2026, and 2.6 percent excluding the volatile food and energy categories.7 The energy index alone was up 15.7 percent over the year, even after a seasonally adjusted 5.7 percent one-month drop tied to a brief Middle East ceasefire.7 The measure the Fed actually targets, the personal consumption expenditures price index, tells a similar story: up 4.1 percent over the twelve months through May, 3.4 percent at its core.8 Both figures match what Logan cited in her own account of the dissent.6

The Committee's own account of why prices are elevated names one culprit directly: "supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain sectors, including energy."1 That is the statement's entire attribution. It does not mention tariffs. Warsh's own spoken opening remarks cast a wider net: "strained supply chains arising from the pandemic, military conflicts, energy-supply disruptions, substantial increases in tariff rates, and yes, the surge in A.I.-related investment."3 Those are the shocks the Committee says it is weighing. Logan's own speech names the same two headline drivers Warsh's remarks add to the statement's list: "tariffs and conflict in the Middle East."6 Outside estimates put numbers on the mix. Moody's Analytics attributes about 0.66 percentage points of its forecast for year-end inflation to the Iran-linked energy shock and about 0.17 points to tariffs.9 A third factor, AI-driven data-center demand, is worth roughly another quarter point.9 Energy is doing more of the work in that breakdown than tariffs are, even though the two enter the political conversation as a pair.

Two ideas do most of the work in the disagreement. The real interest rate is the policy rate minus expected inflation, the figure that actually restrains borrowing and spending. A nominal rate that holds still while expected inflation falls is passively getting tighter. One that holds still while expected inflation rises is passively getting looser. A relative-price or supply shock is a price increase concentrated in specific goods, such as energy or tariffed imports, rather than a broad rise driven by demand outrunning the economy's capacity to supply it. The stakes turn on a third idea: expectations anchoring, the public's confidence that inflation will return to target regardless of where it sits today. Once that confidence breaks, workers and firms start building higher inflation into wages and contracts, which can make the higher rate self-fulfilling. Whether five-plus years above target has already put that anchor at risk, and whether a quarter-point move now would do anything about it, is where the Committee split.

Finish the job now

Inflation has stayed above the Fed's 2 percent target for more than five years. The three dissenters who voted for a hike do not think the current policy rate is tight enough to close that gap, and a modest quarter-point increase now, they argue, is cheaper than the sharp increase an entrenched overshoot would eventually force.

Lorie Logan, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, laid out the fullest public case for a hike thirteen days before she cast her dissenting vote. Over the twelve months through May, she noted, PCE inflation ran 4.1 percent, and core PCE, which sets aside food and energy, ran 3.4 percent, up four-tenths of a point since December.6 Her own reading of where that trajectory is headed is not encouraging: "my best judgment is that inflation appears to be heading toward the mid 2's, not all the way back to 2 percent."6 June's cooler CPI print did not change her mind. "One month of relief is not enough," she said. "It is time to finish the job of restoring price stability."6

The case does not rest on duration alone. "The fact that inflation has been high for a long time, or that it dipped last month, doesn't determine what monetary policy needs to do now," Logan said. "Policy takes time to work its way through the economy. What matters is where inflation is headed from here."6 Where she thinks it is headed is the risk that matters. If elevated inflation becomes entrenched in wages and pricing decisions, the anchor gives way. The Committee would then need "sharper rate increases to bring it back to target, with a larger cost for the labor market. Better modest restriction now than severe restriction later."6 She reads the labor market as able to absorb that trade now. Unemployment averaged 4.3 percent in the first half of 2026, "near most estimates of the lowest sustainable level," and job gains kept coming even as equities rose roughly 20 percent over the trailing year.6 That, in her account, is a labor market with room to tighten into, not one a hike would break.

Logan was not a lone voice. Beth Hammack, president of the Cleveland Fed, dissented on similar grounds: "Inflation is too high. The labor market is right around my level of maximum employment."10 Neel Kashkari, president of the Minneapolis Fed, joined both in preferring the quarter-point increase.1 Three regional presidents dissenting together, in the same direction, at the same meeting, had not happened since September 2016, a pattern independent analysts read as evidence that hawkish sentiment inside the Committee runs wider than the three names on the dissent line.5 Two Board members who did not dissent said something close to it in public before the meeting. Vice Chair Philip Jefferson said it could be "appropriate to reconsider our current policy stance" if inflation did not "start to cool down soon."10 Governor Christopher Waller said the Fed needed "several months" of cooler readings before it could trust the 2 percent goal was back in reach.10 The July vote, on this account, was less hawks against doves than an argument over how urgently to act. Outside forecasters read the dissent count the same way: Omair Sharif of Inflation Insights said, "At this stage, I think we should expect the FOMC to hike rates by 25 basis points in September," and Kathy Bostjancic of Nationwide called it evidence that "policymakers are increasingly more hawkish."11

Lorie Logan, president and CEO of the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, made the most complete public case among the three dissenters, in a speech delivered thirteen days before her vote. She was careful to flag the dissent as her own: "These are my views and, let me emphasize, not necessarily those of my FOMC colleagues."6

Look through the shock

The inflation the Committee is weighing is concentrated in energy and tariffed goods, not a broad economy running too hot, and a rate hike is the wrong tool for a shock like that. Raising rates would squeeze a labor market that is already cooling without doing anything to bring down the price of oil or an imported good.

The textbook case for holding, in former Fed Chair Janet Yellen's words, is to look through the shock. "The 'default strategy' for the Fed should be 'looking through supply shocks,'" she said, because "monetary policy cannot tame supply-driven inflation without exacting unacceptable unemployment costs."9 Her stated exception is narrow: only if inflation expectations "skyrocket" does a central bank need to respond to a shock directly.9 Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody's Analytics, makes the same point more bluntly: "Monetary policy 101 says when there is a supply shock, don't respond. Follow the script. It's worked pretty well." His bottom line: "I don't think they should raise rates."9

Zandi's objection is not only that a hike would fail to touch the underlying prices. It is that trying would cost more than it buys. "That's a dangerous game to play," he said of raising rates. "The labor market is weak and it wouldn't take a lot to push us into a recession."9 Stephanie Roth, chief economist at Wolfe Research, made the same point about the two named drivers directly: "None of those things will be solved by higher rates."9 Goldman Sachs economists reached a similar conclusion in a research note, finding "little reason to think" a limited round of hikes "would provide much help in bringing inflation down."9 Benson Durham, a former Fed official who now runs his own advisory firm, put the case even more plainly: "Rate hikes won't keep the bombs from dropping."9

That the Committee's own statement names only energy, not tariffs, in its attribution sentence matters for this argument specifically. The larger of the two named shocks in Moody's modeling, the Iran-linked energy spike, is also the one least connected to anything a rate hike can touch. Zandi's firm puts it at roughly 0.66 percentage points of forecast year-end inflation, against about 0.17 points for tariffs.9 A quarter-point move by the Committee changes neither the war in the Middle East nor the tariff schedule set in Washington.

Kevin Warsh chaired the meeting that produced the 9-3 hold, and his own words are more equivocal than the economists' case. Even while voting to leave rates unchanged, he struck a hawkish note on the target itself: "There is no soft inflation target, there is no soft implicit target, not on this Committee's watch. There is only a target, and it is 2 percent."3 He does not dispute that shocks are pressing on prices: "I'll be the first to admit the shocks make this job and this policy conjuncture a little tougher."3 Pressed on whether the dissenters were wrong, he declined to say: "I'm going to let the dissenters speak for themselves."3 Asked why the Committee didn't act despite tightening financial conditions, he pointed to markets doing some of the work between meetings: "monetary policy matters not just by what we say, or even what we do... monetary policy matters by how it affects the real economy."3 He called the decision "a period of watchful thinking, not watchful waiting."3 The sharper version of the case he chairs but does not himself make in those terms comes from outside the boardroom, from Zandi and Yellen.9
