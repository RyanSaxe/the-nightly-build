Congress Voted to End the Iran War. The Vote That Would Have Counted Failed.

18 American service members have died in a campaign now three months past the War Powers Resolution's 60-day clock, and whether that clock binds the President turns on a question its own text leaves open: when a run of limited strikes becomes a war only Congress can declare.

8 min read2026-08-04

The strikes outran the clock in May

The United States and Israel began striking Iran on February 28, 2026.1 Two days later the President sent Congress the report the War Powers Resolution demands within 48 hours of committing forces to hostilities.2 In it he said he had acted to "protect United States forces in the region, protect the United States homeland, advance United States national interests."3 Under the statute, the report starts a 60-day clock. After it runs, the President "shall terminate" the use of force unless Congress has declared war, authorized it, or extended the deadline by law.4 The 60 days ran out around May 1, or the end of May if the statute's 30-day extension for the safety of the forces applied. The strikes did not stop. As of early August they run roughly three months past the deadline. Eighteen American service members have died since February,5 and the two governments now hold an uneasy pause while talks are arranged and a planned August 2 strike stands called off.6

Congress has answered, more than once, and the answers are easy to misread. In June the House and Senate both passed House Concurrent Resolution 86, directing the President under section 5(c) of the War Powers Resolution to remove U.S. forces from hostilities with Iran. It cleared the House 215-2087 and the Senate 50-48,8 four Republicans crossing over each time. On July 23 the House passed a second such resolution, 214-208.5 The same day, the Senate took up the one measure that would have carried legal force: Senator Chris Van Hollen's Senate Joint Resolution 180. Unlike a concurrent resolution, a joint resolution goes to the President and becomes law. It failed, 47-49.9 The distinction matters more than the vote counts. A concurrent resolution never reaches the President's desk. Section 5(c)'s attempt to let one order a withdrawal was drained of force by INS v. Chadha, which held that Congress cannot direct the executive through a resolution it never presents for signature.410 What passed cannot bind him; what could have bound him fell short.

Both sides work from the same record and divide over what to call it. Whether more than five months of reciprocal fire, and 18 American dead,5 amount to a war only Congress may declare, or to a long run of limited strikes the President may order alone, is the question the two positions answer differently. The Constitution and the War Powers Resolution both turn on words, "war" and "hostilities," that neither text defines by length or intensity, and the sourced record leaves the count genuinely open.

The strikes are lawful executive action

Article II makes the President Commander in Chief, and limited strikes that stay below a "war in the constitutional sense" fall inside that power without a vote in Congress.

The executive branch has a settled framework for using force without Congress, and it does not rest on the War Powers Resolution. Article II makes the President "Commander in Chief of the Army and Navy of the United States."11 The Office of Legal Counsel has read that grant, most fully in its 2018 opinion on the Syria strikes, to authorize military action on two conditions.12 The first is that the President reasonably finds the action serves important national interests. The second is that the "anticipated nature, scope, and duration" of the fighting will not rise to "a war in the constitutional sense." War in that sense, the opinion says, means "prolonged and substantial military engagements, typically involving exposure of U.S. military personnel to significant risk over a substantial period." Short of that, the President may act alone. The February strikes fit the case the framework was built for: a stated national interest (Iran's nuclear program, its proxies, the safety of U.S. forces and of Israel) met with air power rather than an invasion.3 On this reading the 60-day clock confirms the authority rather than limiting it, since a statute that tells the President to stop after 60 days assumes he could begin on his own.

The strain shows where the opinion itself hedges. Its two-part test "places no limit at all on the president's ability to use significant military force unilaterally," Jack Goldsmith has written, because the executive defines its own national interest and most limited strikes satisfy the nature-scope-duration prong. Steve Vladeck calls each such opinion another step down a slope toward unbounded presidential war power.13 And the 2018 opinion concedes the point the 2026 facts press hardest: "we do not rule out that some imperfect wars may involve such prolonged and substantial engagements as to require that authorization."12 Whether a reciprocal campaign in its sixth month, with 18 Americans dead, is still short of "prolonged and substantial" is the factual question the framework cannot settle on its own terms.

President Trump, who ordered the strikes and filed the report claiming the authority, told Congress he acted on his "constitutional authority as Commander in Chief" and "in the collective self-defense of our regional allies, including Israel."3

Only Congress can authorize a war

Article I gives Congress alone the power to declare war, and the War Powers Resolution requires the President to end hostilities Congress has not authorized once the 60-day clock has run.

The competing framework starts from the text that assigns the power in the first place. Article I gives Congress, not the President, the power "To declare War,"11 and the War Powers Resolution was written to hold that line. It states that the President may introduce forces into hostilities "only pursuant to (1) a declaration of war, (2) specific statutory authorization, or (3) a national emergency created by attack upon the United States."14 None of the three is present here, and the 60-day clock has long since run, so on this reading the strikes have been unlawful since roughly May. Members of both parties have put it in those terms. "We're past the 60 days, so you either follow the law or change it," said Representative Brian Fitzpatrick, one of four Republicans who crossed over.7 Senator Adam Schiff warned that "Congress must reassert its war powers, or it risks losing them for good."15

The reading has two soft spots, and both are on the page in 2026. The first is textual. Most scholars and every recent administration treat the "only pursuant to" list as a statement of policy rather than an operative limit, which leaves the 60-day clock as the only hard command.14 A clock the President can run past asks less of him than a flat prohibition would. The second soft spot is that Congress holds a remedy it has not landed. When 26 members sued to stop the 1999 Kosovo campaign, the D.C. Circuit dismissed the suit in Campbell v. Clinton for lack of standing. Its reasoning: legislators who can pass a binding law or cut off a war's funding must use those tools rather than the courts.16 In 2026 Congress reached for exactly that tool and missed. The binding joint resolution fell short while only the nonbinding concurrent resolutions passed. The withdrawal this reading says the law compels has not been ordered in any form a court would enforce.

Senator Chris Van Hollen, sponsor of the one resolution that would have bound the President, has long held that the war power belongs to Congress alone: "The Constitution gives Congress – and Congress only – the power to declare war."17
