Uber's stakes in other companies add more to its equity value than its debt subtracts

Working from Uber's own Q2 2026 filing, the two-step bridge from enterprise value to a price per share shows the stakes outweighing both the debt and the dilution.

10 min read2026-08-14

Why this matters

A discounted cash flow or a multiple hands you an enterprise value. A stock quote is a price per share. Nothing connects the two automatically. The gap holds which claims sit ahead of the common shareholder, which assets sit outside the operating business, and how many shares a company has effectively promised away. Uber's own Q2 2026 filing carries all of it: real debt, stakes in other companies, options, and a convertible bond. By the end, you will be able to take an enterprise value from any DCF or multiple, and a filing like Uber's, and walk both bridges yourself.

What the net-debt shortcut left out

Enterprise value prices the whole operating business: every free cash flow it will generate, discounted at the cost of all the capital that financed it. That value belongs to every capital provider at once, not just the common shareholder, which is why a share price and an enterprise value cannot be compared directly.1

An earlier lesson crossed from one to the other in a single subtraction: pull net debt out of enterprise value, divide by the share count, done. Its invented firm had one kind of debt and no outstanding options. A real filing rarely cooperates. It carries several claims ahead of the common shareholder, assets outside the operating business, and a share count that keeps growing as employees vest into stock granted years earlier. Uber's Form 10-Q for the quarter ended June 30, 2026, filed August 5, discloses a treasury-stock-method adjustment and an if-converted convertible-note adjustment beside real debt and a portfolio of stakes in other companies.2 This lesson works that filing end to end, in the bridge's two steps: enterprise value to equity value, then equity value to a price per share.

Every claim ahead of the common shareholder

Enterprise value is priced off cash flow that pays every claimant before the common shareholder sees a dollar: lenders first, then any non-controlling interest, then preferred shareholders, only then common equity. Getting to equity value means subtracting every claim. Damodaran, whose framework the earlier lessons lean on, treats debt as "inclusive of all non-equity claims (including preferred equity)." Lease commitments should be "converted into debt" too. Whatever the label, each is a fixed claim ahead of the common shareholder.3

The subtraction is net debt, not gross: a company's own cash could retire that debt today, so only the shortfall is a claim on the business. Not every dollar of cash is free to net off, either. Cash "needed for its ongoing operations," or trapped by a tax or legal restriction, understates equity value if netted out anyway.3 Operating leases are the harder case: Damodaran converts them into debt outright,3 but Uber's balance sheet already carries that liability alongside a right-of-use asset enterprise value's own cash flows already reflect. This lesson leaves leases out of net debt for that reason, said plainly rather than asserted as settled.

The other half of the bridge runs the other way: anything a company owns outside its operations belongs back in equity value, not out. Cash beyond what operations need is the plainest example. So is a cross-holding, Damodaran's term for a stake in another company. A passive holding earns no place in operating cash flow, so its market value is added back separately.3

Walking Uber's bridge in real dollars

UBER closed at $75.88 on August 13, 2026, putting its 2,042,560,121 shares outstanding at a combined $154,989 million.4 Turning that market capitalization into an enterprise value means running the bridge above backward: add back total debt and non-controlling interests, subtract cash and every non-operating stake. The result, about $150,872 million, is not a discounted-cash-flow answer. It is this market price with the bridge unwound, offered so every dollar below has somewhere to come from. Reversing the arithmetic returns exactly the market capitalization it started from.

The cash netted out above is only unrestricted cash and short-term investments. Uber separately discloses $2,307 million of restricted cash and $9,486 million of restricted investments, collateral for its self-insurance program and not free for a shareholder to claim. Netting those out too, since they also read as "cash" somewhere on the balance sheet, would overstate equity value by billions.2 And the non-controlling-interest line is not only Freight Holding's minority owners. It also carries their Series A and A-1 preferred stock, a real preferred-equity claim with no line of its own on the balance sheet. A textbook bridge that expects preferred stock as its own subtraction could miss it entirely.2

The Aurora note and the Aurora stake

Uber's 2028 Exchangeable Senior Notes, $1,324 million, exchange into Aurora Innovation shares, not Uber's, and 61 percent of Uber's Aurora stake is pledged as their collateral.2 Subtracting the notes as debt and separately adding back the full stake is arithmetically correct. But the notes are likely to be settled with the pledged shares themselves, so the two lines are closer to two views of the same collateral than two independent claims. No source here states a rule for netting them together, so the bridge above still lists them apart, with this caveat.

Cross-holdings are supposed to be passive, with no operating tie to the parent.3 Uber's stakes stretch that assumption: on July 16, 2026, Uber signed an agreement to acquire Delivery Hero outright, the very holding this bridge still counts as a passive add-back.2 Aurora is integrated into Uber's own Mobility product; Careem is a former Uber subsidiary and related party; Grab is the closer case of a genuinely passive stake. None of it changes the table's arithmetic, but it is reason to treat the equity-stakes line as individual judgment calls, not one clean category.

What a diluted share count assumes

Equity value divided by shares outstanding today still undercounts the shares a company has effectively promised away. Employees hold options and unvested stock that convert into more shares once exercised or vested; some lenders hold debt that converts into shares instead of being repaid in cash. Both dilute the common shareholder's slice, and a price per share that ignores them overstates what each share is worth.5

The treasury-stock method handles the options and unvested awards: assume every in-the-money option is exercised, assume the cash it raises buys back shares at the period's average market price, and only what is left over adds to the count.6

Damodaran calls this "a sloppy alternative":8 it ignores the time value in an option still years from expiring and has "no effective way of dealing with vesting."9 He would rather price options with an option-pricing model and net that value against equity value directly. Every public filing nonetheless reports under the treasury-stock and if-converted conventions, because GAAP requires them. Uber's own diluted-EPS footnote states plainly that dilution "is reflected by application of the treasury stock method and convertible securities by application of the if-converted method, as applicable."2 That is the number a reader meets in practice, with Damodaran's objection on the record rather than settled.

Convertible debt gets different treatment: the if-converted method assumes the notes convert into shares today and adds back the interest the company would no longer pay.5 Uber's 2028 Convertible Notes would convert into about 23.8 million shares at their stated rate.2 But they settle principal in cash and deliver shares only for value above it. At Uber's $75.88 close against a $72.54 conversion price, a 4.6 percent spread, the shares that actually dilute the count run closer to one million. That is this lesson's own arithmetic, not the filing's, but it is consistent with the 810,000 shares the filing's reconciliation adds for the six-month if-converted effect.2

Uber's own reconciliation, and the price it implies

Uber's own stock plans have granted far more than that net effect implies: 6.1 million options and 71.8 million unvested RSUs, a gross pool of about 77.9 million shares.2 None of that gross count enters the diluted total directly. The treasury-stock method nets out the assumed buyback, roughly 15 million more are excluded as antidilutive, and only 13,343 thousand shares are left as equity awards' net addition below.2 A reader who wants the larger, more dramatic figure for illustration should explain the gap, not quote it as the dilution.

Divide the $154,989 million equity value by 2,060,763 thousand diluted shares instead of the 2,042,560,121 behind the market quote, and $75.88 becomes about $75.21, down 67 cents. That is close to the 0.8 percent gap between the reconciliation's own basic and diluted denominators. The small remainder is the difference between shares outstanding on one day and shares outstanding averaged over six months, not a second dilution effect.

Verdict

Every dollar in this bridge traces to a line in Uber's own filing, but the $150,872 million enterprise value it starts from does not: it was solved backward from the market price, not forward from a forecast. Treat $75.21 as a demonstration of how the bridge moves, not a verdict on what Uber is worth. Swap in a discounted-cash-flow enterprise value instead, and the same bridge carries it to a genuine answer.

The takeaway

The bridge is two steps, not one. Subtract every claim ahead of the common shareholder, debt, non-controlling interests, and preferred stock hiding inside them, then add back whatever sits outside the operating business, cash beyond what operations need and stakes in other companies. Divide by a share count that already counts what employees and convertible-debt holders are owed, not just what is outstanding today. On Uber's own numbers, its stakes in other companies were the biggest mover, adding about $6.13 a share, more than net debt, non-controlling interests, and dilution took away combined. The bridge does not change from one filing to the next. Which line dominates it does, and only running a filing's own numbers through it tells you which.
