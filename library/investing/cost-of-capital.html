Kentucky Power earns 4.4% on equity its regulator sets at 9.75%

That gap is what a cost of capital is built to measure, and by that same measure Costco clears its own by more than 30 points.

10 min read2026-08-02

Why this matters

Every company chasing growth is making a bet: that the next dollar it invests will earn more than that dollar cost to raise. Costco and American Electric Power are both making that bet right now, with real capital and real state regulators watching. One is winning by a landslide. The other is asking regulators for a return regulators already say is fair, and in some of its territories, not getting there. Return on invested capital, the number the last lesson in this course worked out for both companies, only says how much each earned. It does not say whether that return was worth raising the money for. This lesson supplies the number that turns "how much did it earn" into "was it worth it," and follows both companies through it.

What it costs to raise a dollar

On July 31, 2026, the ten-year US Treasury note yielded 4.75 percent.1 Lend the government money for a decade and that is the return, in full, on schedule. It is the safest a dollar can earn in the United States.

A business asking for that same dollar has to pay more. It might not pay the money back, so investors demand a premium on top of that risk-free floor, sized to how risky the business is. Add the premium to the floor and the result is what finance calls the cost of capital. "You will see it described as an opportunity cost, a discount rate and a hurdle rate for investments and it is all of the above depending upon where it is being used and by whom," in the words of NYU finance professor Aswath Damodaran.2 This lesson uses it the third way: as the return an investment has to beat. Put a dollar to work and earn more than its cost of capital, and the investment adds value. Earn less, and it subtracts value, no matter how large the profit looks on its own.

What lenders charge and shareholders demand

That single number is really two numbers blended into one. A company raises money two ways: borrowing from lenders, and selling ownership stakes to shareholders. Each charges a different price, and the cost of capital is the weighted average of the two, sized to how much of each a company actually uses.2

American Electric Power's own 2025 borrowing shows what lenders charge. That year the company sold $2.1 billion of senior unsecured notes at interest rates of 5.38 to 5.85 percent.3 That is the pretax price. Interest payments are tax-deductible, so after tax the government effectively covers part of the bill. At the 21 percent federal statutory rate, the rate this kind of estimate conventionally uses,3 a 5.38-to-5.85-percent bond costs a company roughly 4.25 to 4.6 percent after tax. Call it 4.4 percent.

Shareholders charge more, because they are paid last. If a business runs into trouble, lenders get repaid before any shareholder sees a dime, so equity carries more risk and demands a higher return.4 Build the cost of equity the same way the risk-free rate was built: start at 4.75 percent, then add a risk premium sized to the stock market as a whole rather than one bond. Damodaran estimated that premium, the extra return stock investors demand over Treasuries, at 4.23 percent at the start of 2026, computed from market prices and expected cash flows across the S&P 500.5 That is his own dated estimate, not a fixed constant. The same premium, measured over different multi-decade windows instead of estimated from today's prices, has run anywhere from about 3.9 to 6.2 percent depending on the years chosen and how the average is taken.2 Add his 4.23 percent to the 4.75 percent floor and a business exactly as risky as the market overall costs about 8.98 percent, call it 9 percent, to raise equity capital. That figure assumes a business with what finance calls a beta of one, exactly as risky as the average public company. A riskier business would demand more, a steadier one less. Sizing that adjustment precisely, beta, is a calculation this lesson leaves out.

AEP's own balance sheet shows how the blend actually works, using the book values a company reports rather than market prices, a simplification full valuation work skips.2 At the end of fiscal 2025 the company carried $47.3 billion of debt against $31.1 billion of shareholders' equity, a split of roughly 60 percent debt and 40 percent equity.6 For every $100 AEP raises on that mix, about $60 comes from lenders at roughly 4.4 percent after tax, $2.64. About $40 comes from shareholders at roughly 8.98 percent, $3.59. Add them together and AEP's own blended cost of capital, on this simplified math, comes out to about $6.23 on every $100 raised, a cost of capital near 6.2 percent.

The weighting matters as much as either price. A company funded mostly by debt, like AEP, pulls its blended number down toward the cheaper source. A company funded almost entirely by equity ends up paying close to the cost of equity alone, with barely any cheap debt to blend in.

Costco clears its cost of capital by miles

The prior lesson in this course defines return on invested capital, ROIC: what a business earns after tax, divided by the money invested to earn it.4 Comparing that return to the cost of capital just built is the test. A firm earning more than its cost of capital is earning what Damodaran calls excess returns, and trades at a premium because of it.4 A firm earning less does the opposite. Damodaran checked this across more than 40,000 public companies worldwide in a 2016 analysis. More than half earned returns below their own cost of capital: "growth, across the globe, is more likely to destroy value than to add it, in a company."2

Costco's fiscal 2025 operating income was $10,383 million, taxed at an effective rate of 25.1 percent, for after-tax operating income of about $7,777 million.7 Divide that by the $18,375 million of debt and equity, net of cash, Costco had invested at the start of the fiscal year, and its return on invested capital works out to about 42 percent.8 Costco funds itself almost entirely with equity. At the same fiscal year end it carried $29.2 billion of shareholders' equity against $5.8 billion of debt, a mix of about 83 percent equity.7 Its own cost of capital, by the weighting logic above, sits close to the roughly 9 percent cost of equity worked out earlier, nowhere near AEP's debt-heavy 6.2 percent. A 42 percent return against a cost of capital near 9 percent clears with more than 30 points to spare. By this course's own test, Costco's growth creates value.

APCo and Kentucky Power fall short of theirs

It is tempting to run the exact same test on AEP. Compute a return on invested capital the way Costco's was, blending all of AEP's debt and equity, then compare it directly to the 9.75 percent return its regulators approve for its utilities. That comparison does not work. A return on invested capital is priced across all the capital a company raises, debt and equity combined. An approved return on equity, the number a state regulator sets, prices only the equity slice of one specific regulated utility's rate base, nothing else.9 AEP's regulators are not pricing its whole balance sheet. They are pricing what one state-specific utility owes its shareholders, one jurisdiction at a time.

AEP already runs the like-for-like version of this test itself. Its investor disclosures report an "operating earned" return on equity for each regulated jurisdiction, measured the same way, on the same equity basis, as the return regulators approve for that jurisdiction.9 Folding all of that into one company-wide cost of capital, the way the 6.2 percent estimate above did with a single assumed beta, would hide exactly what matters. AEP runs a dozen state-regulated utilities and transmission lines, each carrying its own approved return. Blending them into one number lets a strong jurisdiction mask a weak one.2

As of March 31, 2025, the pattern runs by jurisdiction, not by company as a whole.10

APCo, AEP's Virginia and West Virginia utility, is the clearest case. It earned 7.9 percent against a 9.75 percent approved return at the end of 2024, and 8.2 percent against the same 9.75 percent three months later.910 Kentucky Power did worse: 4.4 percent against a 9.75 percent approved return, less than half what its own regulator calls fair.10 Growth earning 4.4 or 8.2 percent, when investors require 9.75 percent, adds less value than it costs, if it adds any at all.

Not every AEP jurisdiction falls short. Indiana Michigan Power earned 10.3 percent against roughly 9.85 percent approved, and AEP Ohio earned 10.2 percent against about 9.7 to 9.85 percent approved, both clearing their own approved return the way Costco clears its cost of capital.10 A single company-wide number would have blurred all of this into one average. Testing a return against its cost of capital only means something at the level the capital was actually priced, jurisdiction by jurisdiction here, not the consolidated whole.

The takeaway

A dollar of capital has a price before a business ever puts it to work: what a lender charges to lend it, what a shareholder charges to risk it, blended into one cost of capital. Costco clears that cost of capital by more than 30 points, so every dollar it plows back into the business is worth more than it cost to raise. Kentucky Power and AEP's Virginia-West Virginia utility do not clear the return their own regulators call fair, one by a little, Kentucky Power by more than half. Growth does not fix that. It just puts more capital to work at the same return, doing more of whatever that return was already doing: building value at Costco, and eroding it, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, at some of AEP's utilities. Return on invested capital said how much each company earned. The cost of capital says whether that was ever worth raising the money for.
