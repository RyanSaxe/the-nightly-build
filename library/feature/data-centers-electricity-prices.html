Data-center studies agree on wholesale costs and split on household rates

Sorted by the price each one measures, nine estimates, including a new Wharton and Harvard Business School paper, put the household effect between −6% and +5.2% without settling its sign.

5 min read2026-11-06

The newest paper reports a share of a price rise

Studies published this year agree that data-center load has raised wholesale and capacity prices. They disagree about household rates, mostly because each measures a different price, period or dose of capacity. Two studies of the same residential rate still reach opposite signs.

The newest is a working paper1 by Vishrut Rana of Wharton, Christian Kaps of Harvard Business School and Serguei Netessine.23 Data centers built from 2020 to 2025 raised industrial prices by an average of $5.4 per megawatt-hour, 15% of the observed increase, and residential prices by $1.7, "4% of the observed increase since 2020".1

Those are shares of a rise, not percent changes in price. The $1.7 is about 1% of the 2025 US average residential price of $173 per megawatt-hour.4 We could read only the paper's abstract.

Down the price column, every wholesale and capacity row is positive, whatever the design. The retail rows run from −6% to +5.2%, on doses that differ row by row.

Two of them can be put on one dose, a doubling of capacity. Watten's coefficient, −0.035, is on the inverse hyperbolic sine of capacity, which behaves like a natural log, so per doubling it is the coefficient times ln 2, about −2.4%. The paper's own text says −3.5%.9 USC's figure is +0.62% per doubling, which its own brief calls not causal.7 No paper splits its result before and after the 2023 AI buildout.

PJM, the mid-Atlantic grid operator, pays generators through capacity auctions to be available at peak. Its market monitor calls data-center load growth "the primary reason" for capacity-market conditions.8 E3, in a review funded by the Data Center Coalition, splits one year's price rise, from $29 to $270 per megawatt-day, across five drivers and assigns about half to load growth.12 Both measure payments to capacity sellers, not household bills.

Fixed-cost spreading is the only channel that lowers an average rate

An average rate is the cost a utility recovers divided by the kilowatt-hours it sells, and a data center adds to both.12

Only the denominator pushes the rate down, and only while the costs above it hold still. The Dallas Fed paper makes that a threshold.

With illustrative round numbers, a 10% demand rise and an elasticity of 1 give s* = 1.1/2.1, about 0.52, so over half the rate must be fixed cost. Halve the elasticity and s* = 1.1/1.6, about 0.69. The authors assume new load adds no fixed costs, which they call "unlikely to hold in reality".5 Meeks and colleagues find rate bases and generation investment rising after entry at investor-owned utilities.10

Two instrumental-variable studies of one rate reach opposite signs

Watten's and Meeks's teams both instrument cumulative capacity, with a variable that predicts where data centers go and, by assumption, reaches prices only through them. Both put capacity through a log-like transform, so the residential coefficients read roughly as elasticities: −0.035 against +0.056 to +0.071.

Elhai and Zhou, Harvard researchers not on the Rana paper, tested two retail designs on their data and found "neither design is credible". Most of their utility-states host no data centers. Dropping one event swings an estimate between −$10.0 and +$2.6 per megawatt-hour. Dominion Virginia carries 80% of their instrument's first stage.6 That verdict covers their designs, not the highway-plan instrument.

We would put most weight on the wholesale and capacity rows. Two independently built dispatch models agree on the sign, and the PJM figure re-runs actual auctions. For the household rate, these studies support a size and not a sign: −6% to +5.2%, with Rana's $1.7, about 1%, in between.

Those retail estimates stop at load built by 2025. The 2027/2028 PJM auction already priced 13,018 megawatts of new or forecast data-center load.8 Elhai and Zhou expect backward-looking estimates to "underpredict" later effects, because supply curves are convex.6
