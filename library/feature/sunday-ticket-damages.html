Sunday Ticket damages go back to trial over a $191.26 discount

The Ninth Circuit restored the subscribers' economists and their liability verdict on 30 October but agreed that the $4.61 billion residential award almost certainly comes from a list price minus an average price paid.

5 min read2026-11-05

The panel restores the experts and keeps the retrial

On 30 October a Ninth Circuit panel put the Sunday Ticket subscribers' two economists back in the case, ordered the trial court to reinstate the jury's finding that the NFL broke the antitrust laws, and agreed that damages must be tried again. Judge Anthony Johnstone wrote for Judge Holly Thomas and Judge Joan Lefkow, a district judge sitting by designation. Nobody dissented or concurred.1

The subscribers bought DirecTV's package of out-of-market Sunday games between 2011 and 2023 and won at trial in June 2024. The trial judge then excluded their economists as unreliable and, without them, entered judgment for the NFL. The panel held the exclusions an abuse of discretion, which undoes that judgment.1

List price minus average paid

Instruction 41 told the jury to find the overcharge, the difference between what subscribers "actually paid for Sunday Ticket" and what they "would have paid had there been, no agreement to restrict output."2 The verdict form gives two answers to the cent and no working.3

The NFL's post-trial motion supplied the working, and the district court adopted it. Take $294, the 2018 and 2019 residential list price, and subtract $102.74, which the Ninth Circuit calls "Dr. Zona's disputed estimate" of the average price paid.1 Zona is one of the subscribers' economists. Multiply the $191.26 by 24,105,049 residential subscriptions and you get the residential award to the cent, $4,610,331,671.74. The 506,788 commercial subscriptions give $96,928,272.88, two cents under that award.4

The Ninth Circuit holds that the $191.26, money subscribers may have saved because DirecTV sold below list, "reflected a discount, not an overcharge." Neither input is a competitive price, and the commercial class got a residential discount although commercial subscribers such as sports bars paid what "on average was an order of magnitude higher."1 The subscribers' lower reading treats $191.26 as the price paid and zero as the competitive price. The district court rejected it for a similar flaw, a discount treated as a price,4 and the Ninth Circuit does not mention it.

The reinstated models price the overcharge

Dr. Rascher, the other economist, compares the NFL with top-25 college football telecasts after NCAA v. Board of Regents (1984) opened them to competition. In his world the games air on channels subscribers already have, "so customers don't pay anything extra." Zona modelled one more licensee, which cuts DirecTV's price 49.7%. Against that world, exclusivity cost the residential class $2.81 billion and the commercial class $670 million. His "NFL Tax" model, in which the league kept the price high to protect CBS and Fox audiences, cuts it 24.6%, and the opinion gives no dollar total.1 With a zero competitive price, Rascher's damages are everything paid, roughly $5.6 billion residential and $1.3 billion commercial.4

Our own division of each total by the count the jury multiplied puts the models on the scale of $191.26. Rascher's bar is then the average residential payment, about $232, more than twice the $102.74 that the Ninth Circuit attributes to "a group that included the Subscribers."1 Zona's is about half of that, as a 49.7% cut in those payments would give.

The left panel holds the subscribers' best argument. The residential award sits between Zona's and Rascher's totals, and an award "within the range" of the evidence, they argued, "cannot be set aside." The district court answered that a verdict must also "find substantial support in the record,"4 and the Ninth Circuit applied that standard.1 The residential figure lands in the range without coming from either model. On the right, the commercial award falls below that range: the models put commercial damages at about 7 and 13 times the award.

What the second jury has to find

    Fourteen days after judgment, unless an order changes it.

The panel affirmed only the new trial and said nothing about the district court's alternative of nominal damages, so the next jury starts from no figure.14 The same panel's memorandum affirmed class certification.6

The NFL said it was "disappointed," called its media distribution strategy "by far the most fan-friendly distribution model in all of sports and entertainment," and said it would weigh "further appellate review."7 The subscribers' lawyers did not immediately respond to Reuters.8 The Sports Broadcasting Act exempts the league's pooled sale of "sponsored telecasting,"9 and the NFL's 2020 Supreme Court petition argued that even outside that letter, Congress's "express sanction of the League's essential role in marketing the broadcast rights for NFL games cannot be ignored."10 The Ninth Circuit reads "sponsored" as free, advertiser-supported broadcasting.1 Justice Kavanaugh wrote in 2020 that the defendants could petition again after trial.11 The 2026 appeal "concerns only" injury and damages.1

If the case reaches a second damages jury, that jury needs the number the first one never wrote down, a competitive price for Sunday Ticket. The reinstated testimony offers three: zero, 49.7% below DirecTV's price, or 24.6% below. The smaller of the two dollar totals puts the commercial claims at about seven times the first commercial award.
