Gordon Bell winners compare a measured 105-minute FeMoco run with a quantum projection now cut to 2.5 hours

Their abstract’s “one-fifth” divides by an 8.6-hour estimate that its lead authors at Google Quantum AI replaced in May, and Garnet Chan’s Caltech group had posted a classical energy for the same 76-orbital model in January.

5 min read2026-11-23

What the abstract’s one-fifth divides by

The paper that won ACM’s 2026 Gordon Bell Prize reports a FeMoco “ground-state consensus” on 1,280 LineShine supercomputer nodes in a “measured end-to-end wall time of 105 minutes,” “approximately one-fifth of the best published 8.6-hour single-run projection for fault-tolerant quantum computing on the same active space.”12 That sets a measurement against a projection, and the projection has moved. Guang Hao Low and colleagues at Google Quantum AI, authors of the 8.6 hours, now put the expected run time at 2.5 hours for the same active space.3 Huanchen Zhai, Garnet Chan and colleagues at Caltech had posted a classical energy for it in January, to about chemical accuracy (1 kcal/mol, or 1.6 millihartree).4

FeMoco is the iron-molybdenum cofactor of nitrogenase, the enzyme that converts N2 to NH3.4 An active space is the set of electrons and orbitals a calculation treats explicitly. CAS(113e, 76o) is 113 electrons in 76 orbitals, beyond exact diagonalization. The abstract calls FeMoco the canonical “killer app” for fault-tolerant quantum computing in chemistry “for nearly a decade” and claims “the first exascale classical solution” of this active space.1

A benchmark that started at 54 orbitals

Reiher and colleagues proposed FeMoco as a quantum target in a 2016 preprint, with 54 electrons in 54 orbitals. Their estimates ran from 12 days to 11 hours at 1 millihartree, depending on the logical qubits spent, and assumed “that a good trial state for the ground state is available.”5 Quantum phase estimation makes a projective measurement of the energy, so it needs a starting state that overlaps the true ground state.6

In 2019 Li et al., Chan among them, found that model classically easy, needing “only modest resources.”7 Chan puts it at a few hours on a laptop.6 The same paper proposed the 76-orbital space,7 which became the common benchmark for quantum resource estimates.6

In the left column, the 76-orbital projection fell from 8.6 hours to 2.5 in fifteen months under the same assumed error rate, cycle time and reaction time.83 The 2025 paper had warned that magic state cultivation, which postdated its cost model, might make 8 hours “a substantial overestimate.”8

A measurement, a projection and an extrapolation

Divided by 105 minutes, 8.6 hours gives 4.9, the abstract’s one-fifth, and 2.5 hours gives 1.4. Both ratios are this article’s arithmetic, and each divides unlike things.

The prize run was measured on existing hardware, but the abstract gives no accuracy and does not say what the 105 minutes include, such as how many spin configurations it covers.1 Both Low et al. figures time phase estimation alone. The 2026 preprint “ignore[s] the cost of preparing a good trial state,” which it says “should be strongly subdominant.”3 The 2025 paper had put that preparation at about the same gate count as phase estimation.8 Zhai et al. are the reverse. They ran their calculations, but the answer is an extrapolation. Their only strict bound, a variational energy, sits 8.76 kcal/mol above it, and “there is no formal theory of these extrapolations.”4 The 105 minutes is the only measured wall time in Fig. 2.

What the 105 minutes establishes

Chan wrote in March that FeMoco is “overrepresented” in the literature on quantum computing for chemistry, and that convincing evidence that chemically relevant problems meet the conditions for exponential quantum advantage “has not yet been found.” He is “excited to see quantum computers applied to this problem,” he adds, “just not waiting for them to be built first.”6 He also grants what quantum algorithms offer that extrapolation does not.

Low and colleagues make the same point: “quantum methods can provide accuracy guarantees that are missing when comparing heuristic classical methods and extrapolating.”3 Babbush and colleagues at Google Quantum AI wrote in November 2025 that “no classical method has solved its electronic structure to the accuracy needed for definitive chemical insight,” while calling the debate over exponential speedup “unresolved.”10 Both positions predate the prize.

What the prize adds is on the classical side of Fig. 1: a code sustaining 1.456 EFLOP/s in double precision on 20,480 of LineShine’s 22,680 nodes, and a measured 105 minutes on 1,280 of them.19 No single run on a fixed 76-orbital active space can show how cost grows as the active space grows, and that growth is what Chan’s doubt about exponential advantage concerns.6
