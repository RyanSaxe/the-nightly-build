Microsoft says its own traffic rerouting set off a latent July defect in every region

Microsoft's final report on the 12 November Microsoft 365 outage blames lock contention that hit only its newer, higher-core gateway servers, once maintenance, a Teams failover and the North American peak coincided.

5 min read2026-11-27

A change made in July took Exchange Online down for a day

On 12 November 2026 the gateway servers in front of Exchange Online and other Microsoft 365 services began failing broadly in North America at about 16:00 UTC. Within about two hours, servers in every region were affected.1 Microsoft's final post-incident report, dated 20 November, traces the outage to a lock-contention defect that had run quietly since a July update. Routine load set it off, and the gateways' own traffic redistribution carried the failure to every region.1

Every figure here is Microsoft's. It publishes these reports only in the Microsoft 365 admin center. The public copy is a re-save by 365Fanatic, a Dutch consultancy, and its timeline matches what Microsoft's status account posted during the incident.12 The preliminary report of 15 November matches a root-cause post by Charles Lamanna, a Microsoft executive.34

Each failing unit pushed its load onto the next

Panel A reads from the top. The defect is lock contention inside an internal Windows process, which exhausts a gateway server's CPU and memory. It arrived with an update that began deploying in July and ran worldwide for more than three months without impact. Only newer server models are hit, because more cores allow more concurrent requests and so more contention. Microsoft's load tests ran on older, fewer-core hardware.1

On 12 November three routine conditions added load: nearly 14% of gateway capacity out for a deployment, Teams traffic failed over at 01:07 UTC onto the gateway shared with Exchange Online, and the North American daily peak. Microsoft says the three together are within normal operating parameters.1

The gateway is split into thousands of capacity units. When a unit's servers stop responding, traffic management moves its traffic to healthy units in the same region, which is normally how the service rides out a fault. Here each move put the same load on fewer servers carrying the same defect, so they locked up in turn. A region that runs out of healthy capacity sends traffic to other regions, and those ran the same update.1

The preliminary report blamed that last step on bulkheads between partitions that failed "due to a separate issue".3 The final drops it, and its first repair finding reads "Traffic distribution design can cascade failures to other regions".1

Fifteen hours after the failover, two hours to reach every region

The first band is the gateways carrying Teams traffic off-peak. The final report says services stayed healthy for about 16 hours after the failover, though its own times put the broad failures 14 hours 53 minutes after it. The third band is the night of 12 to 13 November, when restarted servers would not stay up. Customer impact ran 23 hours 50 minutes. The incident was declared resolved at 04:15 on 14 November, 35 hours 55 minutes after it opened.1

Restarted servers failed again as clients reconnected

Microsoft's summary blames the slow recovery on three things: finding a change made months earlier, restarting each server twice, and rolling the fix out across the fleet.1

The report's body adds the loop in panel B of Fig. 1. Returned servers "were immediately overwhelmed by queuing client traffic and became unresponsive again".1 Microsoft's status account reported the same at 00:06 UTC on 13 November.2 Microsoft recovered by patching large groups of capacity units at once, held out of rotation.1

The loop resembles what Bronson and colleagues call a metastable failure, where a feedback loop holds a system in a bad state until a large corrective action.5 Microsoft does not use the term, and it fits only in part. In the paper's definition the bad state persists after the trigger is removed. These servers failed again while the defect was still deployed, and the report never shows a patched server overwhelmed.

One repair item addresses the loop: gradual ramp-up and admission control when servers rejoin. Its due date is TBD. A recovery-procedures item due in the first quarter of 2027 covers the slow rollout of the fix instead. Two items written against how far this failure reached, traffic distribution that cascades between regions and one region's Teams fault failing over worldwide, are due in the fourth quarter of 2026.1 Microsoft has dated the fix for the cross-region cascade and not the one for servers knocked over as they rejoin.
