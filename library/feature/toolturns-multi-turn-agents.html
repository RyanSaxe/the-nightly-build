In ToolTurns, an agent’s failures cluster within a dialogue

All nine models complete 5.8 to 8.4 points more of their three-request dialogues than independent requests would predict, and all score lower on follow-ups than on the opening request.

5 min read2026-11-16

A dialogue counts only if all three requests pass

The best of nine models on the ToolTurns tool-use benchmark passes 69.6% of individual requests and completes 39.8% of whole dialogues. Three independent passes at 69.6% would complete only about 34%, a calculation the paper makes for that one model.1 Against the product of each model’s three per-request rates, all nine complete 5.8 to 8.4 points more, so passes and failures cluster within a dialogue.

ToolTurns is a preprint by Gholami, King and Flanigan, submitted 11 November. Each of its 2,620 problems yields one dialogue (Figure 1), and each agent was run once, with no confidence intervals.1 Their checker-fixer, which rewrites flawed simulated-user turns, cut detected defects, but they did not test whether it changes agent scores.1 The tau2-bench authors audited their own gpt-4.1 user simulator by hand and recorded error rates of 40% in retail and 47% in airline, with 12% and 13% critical, meaning they prevent task completion.2 That is another simulator on other tasks.

Every model passes fewer follow-ups than opening requests

Success averages 67.9% on the opening request and 59.4% on the two follow-ups (my arithmetic from Table 4).1

The paper says the decline does not isolate a cost of carrying context. Its judge sees one request’s slice, so figures retrieved a request earlier can be scored as fabricated, and no run poses the follow-ups as first requests.1 Laban et al. built that control for text tasks: spreading instructions over turns cost 15 models 39% on average, while the same information in one turn kept 95.1% of the single-turn score, so the loss came from the turns.3

Completion runs above what independent requests predict

If the three requests passed or failed independently, a dialogue would complete at the product of the three position rates. For Opus 4.8 that is 74.7% × 65.4% × 68.6% = 33.5%, against 39.8% observed. Figure 3 shows all nine above their products. At 2,620 dialogues the sampling error on a rate near 40% is about one point (my arithmetic).

The tables hold only marginal rates, so they cannot say why. The authors offer two causes and test neither: “some problems are simply harder, or an agent that establishes the right tool and context early tends to keep succeeding.”1 Laban et al. found carry-over in text tasks, where models that take a wrong turn “get lost and do not recover”.3 Repeated trials of a tau-bench task share no context. A gpt-4o agent at about 61% per retail trial still passes all eight about 25% of the time,4 against 1.9% for independent trials (0.618, my arithmetic). Neither shows which operates here.

GPT-5.4-mini leads the rubric and trails on task success

The paper scores each dialogue on a weighted five-aspect rubric. It ranks GPT-5.4-mini first and Opus 4.8 second. Task success ranks Opus first and GPT-5.4-mini eighth.

The paper reports a Spearman correlation of 0.23 between the two orderings.1 Without GPT-5.4-mini it is 0.64 over the other eight. At nine models, 0.23 has a two-sided permutation p-value of 0.55, so it is not distinguishable from zero (my computations from Table 3). The paper traces GPT-5.4-mini’s rubric lead to efficiency and consistency, which reward few, coherent calls even when the answer is wrong, and it makes the fewest calls.1

Cubing the opening rate comes close because two effects offset

An evaluator who measures only the opening request and cubes it lands within 3.3 points of observed completion for eight of the nine models (Table 1). The ninth, GPT-5.4-mini, is 6.9 points low.

The follow-up factor (the two follow-up rates multiplied, over the opening rate squared) runs from 0.59 to 0.94, pulling completion under the cube. The clustering factor (observed completion over the independent product) runs from 1.19 to 1.51, pushing it back up. Each nearly undoes the other in most rows.

BFCL, the Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard, scores a multi-turn entry correct only if it passes every turn, the same rule as all-three-pass.5 It publishes no per-turn rates, so it cannot test clustering. Its single-turn scores say little about the all-turns score. Among the 75 leaderboard entries with at least 80% single-turn accuracy, multi-turn accuracy runs from 0.4% to 77.4% (my computation from the leaderboard data of 16 November; the tasks differ).5 Claude Opus 4.5 scores 68.4% with native function calling and 16.1% in prompt mode, at single-turn accuracies of 88.6% and 89.7%, a sensitivity to protocol like GPT-OSS-120B’s plain-text answers.

An opening-request score sees neither the follow-up decline nor the clustering. To estimate dialogue completion, score each follow-up in its dialogue and the dialogue as one unit.
