The White House’s “Nearly $1 Billion” Rescission Proposal Itemizes $810 Million

Its posted proposal contains $809,501,022, while the legal clock depends on congressional receipt that the public record does not establish.

7 min read2026-09-27

Eleven lines produce two totals

On September 25, the White House announced “another nearly $1 billion” in what it called pocket rescissions. Its 11 public bullets add to $810 million.1 The special message posted by the Office of Management and Budget gives exact figures for the same 11 proposals. They total $809,501,022, which is $190,498,978 short of $1 billion. The message rounds its own total to $810 million.2

Those figures describe proposed rescissions. Under the Impoundment Control Act, a rescission is a cancellation of budget authority that the president proposes and Congress may enact. The proposal does not cancel the money by itself. The act permits withholding while Congress considers the request, then says the money “shall be made available for obligation unless” Congress completes a rescission bill within 45 calendar days of continuous congressional session.3 An obligation is a legal commitment by the government to pay.

The special message identifies each target by a Treasury appropriation fund symbol, or TAFS. Each symbol's source-year pair matters. A 2025/2026 balance and a same-named 2026/2027 balance are different budget authority with different availability periods.

The enacted laws and the message together establish that these 11 identified balances end their availability for new obligations on September 30. They do not establish that every dollar under each broad account name ends then. Fiscal 2026 Housing Counseling Assistance, for example, remains available through September 30, 2027, while this proposal targets its 2025/2026 predecessor. Education for the Disadvantaged also contains advance-funded money that remains available into 2027.6

September 30 is not day 45

Five days separate the September 25 announcement from fiscal year-end. The ICA does not count ordinary elapsed days from an announcement or a web posting. Its 45-day period begins after Congress receives the special message and excludes a day when either chamber is adjourned for more than three days to a date certain. The act requires transmission to both chambers on the same day and allows delivery to the Clerk of the House or Secretary of the Senate during an adjournment.3

The posted cover letter is addressed to House Speaker Mike Johnson. No public record located by the September 27 reporting cutoff establishes receipt by both chamber officers. The legal clock's start date therefore remains unknown. The 2025 episode shows why that field matters: GAO found that the House received that message on August 28 and the Senate on September 8, and it counted the continuous-session period from September 9 through October 23.12

The claim changes with the document

A single phrase such as “the funds were rescinded” collapses records that answer different questions. The public file has enough evidence to identify a proposal and the enacted end dates for its specified balances. It does not have enough evidence to turn the proposal into an accomplished cancellation.

Expiration has a narrower effect than cancellation. A fixed-period appropriation may be charged only for expenses properly incurred during its availability period. After that period, the account ordinarily remains open in expired status for five fiscal years so existing obligations can be recorded, adjusted and paid. Only when the account closes is the remaining balance canceled.13 The proposal, the September 30 lapse in new-obligation authority and the later statutory cancellation are three separate events.

The dispute begins where the clocks diverge

The administration's strongest case starts with the text of the ICA. Its 2025 Supreme Court filing argued that the rescission provision allows withholding for the full 45-day period, sets no deadline for sending a special message and contains no end-of-fiscal-year bar. The neighboring deferral provision expressly bars a deferral beyond the end of a fiscal year. On this reading, Congress knew how to write that restriction and omitted it from the rescission process. The government also invoked late-year practice under Presidents Ford and Carter.14 A Congressional Research Service analysis laid out the same textual and structural argument while describing the competing view.15

GAO reads the act's mandatory release language together with Congress's enacted availability period. In a 2018 decision, it concluded that the ICA permits a temporary hold, but requires release early enough for “prudent obligation before the amounts expire.”16 GAO applied that conclusion to the 2025 pocket-rescission package and found the withholding unlawful.12 Senator Patty Murray, the vice chair of the Senate Appropriations Committee, made the current congressional objection in political terms on September 25, calling the 2026 move illegal and describing it as “over $800 million” in funding.17 Her statement also alleges withholding outside this 11-item package; those broader allegations are not included in its $809,501,022 total.

The Supreme Court's September 2025 intervention did not settle this merits dispute. The Court stayed part of an injunction after finding that the government had made a sufficient preliminary showing about private plaintiffs' Administrative Procedure Act claim and mandamus remedy. Its order said the ruling “does not represent a final determination on the merits.”18 GAO's decisions likewise are congressional-branch legal opinions, not Article III judgments.

September 30 can make the targeted balances unavailable for new obligations because Congress attached that date when it appropriated them. Whether the executive branch may withhold those balances until that date after posting this rescission proposal is the legal question the Supreme Court's 2025 order did not decide.18 At most three potentially countable session days fall before expiration, but without both receipt dates the public record cannot establish even that many.
