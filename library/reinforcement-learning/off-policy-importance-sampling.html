Reinforcement Learning

Importance sampling values a policy from episodes another policy played

Scaling each return by a product of per-move probability ratios makes the average unbiased, but on Sutton and Barto's one-state loop its variance is infinite unless the behavior moves left over 90% of the time.

19 min read2026-11-09

Why this matters

An agent that explores makes moves its final policy never will, so its episodes describe the exploring policy. To value another policy, such as the greedy one Q-learning learns about, it must use episodes that policy never played. Monte Carlo prediction can do this if, before averaging, it multiplies each return by how much likelier the target policy makes the episode than the behavior policy that played it. That factor is a product of per-move probability ratios, and the method is called importance sampling. Its plain average is unbiased, and its ratio-weighted average is biased but bounded. Given the two policies' move probabilities, you can work out a logged episode's ratio by hand, prove the plain average unbiased, and find which behaviors give it infinite variance on a loop. Off-policy n-step methods and PPO's objective, both later in the course, are built on the same ratio.

How much likelier the arrows make each logged episode

Suppose an agent on the slippery grid has a log of 1,000 episodes. The ε-greedy policy around the hand-drawn arrows played them with ε = 0.5, each from a random cell other than the goal and the pit. We want what the arrows themselves earn from such a start. exact_value solves it from the grid's rules as 1.36.

In the terms of the SARSA lesson, the arrows are the target policy, written π, and the ε-greedy policy is the behavior policy, written b. Averaging the log's returns, as Monte Carlo prediction does, gives −5.06, near the ε-greedy policy's exact value of −4.81. Every return in the log is a sample of what b earns.

Two episodes from the log show what has to change (Fig. 1). Episode A aims along the arrow on all three of its moves, and its last move slips into the pit. Episode B aims right in cell 11, where the arrow points up, slips to where the arrow would have taken it, and reaches the goal.

The arrows make each of A's aims with probability 1, and the ε-greedy policy makes each with probability 1 − ε + ε/4 = 0.625. Each of A's aims is therefore 1/0.625 = 1.6 times as likely under π as under b. A slip has probability 0.1 whichever policy aimed, so it drops out of the comparison, and episode A is 1.6³ = 4.10 times as likely under the arrows. B's first aim has probability 0.125 under b and 0 under π. The arrows never play episode B, so it should count for nothing, though it reached the goal.

The general rule compares the two probabilities of any episode S0, A0, S1, …, ST. Under π it has probability ∏ π(At|St) p(St+1|St, At), where p is the environment's probability of the landing cell. Under b it has the same product with b in place of π. Their quotient is the episode's importance-sampling ratio.1

The environment's probabilities cancel, so the ratio needs only the two policies and the aimed moves, which every logged step records.

Scaled returns average to the target's value wherever b covers π

A return multiplied by its episode's ratio is called a scaled return, ρG. For episodes that start in a cell s, scaled returns average to vπ(s), the target policy's value. Sutton and Barto state this as their equation 5.4 without deriving it.1 The derivation sums over the episodes τ that b can play from s, with probabilities Pb(τ) and Pπ(τ) under the two policies.

Line 3 needs every episode π can play to be one b can play. That holds when b(a|s) > 0 wherever π(a|s) > 0, a condition called coverage.1 Coverage forces b to be stochastic wherever it differs from π. The ε-greedy policy gives every move at least ε/4, so it covers any target on the grid. Without coverage, line 2 sums only b's episodes, so the episodes of π that b never plays drop out of the average. With G replaced by 1, the same lines show that under coverage the ratios themselves average to 1.

Ordinary importance sampling averages the scaled returns of n episodes. Each term averages to vπ(s), so the estimate is unbiased for every n. Weighted importance sampling divides by the sum of the ratios instead.1

The weighted estimate gives return i the weight ρi⁠/⁠Σj ρj. The weights are nonnegative and sum to 1, so the estimate lies between the smallest and largest return in the log. The ordinary estimate has no such bound. Episode A alone adds 4.10 × −18.10 = −74.14 to its sum, far below the grid's lowest return of −20. The weighted estimate is a ratio of two averages, so it is biased. The bias goes to zero as n grows, since its numerator over n approaches vπ(s) and its denominator over n approaches 1.1

Each episode contributes one return, from its first cell, so both estimates are first-visit methods. The every-visit versions of both are biased.1

Q-learning needs no ratio. Its target starts from the move already made, and its max is the greedy target's own average over the next move, as the SARSA lesson derived.1

When a loop makes the ordinary estimate's variance infinite

Sutton and Barto built a one-state case where ordinary importance sampling fails (Fig. 2).1 In state s, moving left returns to s with probability 0.9 and otherwise ends the episode with reward +1. Moving right ends it with reward 0. The target always moves left, so with no discount its value is 1. Write β for the behavior's probability of moving left.

A move right sets the ratio to 0, since π never makes it. An episode that loops k times and then ends with left has ratio β−(k+1) and return 1, so the weighted estimate averages only returns of 1. The ordinary estimate's variance is Var(ρG)/n, finite only if the scaled returns have a finite mean square. One factor of ρ in that square trades b's probabilities for π's, as in the derivation above.

Each pass around the loop multiplies the episode's probability under π by 0.9 and its ratio by 1/β. When 0.9/β is at least 1 the terms never shrink, and the sum is infinite. That holds for every β up to 0.9, so even a behavior that moves left 90% of the time gives the ordinary estimate infinite variance. Above 0.9 the sum is 0.1/(β − 0.9). At β = 0.95 that is 2, so the scaled returns have variance 2 − 12 = 1.

The divergence also needs episodes that can loop without limit. If no episode could run past T moves, the ratio would be at most β−T and the variance finite, though possibly enormous.

loop.py runs ten times for a million episodes at each β (Fig. 3). At β = 0.5 the estimates jump whenever a long all-left episode arrives, and the largest scaled return in a run is between 8,192 and 131,072. After a million episodes the ten estimates lie between 0.735 and 0.893. With uv run loop.py 0.95 they end between 0.999 and 1.002, about the spread of 1/√n that a variance of 1 predicts. The weighted estimate is exactly 1 in every run from the first episode that ends with left.

At β = 0.5 the ordinary estimate still converges to 1 with probability 1. The episodes are independent and their scaled returns have a finite mean, which is all the strong law of large numbers needs. Sutton and Barto's caption for their own ten runs says the estimates "do not converge to this value".1 Their text says the runs "fail to converge" even after millions of episodes,1 as the runs in Fig. 3 do after one million. As a statement about the limit, the caption is wrong. No number of episodes, though, gives the ordinary estimate a finite standard deviation.

Problems

1. On the loop with β = 0.95, about how many episodes give the ordinary estimate a standard deviation of 0.01? Compare the answer with the output of uv run loop.py 0.95.

Answer

The scaled returns have variance 0.1/(0.95 − 0.9) − 1 = 1, so the average of n of them has standard deviation 1/√n. A standard deviation of 0.01 needs n = 10,000. At 10,000 episodes the ten runs lie between 0.986 and 1.022.

2. Suppose every return in a log from the grid were 10 larger, and the true value with them. How does the error of each estimate change?

Answer

The weighted estimate becomes Σρi(Gi + 10)/Σρi = Vwtdn + 10, so its error is unchanged. The ordinary estimate becomes Vordn + 10 × (1/n)Σρi. It stays unbiased, since the average ratio has mean 1, but its error gains 10 times that average's distance from 1. The ordinary estimate's error depends on where the returns sit relative to 0, and the weighted estimate's does not.

The takeaway

Importance sampling values a target policy π from episodes that a behavior policy b played. It multiplies each return by ρ, the product over its episode of π(a|s)/b(a|s). The environment's probabilities cancel out of that ratio. When b covers π, these scaled returns average to vπ, so their plain average, ordinary importance sampling, is unbiased. Weighted importance sampling divides by the sum of the ratios instead, which bounds it by the returns at the cost of a bias that fades. The ordinary estimate's variance is infinite when episodes can loop without limit and each pass grows the ratio at least as fast as it shrinks the episode's probability under π.
