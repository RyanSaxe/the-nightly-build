Reinforcement Learning

Reporting position in raw millimetres keeps PPO from using a cart's velocity

One step size has to suit the largest input, so the units decide how fast a linear learner learns until each component is standardized.

22 min read2026-11-13

Why this matters

An environment reports its observation in whatever units its author picked, and a learner that takes gradient steps uses one step size for all of them. The component with the largest scale caps that step size, and the weight on the smallest learns slowest under the cap. For linear features, the expected update from the semi-gradient lesson measures both. Each eigenvalue of E[xxᵀ] sets the pace of one direction of the weights, so the eigenvalues give the largest stable step size and the slowest weight's pace before any run. Centring each component and dividing it by its standard deviation, called observation normalization, takes the units out. For PPO, the normalization statistics become part of the agent, saved and reloaded with it.

A step that suits metres overshoots in millimetres

Dock is a small environment built for this lesson. A cart on a 2 m track has to stop at a dock at the right end, arriving at 0.1 m/s or less, and a faster arrival bounces it back. Each step the agent pushes left, coasts or pushes right, and every step pays −1, so a return is minus the number of steps to dock. The observation is the cart's position and velocity (Fig. 1). With units="m" the position is in metres, and with units="mm" it is in millimetres. The velocity stays in metres per second.

Suppose an agent learns a fixed policy's value with a linear estimate v̂ = wᵀx, where x = (1, position, velocity), by gradient Monte Carlo. The constant 1 gives the estimate an offset. Every weight starts at 0. From the state in Fig. 1, dock.py's scripted policy docks in 19 steps, so the return G is −19. With step size α = 0.1, the update w ← w + α(G − v̂)x adds −1.9x to the weights (Table 1).

millimetres (1, 500, 0.2) (−1.9, −950, −0.38) −475,002

In metres the estimate moves an eighth of the way to −19. In millimetres it lands about 475,000 past it. Only the position differs, and it is 1,000 times larger, so its weight's change is 1,000 times larger. The weight it needs is 1,000 times smaller, since a weight per metre is the same number divided by 1,000 per millimetre. Against what it needs, the change is 10⁶ times larger. A step size 10⁶ times smaller would undo that, and the velocity weight, whose input has not changed, would then move 10⁶ times more slowly. Averaged over many states, the same arithmetic sets the largest stable step size and the pace of the slowest weight.

Each eigenvalue of E[xxᵀ] sets one direction's pace

Averaged over the states it updates, the gradient Monte Carlo update takes the form the semi-gradient lesson gave TD's expected update, with the return in place of TD's target.

Subtract w*, and the error e = w − w* follows the recursion that the deadly-triad lesson read stability from. Here A = E[xxᵀ] is symmetric, unlike TD's E[x(x − γx′)ᵀ], so its eigenvalues λᵢ are real and at least 0, and its orthogonal eigenvectors uᵢ split the error into independent directions.

Each direction shrinks when |1 − αλᵢ| < 1, so one step size has to stay below 2/λmax, the bound LeCun and colleagues derive for any quadratic error.1 The error along the smallest eigenvalue's direction is then multiplied by at least 1 − 2λmin/λmax per step, so the number of steps it takes to shrink grows with the condition number κ = λmax/λmin. The step size 2/(λmin + λmax) gives the slowest and fastest directions factors of the same size, (κ − 1)/(κ + 1), and no single step size does better.2

Reporting one component as numbers c times larger, here c = 1,000 for millimetres, replaces x with Dx, where D is diagonal with c in that component's place and 1 elsewhere. A becomes DAD, which scales the component's row and column by c and its diagonal entry by c².

Suppose component i is uncorrelated with the other features, so that E[xᵢxⱼ] = 0 for every j ≠ i. Its unit vector is then an eigenvector of A with eigenvalue E[xᵢ²], and DAD multiplies that eigenvalue by exactly c². Against the constant feature, E[xᵢ · 1] = E[xᵢ], so uncorrelated means a mean of zero. With correlated features an eigenvector mixes several components, and its eigenvalue moves by a factor of the order of c², not exactly c².

Standardizing replaces each component xᵢ with (xᵢ − mᵢ)/sᵢ, where mᵢ and sᵢ are its mean and standard deviation. A change of units scales xᵢ, mᵢ and sᵢ alike, and an offset shifts xᵢ and mᵢ alike, so both cancel. Standardized components have mean 0 and variance 1, so A holds a 1 for the constant feature, zeros beside it, and the components' correlation matrix in the rest. Uncorrelated components give A = I and κ = 1. Correlated ones keep some elongation: equalizing variances "will not make the error surface spherical".1 Centring matters on its own, since "a nonzero mean in the input variables creates a very large eigenvalue".1 The first problem below works one out.

The runs below apply the expected update, the average over every start, which is what 2/λmax bounds. A run that updates on one sample at a time multiplies that sample's residual G − v̂ by 1 − α‖x‖², 0.87 and −24,999 in Table 1, so its step size also has to suit the longest feature vectors it meets.

A million stable steps in millimetres leave the velocity weight mostly unlearned

dock.py holds the environment and the scripted policy, and every listing here runs with uv run from one folder that holds it, on Gymnasium 1.4.0, the current release.3 Run on its own, it checks Dock with check_env in both unit systems and prints the return behind Table 1.

linear.py fits the scripted policy's return from each of 2,000 start states, once for each form of the observation, and leaves out the states each episode visits after its start. Dock draws a start's position and velocity independently, each with a mean of zero, so at the starts the features are close to uncorrelated.

Each bound sits where the eigenvalues put it. In metres the constant feature has the largest eigenvalue, 1, so α has to stay below 2. In millimetres the position's eigenvalue is 999,997 times its value in metres, which is c² = 10⁶ up to the sample's slight correlation, and the bound falls to 7.4 × 10⁻⁶. In every row, 1.01 times the bound sends the weights more than 10⁵ times w*'s length away within 1,000 steps, and 0.99 times the bound does not.

The pace follows κ. In metres κ is 34, so each step at 2/(λmin + λmax) multiplies the distance from w* by (κ − 1)/(κ + 1) = 0.94 or less. That predicts 1% within 77.5 steps, and the run takes 76. Standardized, κ is 1.03 and one step is enough. In millimetres κ is 9 million and the prediction is 21 million steps. After a million, the velocity weight has reached 3.8 of its 19.6, so the estimate still misses most of what velocity tells it. The position weight is no further along. Its direction has the largest eigenvalue, whose factor is −(κ − 1)/(κ + 1), so its error flips sign every step and shrinks just as slowly.

In metres the contours of Fig. 2 are ellipses three times taller than wide, because the position's eigenvalue is 9 times the velocity's. The position weight reaches its best value within six steps, and the velocity weight climbs for the other seventy. Standardized, the contours are circles, the negative gradient points at w*, and the first step lands beside it. Drawn in millimetres, the ellipses would be 3,000 times taller than wide.

PPO on raw millimetres steers by the position's sign

Stable-Baselines3 (SB3) 2.9.2 is the current release of the library whose PPO agent the course has already trained.4 Its default policy feeds the observation through two layers of 64 tanh units, trained with Adam.5

SB3's VecNormalize wrapper keeps a running mean and variance of each observation component and passes the agent (obs − mean)/√(var + 10⁻⁸), clipped to ±10.6 By default it also scales rewards, and norm_reward=False turns that off, so only the observation differs between runs. agents.py trains PPO with SB3's defaults for 50,000 steps per run, on raw and on normalized observations in both unit systems, with seeds 0 to 4. Each frozen greedy policy plays 100 episodes in an evaluation environment seeded apart from training. The 20 runs take three to four minutes on four CPU cores.

Every raw run in millimetres ends with a policy that ignores velocity. Seed 0's policy pushes away from the track's centre at any speed (Fig. 3), and fewer than half its episodes dock. A start left of the centre sits at the buffer until the 200-step limit ends the episode. A start right of it docks after about nine bounces, since in Dock's 0.1 s steps a cart pushed right returns from each bounce about one push, 0.05 m/s, slower. The other four policies also split the track at its centre and pick one move for each side. Seed 1 pushes toward the centre from both sides, so its cart never reaches the dock.

Through VecNormalize, runs in metres and in millimetres spread over much the same range of steps, and seed 2 is the weakest in both, so a normalized run's result depends on its seed whatever the units. Each normalized policy pushes right below some speed and coasts or pushes left above it. Run as uv run agents.py 5, the script trains seeds 5 to 9 instead. Their raw millimetre policies again all but ignore velocity and their normalized ones use it, and their weakest normalized seed almost never docks, in metres and in millimetres alike.

The eigenvalue argument covers one step size and a linear estimate, and SB3's PPO has neither. In metres the linear learner comes within 1% of w* in 76 steps. PPO's raw policies there still use velocity at as few as 2% of grid positions, against 83% or more through VecNormalize, so the linear learner's κ does not predict how PPO does. PPO's tanh units saturate on large inputs, which LeCun and colleagues say leaves the error surface flat far from the origin.1 Adam gives every weight its own step size, unchanged when that weight's gradient is scaled.7 These runs do not separate either effect from conditioning, so they show what the units do to PPO without showing why.

Evaluating a normalized policy needs its saved, frozen statistics

A normalized policy learned from observations shifted and divided by its own running statistics, so those statistics are part of the policy. SB3's model.save leaves them out,8 and agents.py saves them beside the model with env.save("vecnormalize.pkl"), as SB3's own example does.9 VecNormalize.load(path, env) restores the statistics and the flags saved with them, training=True among them, so agents.py sets training and norm_reward to False after loading. The statistics then stay frozen and the rewards stay raw.69

A newly built VecNormalize(env, training=False, norm_reward=False) starts at mean 0 and variance 1, so it passes raw observations through, clipped to ±10.6 For the fresh statistics row of Table 2, agents.py plays the five normalized millimetre networks that way, and none docks in more than 8% of its episodes. Four of the five networks from seeds 5 to 9 fail the same way, and the fifth docks less often than through its own statistics.

Problems

1. Dock could report the position as the distance from the buffer, 0 to 2 m, in place of the distance from the centre. With x = (1, distance, velocity) in metres, what happens to A's largest eigenvalue and to κ, and what does standardizing do?

Answer

The distance is the position plus 1 m, so its mean is 1 and its mean square is 1 + 0.272, with 0.272 the position's eigenvalue from linear.py. The constant and the distance share the block [[1, 1], [1, 1.272]], whose eigenvalues add to 2.272 and multiply to 0.272: about 2.14 and 0.13. κ rises from 34 to about 72, and the largest stable step size falls from 2 to about 0.93. Standardizing subtracts the mean, so it gives the same features as before.

2. Suppose Dock's starts made the position and velocity correlated, with correlation 0.9. After standardizing, what are A's eigenvalues and κ, and which single step size is best?

Answer

A holds a 1 for the constant feature and the correlation matrix [[1, 0.9], [0.9, 1]], so its eigenvalues are 1, 1 + 0.9 and 1 − 0.9. κ is 1.9/0.1 = 19, so the error surface stays elongated. The best single step size is 2/(0.1 + 1.9) = 1, with a factor of 18/20 = 0.9 per step in the slowest direction.

The takeaway

For a linear estimate learned with one step size α, the expected update multiplies the error along each eigenvector of E[xxᵀ] by 1 − αλ, so the largest eigenvalue caps α at 2/λmax and the smallest sets the slowest weight's pace. Reporting an uncorrelated component as numbers c times larger multiplies its eigenvalue by c². In Dock, a linear learner that came within 1% of its best fit in 76 steps in metres had not done so after a million steps in millimetres. PPO on raw millimetres all but ignored velocity in every seed, an effect of the units that these runs measure without explaining. Standardizing each component takes the units out, provided the agent keeps its statistics, saved beside it and frozen to evaluate it.
