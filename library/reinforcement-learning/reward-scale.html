A courier paid for every move never delivers

On a seven-by-four delivery grid, paying in cents keeps every optimal route, and clipping rewards to [−1, 1] sends the courier from its depot to the near drop-off.

19 min read2026-11-21

Why this matters

Before a courier agent trains, its rewards may be rewritten for the learner's sake: paid in cents instead of dollars, clipped to [−1, 1], shifted by a constant on every move, or divided by a running statistic. The optimal policy is the one whose value is largest in every state, so the question is whether a change can reorder the policies' values. Multiplying every reward by a positive number multiplies every return by it, and the order stays. Clipping changes the ratios between rewards. Adding a constant adds an amount that grows with the episode's length, which differs from policy to policy wherever episodes can end. Ng, Harada and Russell named this question policy invariance under reward transformations. Two short derivations will let you take a reward wrapper and say whether it can change the optimal policy of the environment it wraps.

A fee ten times as large is worth five more moves

A courier works a grid of 7 columns and 4 rows. It starts at the depot, on the left edge one row from the top, and moves up, right, down or left. A move into the edge leaves it where it was. Two cells are drop-offs, and a move into either ends the episode. The near one, two moves from the depot, pays 1. The far one, in the top right corner seven moves away, pays 10. Every other move pays −1, a common charge for asking an agent to be quick.1 Read the rewards as dollars: a fee for each delivery and a dollar of fuel for each move. The discount is γ = 0.95 throughout.

From the depot, the near route is one fuel charge and then the fee, worth −1 + 0.95 × 1 = −0.05. The far route is six charges and then a fee discounted by 0.95⁶, worth −5.30 + 7.35 = 2.05. The far drop-off wins by 2.10, so the optimal courier spends five more moves for the larger fee. Value iteration finds the optimal move in every cell (Fig. 1).

Three cells in the bottom left corner deliver near. From the cell left of the near drop-off, the near fee is one move away and worth 1, while the far drop-off is eight moves away and worth 0.95.

In courier.py, rule returns the next cell, the reward and whether the episode terminated. Courier wraps it in Gymnasium's reset and step, and value_iteration sweeps the Bellman optimality backup over the same function. Each entry of REWARDS is a reward version, a function applied to every reward. uv run courier.py prints what each choice is worth from the depot under each version, in under a second.

Paying in cents multiplies every value by 100

Now pay the courier in cents. Every reward is multiplied by 100, so a move costs 100 and the fees are 100 and 1,000. In the cents row of the output, every value from the depot is 100 times the dollar row's, and every cell keeps its greedy move.

To see why, multiply every reward by a number k. A return weights each reward by a power of γ and adds them up, so k comes out of the sum.

A value is an average of returns, so v′π(s) = k vπ(s) for every policy π and every state s. When k > 0, multiplying both sides of vπ(s) > vπ′(s) by k keeps the inequality. A policy whose value is largest in every state under one reward is largest under the other, so the two rewards have the same optimal policies, with v′* = k v* and q′* = k q*. The greedy move in a cell has the largest q*, and multiplying the cell's four action values by the same positive k leaves the largest one largest. A negative k reverses every inequality and makes the worst policy optimal. With k = 0 every policy is optimal.

Ng, Harada and Russell state the result in a footnote: beyond their shaping theorem, the only change with the same kind of guarantee is to rescale rewards by a fixed positive factor.2 The derivation is about the expected discounted return, the objective of every lesson so far. Under the entropy-regularized objective of soft actor-critic, which a later stage teaches, the reward's scale changes the learned policy.3

Clipped at 1, the far fee no longer pays for its extra moves

Clipping to [−1, 1] replaces each reward with the nearest number in that range. On the courier it changes one number. The far fee of 10 becomes 1, while the fuel charge of −1 and the near fee of 1 are already in range. From the depot, the far route is now worth −5.30 + 0.95⁶ × 1 = −4.56, and the near route is still worth −0.05. With equal fees, the nearer drop-off wins. Sixteen of the 26 cells now deliver near, every cell that is fewer moves from the near drop-off than from the far one (Fig. 2).

The scaling derivation needed one k for every reward. Clipping multiplies the far fee by 1/10 and every other reward by 1, so the ratios between rewards change, and with them the order of the routes. A clip that touches no reward changes nothing, so whether a clip moves the optimal policy depends on the task (Problem 1).

DQN, the network version of Q-learning that a later stage builds, clipped Atari rewards to [−1, 1] during training, to make one learning rate easier to use across 49 games. Its authors state the cost: the agent cannot tell rewards of different size apart.4 Stable-Baselines3's ClipRewardEnv for Atari takes the reward's sign instead.5 The two agree on Atari's whole-number score changes and part on a reward such as 0.3, which clipping keeps and the sign turns into 1.

A constant added to every reward pays longer trips more

Now add a constant c to every reward, the fees included. Sutton and Barto ask what this does in a task that never ends and in an episodic task such as a maze, and print no answer.1 Take an episode that terminates after N moves. The shift adds c to each of its N rewards, with the powers of γ those rewards carry.

In a continuing task, one with no terminal state, N is infinite under every policy, and every return gains the same c/(1 − γ). Every value rises by that amount and no policy's rank moves, which answers the first exercise. In an episodic task the amount depends on N, and N depends on the policy. With c > 0, a policy whose episodes last longer gains more, and one whose episodes never end gains the most. That answers the second exercise: a constant can change the optimal policy whenever episodes can end.

On the courier, c = 1 makes fuel free. A move pays 0, and the fees become 2 and 11. The two-move route from the depot gains 1 + 0.95 = 1.95 and the seven-move route about 6, so the far drop-off's lead grows. The three cells that delivered near now deliver far (Fig. 3, fuel-free panel). From the cell left of the near drop-off, a fee of 2 one move away loses to a fee of 11 eight moves away, since the longer trip no longer burns fuel.

With c = 2 the courier is paid by the hour. Every move pays +1, and the fees become 3 and 12. Staying out of both drop-offs forever is worth 1 + 0.95 + 0.95² + ⋯ = 1/(1 − 0.95) = 20, the kind of geometric sum that priced the loop in specification gaming. Delivering far from the depot is worth 14.12, so the optimal courier never delivers, from any cell (Fig. 3, hourly panel). Here the discount has to be below 1. At γ = 1 staying out would have no finite value, and value iteration would not converge.

Adding c is the shaping reward of the constant potential Φ = −c/(1 − γ), since γΦ − Φ = c. The potential is the same at the drop-offs, where potential-based shaping needed 0. The last term of the shaped return, γNΦ(SN), is then −γNc/(1 − γ), which depends on when the courier delivers and vanishes only for an episode that never ends.

Q-learning finds each version's routes from rewards alone

Value iteration solved each version from its rules. A Q-learning agent has only the rewards it receives, so we check that it ends on the same routes. learn.py trains it on Courier under each reward version, which Gymnasium's TransformReward wrapper applies. A TimeLimit of 100 moves cuts any episode that has not delivered. The cut is a truncation, so the target still bootstraps there and aims at value iteration's values. The agent is ε-greedy with ε = 0.5 and a constant step size of 0.1, and each episode starts in a random cell. Each version gets five runs of 20,000 episodes, graded against its own q*. learn.py needs courier.py in the same folder and runs in about 40 seconds.

learn.py, complete

Every run delivers where value iteration says from all 26 cells, and ends within 0.001 of its version's q*, so Figs. 1 to 3 also show where the learned agents deliver. The clipped and dollar rows differ only in the far fee. Put it back at 10, and the clipped agent's near delivery from the depot becomes the dollar agent's far one. The hourly and fuel-free rows differ only in a dollar per move. Take it off the hourly wage, and the agent that never delivered now delivers from every cell.

Only a nonzero start lets Q-learning tell cents from dollars

Q-learning started from estimates of 0 makes the same choices in cents as in dollars. If every estimate in the cents run is 100 times the dollar run's, so are its target and its update, and every greedy choice is the same. Both runs start at 0 and use the same seed, so that holds after every update. A learner can tell cents from dollars only through a number that stays put when the rewards are multiplied, such as a nonzero starting estimate. Sutton and Barto call starting estimates of +5 wildly optimistic for true values with mean 0 and variance 1.1 Paid in cents, the same +5 would sit near the middle of the true values.

A reward normalizer divides. By default, VecNormalize in Stable-Baselines3 2.9.2 divides each reward by the standard deviation of a running discounted sum of rewards and then clips the result to ±10.6 The PPO code that Engstrom and colleagues studied divides the same way, and neither subtracts a mean.7 While its divisor holds still, a normalizer is a positive scale, and its clip touches only a reward more than ten divisors large. Subtracting the mean too would add a constant to every reward, the kind of change that kept the hourly courier from delivering. In OpenAI Baselines issue #538, unixpickle, one of its two assignees, explains why the code does not subtract it: doing so would change the environment. In his example, an agent whose rewards are all 1 wants to live, and one whose rewards are all 0 does not care whether it lives or dies.8

Problems

1. A second courier company uses the same grid and discount but pays 0.2 at the near drop-off and 0.9 at the far one, and charges 0.01 for every other move. From the depot, where does the optimal courier deliver under these rewards, under the rewards clipped to [−1, 1], and under their signs?

Answer

Far, far and near. The far route is worth -0.01\,(1 - 0.95^6)/0.05 + 0.95^6 \times 0.9 = 0.61 and the near route −0.01 + 0.95 × 0.2 = 0.18. Every reward already lies in [−1, 1], so clipping changes nothing. The sign turns the fees into 1 and the charge into −1, which is the clipped courier of Fig. 2, and from the depot that courier delivers near.

2. Keep the hourly courier's +2 on every move that does not deliver. What should a delivery move pay so that the optimal policy is the dollar courier's again?

Answer

Its dollar fee plus 2/(1 − 0.95) = 40, so 41 near and 50 far. Give every cell that is not a drop-off the potential Φ = −40, and the drop-offs 0. A move between two such cells then gains 0.95 × (−40) + 40 = 2, and a delivery gains 0 + 40 = 40, so the change is that potential's shaping reward. By potential-based shaping, every return from a cell gains the same 40, whether the courier delivers or not, so every optimal move is the dollar courier's.

The takeaway

A change to the rewards keeps the optimal policy when it keeps the order of every policy's values. Multiplying every reward by k > 0 multiplies every return by k, so the courier paid in cents takes its dollar routes. Clipping to [−1, 1] makes both fees 1 and sends the courier at the depot to the near drop-off. Adding c to every reward adds c(1 − γN)/(1 − γ) to an N-move episode, which differs between policies whenever episodes can end. At c = 2 the courier never delivers. Q-learning learned every version's routes. A normalizer that only divides is a positive scale until its divisor moves or its clip cuts a reward.
