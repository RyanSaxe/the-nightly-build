Reinforcement Learning

Q-learning values a rolling ball's cell by how often each speed reaches it

Gymnasium's two-frame stack gives Q-learning's table the speed that one cell leaves out, and the agent then hits all 12 pitches at every exploration rate tried.

20 min read2026-11-14

Why this matters

An environment often reports only part of its state, such as where a ball is but not how fast it moves. A table of action values keyed on that observation gives one entry to situations that need different moves. The Markov property, so far asked of states, can be asked of observations: the next observation and reward should depend only on the current observation and action. Giving the agent its last few observations as one observation, called frame stacking, restores the property when those observations fix the missing part. DQN, a deep Q-learning agent for Atari games, takes its four most recent frames as one input because, its authors write, one screen can't tell many game states apart.1 Here you predict from visit counts what Q-learning learns when a ball's speed is hidden, and why exploration changes it.

At cell 2, the right move depends on a speed the agent can't see

Pitch is a small environment built for this lesson (Fig. 1). A ball rolls along a track of ten cells toward the agent at cell 0. It starts in one of cells 4 to 9 at a speed of 1 or 2 cells per step, and each of these 12 pitches is equally likely. Each step the agent waits or swings, and it gets one swing. A swing hits if the ball reaches cell 0 during that step, which pays 1. A miss or a ball that gets past pays 0, and any of the three ends the episode. The observation is the ball's cell.

Suppose the ball is at cell 2. If it came from cell 3, its speed is 1, and a wait brings it to cell 1, where a swing hits. If it came from cell 4, its speed is 2 and it reaches cell 0 during this step, so only a swing now hits. The two balls need opposite moves, and both show 2.

Call the cell and the speed together the hidden state. Hidden states that give the same observation are aliased, after Whitehead and Ballard's term perceptual aliasing.2 The hidden state has the Markov property. The cell doesn't, because what follows a wait at cell 2 depends on the cell before it.

A table keyed on the ball's cell

pitch.py holds the environment. Run on its own, it checks Pitch with check_env and prints two frame stacks used later.

qlearn.py is Q-learning with its table keyed on whatever the environment returns, a cell or a stack of cells. An episode lasts at most nine steps, so the update doesn't discount. With rewards of 1 and 0, the optimal value q*(s, a) is then the chance of a hit when the agent takes a in hidden state s and plays the best moves after. Each estimate's step size is N(x, a)−0.7, with N counting its updates.

runs.py trains 20 agents, seeds 0 to 19, for 20,000 episodes at ε = 0.1. It prints the share of the 12 pitches their greedy moves hit and their mean estimates at cells 2 and 3, beside columns the next section derives. It needs pitch.py and qlearn.py in its folder and takes up to 30 seconds per setting on one CPU core.

runs.py, complete, and its output with one frame

Every agent hits 9 of the 12 pitches. At cell 2 the agents put a wait at 0.64 and a swing at 0.36, so they wait, and the fast balls from cells 4, 6 and 8 get past. Neither number is a ball's value. A wait is worth 1 to the slow ball and 0 to the fast one, and a swing the reverse.

Each entry averages over the hidden states behind it

Each Q-learning update moves an estimate part of the way toward its target. As in the sarsa lesson, the estimates stop drifting when each equals its target's average. Here that average runs over the visits to entry X in which the agent takes a. They come from the hidden states s that give X, written s → X. Per episode the agent visits s an expected ηπ(s) times, the count from the semi-gradient lesson, and takes a with probability π(a | X), which is the same for every s behind X and cancels. From s, the target averages to the expected reward plus the next entry's discounted largest estimate.

The shares come from π. A policy that visits the hidden states behind X in other proportions changes the entry for X, though the environment is the same. With one hidden state per observation, π drops out. Singh, Jaakkola and Jordan's proof holds the behavior policy fixed, while an ε-greedy agent's policy moves with its estimates.4 In Pitch the shares at cells 2 and 3 depend only on moves from cell 3 up, where a swing always misses and the greedy move is a wait from the first episode. The runs therefore test equation (4) at shares that ε alone sets.

The shares count which starts reach a cell. With ε near 0, all six slow balls reach cell 2, and so do the fast balls from even cells, while those from 5, 7 and 9 jump from cell 3 to cell 1. Cell 1 is worth 1 to every ball, since a swing there always hits, so a wait at cell 2 gets (6 × 1 + 3 × 0)/9 = 2/3 and a swing 1/3.

The runs learned 0.64, not 2/3, because exploration also sets the shares. A slow ball takes twice as many steps to reach cell 2, and it survives each one with probability 1 − ε/2, the chance of no exploratory swing. At ε = 0.1 the extra steps cut the slow share to 0.64. The function exact in runs.py applies these weights to every entry, and its column matches the runs.

Singh, Jaakkola and Jordan define an observation's value as the share-weighted average of its hidden states' values, which Monte Carlo's averaged returns reach and equation (4) in general does not.4 At cell 3 a wait is worth 1 to every ball, since the greedy moves after it, a wait at cell 2 and a swing at cell 1, hit both speeds. Its entry is 0.76, because a slow ball's target is cell 2's wait estimate, 0.64, which also counts the fast balls from cells 4, 6 and 8 that never pass cell 3. With starts only in cells 5, 7 and 9, no fast ball reaches cell 2, and the entry rises to 1.00, though cell 3 still holds both speeds.

The last two cells give the speed

The speed is the earlier of two consecutive cells minus the later. Feeding the agent its last k observations as one observation is frame stacking, a version of what Sutton and Barto call the kth-order history, updated by shifting the newest observation in and the oldest out.3 Their version keeps the actions in between as well. The library wrappers keep only observations, which in Pitch loses nothing, since a swing ends the episode and every earlier action is a wait. Where earlier actions change the hidden part in ways the frames don't show, the stack needs them.

Gymnasium's FrameStackObservation keeps the last stack_size observations, oldest first.5 The function key in qlearn.py makes each stack a table entry. Stable-Baselines3's VecFrameStack, which stacks frames for its agents, accepts only Box and Dict spaces, so it can't wrap Pitch's cells.6 At an episode's first step Gymnasium's wrapper pads the stack by repeating the one real observation, or with zeros when padding_type="zero".5 pitch.py printed both for a ball that starts in cell 6 at speed 2.

The padded stack [6 6] shows a ball that hasn't moved, and both speeds produce it, so two frames fix the speed only from the second step. VecFrameStack pads with zeros, so its first stack is aliased too.6 Pitch's starts make the first step harmless. Every ball starts at cell 4 or farther, so not even a fast ball reaches cell 0 on the first step. A wait is worth 1 to every hidden state behind a first stack, and so is the entry, their average. The first problem shows what a nearer start costs.

Every two-frame agent hits all 12 pitches. Its entries hold each ball's own values (Fig. 2), and both cell-3 entries are worth 1. The stack is the only change from the one-frame runs, so the hidden speed accounts for all three misses.

Exploration changes what one frame is worth

In the sarsa lesson, π dropped out of Q-learning's averaged target. Equation (4) brings it back through the shares. Passing --eps 0.1 0.3 0.5 0.7 0.9 repeats both sets of runs at five rates (Fig. 3).

The output of the runs at five rates

A higher ε ends more slow balls before cell 2, so the one-frame estimate for a wait there falls and the one for a swing rises. Equation (4) has them cross at ε = 0.55, and at ε = 0.5 some runs already swing. From ε = 0.7 every run swings at cell 2 and loses all six slow balls, hitting 6 of 12. The two-frame estimates stay at 1 and 0, and those agents hit every pitch at every rate.

Problems

1. Add cell 2 to Pitch's starting cells, so that 14 pitches are equally likely. Which hidden states share the two-frame agent's first stack [2 2], what does equation (4) give for its entry, and what share of pitches can the agent hit? Would padding_type="zero" help?

Answer

The slow and the fast ball that start in cell 2 share it. The slow ball needs a wait and the fast ball a swing. Each is visited once, at the first step, so the shares are equal and the entry is 0.5 for each move. Either move misses one of the two, so the agent hits 13 of 14 pitches, 0.93. Zero padding gives [0 2], which the same two balls produce, so it doesn't help.

2. Give each ball a hidden acceleration of 0 or 1 too, so that after each step its speed grows by that amount. How many frames make the observation Markov, and from which step of an episode?

Answer

Three. Two frames give how far the ball last rolled but not how far it rolls next, so [5 3] can go on to cell 1 or to cell 0. Three frames give two distances, whose difference is the acceleration. The stack holds three real cells only from the third observation, so the starts would have to make the first two decisions the same for every ball.

The takeaway

A table keyed on an observation that hides part of the state gives one entry to every hidden state behind it. Q-learning's entry rests at the average of their targets, weighted by how often the policy visits each, so in Pitch exploration moves the entry for cell 2 and, past ε = 0.55, flips the agent's move there. Stacking the last two cells gives the speed, which restores the Markov property from the second step on, and Pitch's starts make the first step harmless. Q-learning then hits all 12 pitches at every ε tried.
