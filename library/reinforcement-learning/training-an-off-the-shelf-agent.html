Reinforcement Learning

PPO's greedy arrows outscore the sampled moves its training curve averages

Of five Stable-Baselines3 runs on SlipperyGrid, an exact solve finds three on the optimal arrows and two one arrow short, a gap that ten evaluation episodes per run cannot detect.

18 min read2026-11-08

Why this matters

Stable-Baselines3 is a library of off-the-shelf agents. While one of its agents trains, it logs a training curve, the average return of recent training episodes. Its PPO agent plays those episodes with moves sampled from a stochastic policy, so the curve scores that sampling policy. A trained agent should be judged by a different policy. Freeze the network, take its most probable move in each state, and play that greedy policy in a separate evaluation environment, for several training seeds, beside baselines such as random moves and a scripted policy. Henderson and colleagues call judging the policy an agent ends with the policy optimization view. On SlipperyGrid the course can solve every one of those values exactly, the sampling policy's included. On an environment of your own, each baseline is played in the evaluation environment too, for a number of episodes set by the smallest gap the comparison has to show.

Seed 0's training curve and its frozen arrows

We train the PPO agent of Stable-Baselines3, SB3 for short, on SlipperyGrid with seed 0 and a budget of 50,000 steps. SB3 logs its training curve as rollout/ep_rew_mean, the average over the last 100 training episodes of each episode's undiscounted sum of rewards.123 The course discounts every return at GAMMA = 0.9, so we record the same 100 episodes' returns at GAMMA, and in this lesson the training curve means that discounted average. Every value here is averaged over starts drawn uniformly from the 14 cells reset uses, as the course's baselines were solved. Seed 0's curve ends at 1.01. Random moves earn −10.90 and the hand-drawn policy earns 1.36, so by its curve the agent ends a little below the arrows drawn by hand.

Then we stop training and keep the network fixed. Its policy, which no longer changes, is called a frozen policy. In each cell we take the move the network rates most probable. Those 14 moves, one per cell, are seed 0's frozen greedy policy, and every one of them is optimal (Fig. 1). exact_value from baselines.py solves a policy's value from the grid's rules, and it puts this policy at 1.61, the value of an optimal policy.

The curve scores a different policy from the frozen greedy one. PPO's policy is stochastic: in each cell its network gives each of the four moves a probability, and in training the agent samples its move from them.4 Seed 0's network makes an optimal move the most probable one in every cell, yet in every cell a sampled move is sometimes not optimal (Fig. 1), and in cell 9 it is optimal only 0.57 of the time. Training episodes make those other moves and lose value on them, much as an ε-greedy agent pays the price of ε for its random moves. The greedy policy never makes them.

PPO as a library call

SB3 trains its agents on environments with Gymnasium's interface, and 2.9.2 is its current release.5 The course builds PPO in its stage on policy gradients, and nothing here depends on how PPO updates its network.

agent.py trains it on SlipperyGrid and sets one hyperparameter. PPO's discount, gamma, becomes the course's GAMMA = 0.9 in place of SB3's default of 0.99,6 so the agent maximizes the return that exact_value measures. Every other setting keeps SB3's default. The listing's Returns wrapper records every training episode's return at GAMMA, and the training curve averages the last 100.

move_probs reads the policy's probabilities in all 16 cells at once. greedy_moves keeps the most probable move in each cell, the move SB3's model.predict returns with deterministic=True, and asserts that the two agree.4

The listings run with uv run from one folder that also holds gridworld.py, estimators.py, mc_control.py, control.py, grid_env.py and baselines.py, from the lessons in the Background band. SB3 does not promise the same results across PyTorch releases or platforms,7 so your printed numbers may differ from these.

Which policy the training curve scores

Machado and colleagues argue for reading the training curve. For Atari games they propose reporting the average of the last training episodes, a protocol “requiring an agent to perform well while it is learning”,8 because scoring a policy after learning “hides issues of sample efficiency”.8 Henderson and colleagues name two views. The online view scores the rewards an agent collects while it learns, and asks how well it does as it learns. The policy optimization view scores the policy the agent has learned, in an evaluation kept apart from learning, and asks what policy the agent ends with.9 That is the question when an agent is put to use after training, and this lesson asks it.

For PPO, SB3's guide advises trying the most probable move in place of a sampled one, which “frequently leads to better performance”, and warns that the training curve “is a good proxy but underestimates the agent's true performance”.10 SB3's log and this lesson's curve average the same episodes, so the warning can be checked here. exact_value takes any policy as a table of move probabilities, so it solves the policy PPO samples from, move_probs(model), as readily as the greedy policy. seeds.py trains seeds 0 to 4 and scores each run both ways (Table 1).

With sampled moves the five networks are worth 1.36 to 1.43, about what the hand-drawn policy earns. With greedy moves the same networks are worth 1.51 to 1.61, and three of them are optimal. Which policy is scored decides whether an agent looks no better than arrows drawn by hand or nearly optimal.

Training episodes are played with sampled moves, so the curve estimates what sampled moves earn. PPO updates its network once per batch of 2,048 steps,63 and in every seed the last 100 episodes fall inside the last batch, so the network one update before the frozen one played them. seeds.py prints a standard deviation of 8.6 for one training episode's return, so the curve's end has a standard error of 0.86. Each seed's end lies within about one standard error of its sampled value, from seed 1's 0.45 against 1.41 to seed 4's 1.94 against 1.43, so the curve cannot show what the final update changed.

So SB3's warning holds for what the curve estimates, since in every seed sampled moves are worth less than greedy ones. One curve's end is too noisy to show it. Four of the five fall below the hand-drawn 1.36, though all five greedy policies earn more than it, and seed 4's lies above even its greedy 1.61.

Ten evaluation episodes cannot sort five seeds

Without an exact solve, a frozen policy is scored by playing it. SB3's guide asks for a separate test environment to score an agent while it trains, “because most algorithms use exploration noise during training”.10 After training, a second SlipperyGrid changes only the random stream that draws starts and slips. seeds.py builds one with a seed training never uses and the same 200-move TimeLimit. SB3's own evaluation function, evaluate_policy, sums rewards undiscounted,11 so seeds.py plays the greedy policy for 10,000 episodes with returns from baselines.py. The first 10 match evaluate_policy's default count.11

Ten episodes put every seed far above random moves' −10.90 (Fig. 2). They cannot place a seed between the hand-drawn and optimal values. seeds.py prints a standard deviation of 8.5 for one evaluation episode's return, so the mean of 10 has a standard error of 2.7, more than ten times the 0.25 between those two values. The 10-episode means run from 1.27 to 5.82, and they rank seed 0, whose arrows are all optimal, last.

With 10,000 episodes the standard error falls to 0.09, and every mean lands near its exact value. The order can still be wrong. Seed 3 scores 1.59 and seed 4 scores 1.50, yet seed 4's arrows are all optimal and seed 3's are not. Their exact values, 1.55 and 1.61, differ by less than one standard error.

Seeds 2 and 3 each end with one arrow that is not optimal (Fig. 3). Seed 2 aims right in cell 8, and seed 3 aims left in cell 9. With that one cell aimed up instead, each greedy policy is worth 1.61, the line under each grid in Fig. 3, so the single arrow accounts for each shortfall. These runs do not show why a seed stops one arrow short, which depends on how PPO updates its network.

The five seeds share code and settings, and they still end on different greedy policies. Seeds 5 to 9, from uv run seeds.py 5, end the same way, four of them on the optimal arrows.

So report each seed's number and set it between baselines. On an environment of your own, with no exact solve, the baselines are random moves and a scripted policy you write, as the course wrote the hand-drawn one, each played in the evaluation environment like the agent. Choose the number of episodes from the smallest gap d the comparison has to show. A mean of n episodes has a standard error of σ/√n, where σ is the standard deviation of one return. With n ≥ (3σ/d)², a true gap of d spans at least 3 standard errors, a size the lesson on checking a custom environment flagged as more than noise, so noise rarely reverses the order of two policies that far apart. When both means are estimated, each needs about twice as many episodes. On SlipperyGrid the hand-drawn value is exact, so telling an optimal seed from it, a gap of 0.25, takes (3 × 8.5 / 0.25)², about 10,000 episodes, the count seeds.py ran. Were the hand-drawn policy played too, each would need about 20,000. Telling seed 3's 1.55 from the optimal 1.61, a gap of 0.06, takes some 180,000.

Problems

1. SB3's DQN agent takes a uniformly random move with probability 0.05 in training, once the first 10% of its training steps are over.12 Suppose a DQN run ends with the optimal arrows. Where would the end of its training curve sit on average? Solve the ε-greedy policy around those arrows with exact_value.

Answer

Its training episodes play the ε-greedy policy around the optimal arrows, with ε = 0.05. exact_value solves that table directly. price.py runs from the same folder as the listings above.

The curve would end near 1.08 on average, below the hand-drawn policy's 1.36, while the same run's frozen greedy policy earns 1.61. Any one window of 100 episodes adds its noise on top.

2. SB3's EvalCallback scores the current policy every eval_freq steps on 5 evaluation episodes by default. It records the highest mean so far and, given a save folder, saves that model as the best one.13 Suppose an optimal policy that never changes were scored that way 30 times, with returns at GAMMA. Would the highest of the 30 means be near 1.61?

Answer

No. Each 5-episode mean has a standard error of about 8.5 / √5, nearly 4, so the 30 means scatter widely around 1.61, and the highest lies several units above it. Machado and colleagues call the best score across training “a statistically biased estimate” that needs a second, independent evaluation.8

The takeaway

A training curve averages training episodes, and PPO plays those with moves sampled from its policy, so the curve scores that sampling policy. A trained agent is judged by its frozen greedy policy instead, played in a separate evaluation environment, seed by seed, between baselines. On SlipperyGrid the sampled policies of five seeds were worth 1.36 to 1.43 and their greedy policies 1.51 to 1.61, against 1.36 for the hand-drawn policy and −10.90 for random moves. Two seeds stopped one arrow short of optimal. Ten evaluation episodes, with a standard error of 2.7, could not show which two, and 10,000 still ranked one of them above an optimal seed.
