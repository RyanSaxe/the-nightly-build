Reinforcement Learning

The value of a state is an average you can solve for

On a slippery 4×4 grid, 20,000 episodes and one linear solve put cell 13 at about −2, and the discount turns out to be needed only by a policy that never ends.

23 min read2026-10-31

Why this matters

A walker on a slippery grid follows a fixed rule for its moves. How good is each cell to stand on? One run says little: on the grid you are about to see, one walk from cell 13 scored 0.63, and the true value of that cell is −2.01. How do you get the −2.01 without running the grid forever?

DeepMind needed the same quantity for Go. To build the network in AlphaGo that scores a position, they fixed one way of playing, had it play itself, and regressed the network on over 30 million positions, each from a separate game, to predict the outcome when that way of playing takes both sides. Go has too many positions to give each one an equation. A grid of sixteen cells has room for all of them, so we can solve for the exact answer and check it against a simulation.

01
Sutton and Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction: chapter 3 (printed pp. 47-60) gives these definitions in the field's standard notation, with more examples.

One episode on a slippery grid

The grid in Fig. 1 has sixteen cells, numbered 0 to 15 row by row from the top left.

The environment takes an action and returns a reward and a next state. The agent is whatever supplies the action, and everything else is the environment.1 A state is what the agent has in hand when it chooses, here its cell number. An action is one of up, right, down or left. A reward is the number the environment pays for one move. At time t the agent in state S_t takes action A_t, and one step later it receives reward R_{t+1} and is in state S_{t+1}. In Fig. 1 the agent in state 13 takes up, receives −1 and lands in state 9.

Moves slip. With slip probability 0.2, a move goes as intended with probability 0.8 and to each perpendicular side with probability 0.1, and a move into an edge leaves the agent where it is. Arriving at cell 3, the goal, pays +10. Arriving at cell 6, the pit, pays −20. Any other move pays −1. The goal and the pit are terminal states: arriving at one ends the run.1 An episode is one run from a start state to a terminal state, seven moves in Fig. 1.

A policy, which Bellman's 1954 address framed as a prescription in terms of the current state, is the rule that picks the action in each state.2 Formally it maps each state to a probability for each action, \pi(a \mid s).1 The small arrows in Fig. 1 are one policy, drawn by hand, with probability 1 on a single action in each cell. From here on it stays fixed, and the question is how good each cell is under it.

The Markov decision process

The formal object is the Markov decision process, a decision process Bellman set out in 1957 and now written as the tuple (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma): states, actions, transition probabilities, rewards and a discount rate, the weight that shrinks later rewards.34

For cell 5 and action up, P puts 0.8 on cell 1, 0.1 on cell 4 and 0.1 on cell 6. The reward here depends only on the arrival cell, so it is written R(s').

The Markov property is the assumption hidden in that notation: the next state depends on the history only through the current state and action.4

Read loosely, this says the past does not matter. The past does matter: it decided which cell the agent stands in. The property says that once the cell is known, the path that led there changes nothing about what comes next. It is a restriction on the state, which has to carry every aspect of the past that makes a difference to the future.1

Value is an average of returns

The return G_t is the sum of the rewards after time t, each weighted by a power of the discount rate \gamma.1

Fig. 1 uses \gamma = 0.9. That episode returned 0.63: six moves at −1 with falling weights, then the goal's +10 at weight 0.53. The same sum obeys a recursion, G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1}.1

Any five-move walk to the goal, the fewest moves possible, returns 3.12, and 8,386 of the episodes did that. The sample mean is −1.94 with a standard error of 0.05. "How good is cell 13" is taken to mean this average, because slips make episodes from one cell differ and no single episode answers it.

The value of state s under policy \pi is that average taken over all episodes, the expected return from s, and it is 0 at a terminal state.1 Exactly, v_\pi(13) = -2.01.

The Bellman expectation equation

The definition needs whole episodes. Peeling off the first move gives an equation that needs only one move. The first step replaces the return with its recursion.

The next step averages over the arrival cell. Let P_\pi(s' \mid s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) P(s' \mid s, a) be the probability that the policy and the environment together take s to s'. Landing in s' fixes the first reward at R(s').

This is the step that needs the Markov property. Given S_{t+1} = s', each later state depends only on the state and action before it, and the policy picks each action from the current state alone. So everything later has a distribution that does not depend on S_t, and the condition on S_t drops. Neither the transition probabilities nor the policy changes with time, so what is left is the definition of v_\pi(s').

Substituting gives the Bellman expectation equation.4

The intended move contributes 2.107 and the 0.1 chance of the pit contributes −2.000, so a cell three moves from the goal is worth 0.06. The arithmetic uses v_\pi(1), and v_\pi(1) has v_\pi(5) in its own equation, so one substitution does not finish the job.

Cycles between cells force a joint solve

The Bellman expectation equation is one linear equation for each of the 14 non-terminal cells, in 14 unknowns.1 Fig. 4 shows which unknowns each equation uses.

At slip 0 each cell has one successor and the graph has no cycles, so the equations solve back to front in one pass: v(2) = 10, then v(1) = -1 + 0.9 \times 10 = 8, and on through the order in Fig. 4.5 The code runs that pass, and it matches a matrix solve to within 9 \times 10^{-16}.

Slip 0.2 adds arrows that rule this out. Cell 1 can slip down to cell 5, and cell 5 moves up to cell 1, so each value appears in the other's equation and neither can be computed first. A cell appearing in its own equation is a smaller matter. Cell 2 can slip up into the edge and stay put, which only takes a division.

Only cycles through two different cells force a joint solve. If the cells can be ordered so that each equation uses only itself and earlier cells, then at \gamma < 1 one pass with one division per cell solves the system. Such an order exists exactly when no cycle runs through two or more cells, because two cells on one cycle would each have to come before the other.

Randomness is not the cause: turning cell 1 to face cell 0 at slip 0 makes a cycle with no randomness in it, and cells 0 and 1 then have to be solved together. At slip 0.2 the groups in Fig. 4 are the graph's strongly connected components, and solving them in order, each from the ones before it, also matches the matrix solve.6

The direct solve

Let Q be the matrix P_\pi restricted to non-terminal cells, since the goal and the pit have value 0. Let b(s) = \sum_{s'} P_\pi(s' \mid s) R(s') be the expected reward of one move from s. All the equations together read v = b + \gamma Q v, which solves in one step.4

Sweeps

Iterative policy evaluation starts from a guess and applies the equation as an update, v_{k+1} = b + \gamma Q v_k, replacing every cell once per sweep.1

From v_0 = 0, sweep k holds the expected discounted reward over the first k moves from each cell, so news of the goal and the pit spreads outward one cell per sweep. Cell 12 is six moves from the goal. It sinks to −4.43 at sweep 5 while only step costs and the pit are in reach, and has climbed to −2.19 by sweep 8. The converged panel shows the risk: cell 13 is one move closer to the goal than cell 12 and is worth less, −2.01 against −1.86, because its episodes spend more moves beside the pit. The straight route from 13 passes the pit at cells 5 and 2, and a slip to the right takes it past cells 10 and 7. The straight route from 12 passes the pit only at cell 2.

Why sweeps converge under a discount

Write T(v) = b + \gamma Q v and \lVert x \rVert_\infty = \max_s |x(s)|, the max norm. For \gamma < 1 the map T is a contraction in this norm: it multiplies the distance between any two vectors by at most \gamma.78

The inequality holds because each entry of Qx is a sum of entries of x with non-negative weights totalling at most 1. The same bound gives \lVert (\gamma Q)^k \rVert_\infty \le \gamma^k, so \sum_k (\gamma Q)^k converges. Multiplying by I - \gamma Q telescopes to I, so the sum is the inverse, and v exists and is unique. Since T(v) = v, putting u = v_k and w = v gives one factor of \gamma per sweep, from every start.

Only a policy that never ends needs the discount

The discount sets how much a reward counts by how far ahead it lies: k moves ahead, it carries weight \gamma^k. At γ = 0 each cell shows its expected next reward. Cell 0, three moves from the goal, climbs through −1.00, −0.52, 2.16 and 3.43 as γ goes from 0 to 1.

The last panel has no discount at all, and the solve and the sweeps both work: the largest error is 1.35 \times 10^{-7} after 30 sweeps. The contraction proof gives nothing here, because its factor is 1 and cell 0's row of Q sums to 1. What works instead is that this policy is proper: every cell has a path of positive-probability moves to a terminal cell.9 Take m long enough for all those paths and let \epsilon be the smallest probability, over cells, of ending within m moves, which is positive because each cell has such a path. Row sums of Q^m are survival probabilities, so \lVert Q^m \rVert_\infty \le 1 - \epsilon and \lVert Q^{jm} \rVert_\infty \le (1-\epsilon)^j \to 0.10 Since \lVert Q \rVert_\infty \le 1, the powers in between are no larger, so Q^k \to 0. That puts the spectral radius \rho(Q), the largest eigenvalue magnitude, below 1 and makes \sum_k Q^k the inverse of I - Q, so v exists and is unique.10 The error after k sweeps is Q^k (v_0 - v), so it goes to 0 from every start.

Changing four arrows so that the bottom row presses against the bottom wall gives the policy called STUCK in the code. A slip there only moves along the wall, so no episode that enters the bottom row ever leaves it.

The bottom row's block of Q has rows summing to 1, so \rho(Q) = 1 and I - Q has no inverse. Every move there pays −1, so at γ = 1 cell 13 falls by 1 per sweep without end. Every γ below 1 restores a finite answer: paying −1 forever is worth -1/(1-\gamma), which is −10 at γ = 0.9 and −100 at γ = 0.99.1 With that −1 per move, a cell falls exactly when an episode from it can get stuck, so at γ = 1 cells 8 to 15 fall and the top two rows settle at the values in Fig. 6. A discount gives every policy an answer, and a terminal cell gives one only to policies that reach it. Whether cells must be solved together is a separate question: the fixed policy at slip 0.2 has cycles and still ends with probability 1.

The measured rate

The error after k sweeps is (\gamma Q)^k (v_0 - v), and the asymptotic factor per sweep of such an iteration is the spectral radius of its matrix.11 Here \rho(\gamma Q) = 0.4404, measured at 0.4406 by sweep 30, against the 0.9 of the bound, which is the worst case and which the stuck policy attains. The radius has to be that of Q, restricted to non-terminal cells, because the goal and the pit hold value 0 and carry no error. Kept as absorbing rows, they add an eigenvalue 1 for every policy, which would put the radius of the scaled matrix at exactly γ. Its eigenvectors are nonzero at the goal or the pit, so sweeps from v_0 = 0 never excite it. The guide in Fig. 8 is anchored at sweep 30 and describes the tail only, and the first sweeps fall more slowly than it.

Two files that print the results

Changing the line GAMMA, SLIP = 0.9, 0.2 reruns everything at another setting. gridworld.py holds the environment and the two policies as arrays. The sweep keeps two arrays, the old values and the new ones.1

Problems

1. By hand, find v_\pi(7) at \gamma = 1 and slip 0.2. Cell 7's policy is up.

Answer

Up reaches the goal with probability 0.8, a slip left reaches the pit with probability 0.1, and a slip right hits the edge with probability 0.1. So v = 0.8(10) + 0.1(-20) + 0.1(-1 + v), which gives 0.9 v = 5.9 and v = 6.56. That matches the γ = 1 grid in the output.

2. Once the fixed policy's error is on its tail at γ = 0.9, how many sweeps does it take to shrink the error by a factor of 1,000? How many does the bound \gamma^k predict?

Answer

\ln 1000 / \ln(1/0.4404) = 8.4, so 9 sweeps. The bound predicts \ln 1000 / \ln(1/0.9) = 65.6, so 66 sweeps.

The takeaway

The value of a cell is the average return from it, and you do not have to simulate to get it. The Bellman expectation equation turns that average into a linear system, which put cell 13 at −2.01, where 20,000 episodes had put it at −1.94 give or take 0.05.

The cells have to be solved together only where a cycle runs through more than one of them, and the discount is not what made the fixed policy solvable. It converges at γ = 1, its error settling to a factor of 0.49 per sweep. Press four of its arrows into a wall and γ = 1 sends the bottom row down by 1 per sweep without end. That second policy is the one that needs a discount, and I would remember the pair of them before any of the equations.
