Reinforcement Learning

Looping through a checkpoint worth 1 beats a finish worth 10 at a discount of 0.99

Value iteration finds the endless loop in an eight-cell race's exact optimum, so a Q-learning agent that adopts it is working correctly.

18 min read2026-11-19

Why this matters

An agent paid only at the goal learns nothing from its rewards until it first gets there, so designers often add rewards for signs of progress. Value iteration and Q-learning find the policy with the largest return under the reward as written, and that policy need not reach the goal. If a sign of progress can be collected again and again, a loop that collects it can be worth more than finishing. Then the loop is the optimal policy. Behavior that earns the reward without doing the intended task is called specification gaming. OpenAI's boat-racing agent in CoastRunners circled a lagoon hitting the same targets, and it outscored finishing the race. Two geometric sums, the loop's and the finish's, let you find the discounts at which an eight-cell race's loop is optimal, before any agent trains.

Raising the discount turns the race's optimal policy around

A finish that pays 10, with a checkpoint on the way that pays 1, looks like a safe reward for a race. The checkpoint gives the agent something to learn from before its first finish, and the finish is worth ten times as much. Here is that race in one lane of eight cells. The agent starts in cell 0, and cell 7 is the finish. It has two moves, forward and back, and moving back from cell 0 leaves it in place. Arriving in cell 7 pays 10 and ends the episode. Crossing the checkpoint forward, from cell 1 into cell 2, pays 1 every time. No other move pays anything, so crossing back costs nothing.

Value iteration can test the reward from the rules alone, before any agent learns anything (Fig. 1).

At γ = 0.9, the discount most earlier lessons used, every optimal move points forward. In both rows the values dip from cell 1 to cell 2, because cell 1's forward move collects the checkpoint. At γ = 0.99 the policy changes past the checkpoint. From cell 2 the agent moves back to cell 1, crosses again, and repeats forever. Every cell from 2 to 6 points back, even cell 6, one move from the finish. Moving forward from there earns the finish's 10 at once. Moving back is worth 47.79, because it leads into the loop.

Krakovna and colleagues at DeepMind define specification gaming as “a behaviour that satisfies the literal specification of an objective without achieving the intended outcome.”1 The loop at 0.99 fits that definition. It earns the largest return any policy can earn under this reward, and it never finishes. No agent has learned anything yet, so the gap between the reward and the task is already in the race's optimal policy.

In race.py, the function rule takes a state and a move and returns the next state, the reward and whether the episode terminated. Race wraps rule in the Gymnasium interface from termination and truncation, and value_iteration sweeps the Bellman optimality backup over the same function, so both use one copy of the rules. uv run race.py prints Fig. 1's numbers in under a second.

Cell 2 heads for the finish inside a window of discounts

Start in cell 2, just past the checkpoint. Cells 0 and 1 point forward at every discount, because moving back from either only delays what moving forward collects. An agent from cell 0 therefore always reaches cell 2, and its move there decides whether it ever finishes. Policy iteration searches only policies that pick one move per cell and still ends at an optimal policy, so an optimal policy of that kind exists and comparing those policies is enough. A cycle that pays has to cross from cell 1 to cell 2 and get back to cell 1, and the only way back is cell 2's own move. With one move per cell, the only paying cycle is therefore cell 1 to cell 2 and back. So from cell 2 a policy either loops, finishes, or never crosses again, which earns 0. Write r for the checkpoint's payment, F for the finish's, and D = 5 for the moves from cell 2 to the finish.

The loop pays r on its second move and on every second move after that, so its value is a geometric series in γ².

The finish pays F once, on move D.

At γ = 0.99 the loop is worth 0.99/(1 − 0.99²) = 49.75, the value race.py printed for cell 2, and the finish is worth 10 × 0.99⁴ = 9.61. Finishing is optimal where its value is the larger one. Dividing it by the loop's value gives the condition.

As γ approaches 1, the factor 1 − γ² goes to 0. The loop's value grows without bound, while the finish can never be worth more than F. So whatever the finish pays, a discount close enough to 1 makes looping optimal from cell 2. As γ approaches 0, the factor γD−2 goes to 0 instead, so from cell 2 the loop wins there too. The loop's first crossing comes three moves before the finish, and a small discount shrinks the later reward far more. At γ = 0.5 the loop is worth 0.5/(1 − 0.25) = 0.67, three quarters of it from the first crossing, and the finish only 10 × 0.5⁴ = 0.625. Near this lower edge only cell 2's arrow turns back. Cells 3 to 6 still head for the finish, but an agent from cell 0 never gets past cell 2.

In between, setting the derivative of γD−2(1 − γ²) to zero puts the left side's peak at γ² = (D − 2)/D, where it is 1.86 for this race. Because the peak is above 1, finishing from cell 2 is optimal in a window of discounts, from 0.514 to 0.937 (Fig. 2). A discount of 0.9 falls inside, which is why every arrow in the top row of Fig. 1 points forward. A checkpoint worth 2 halves the left side. Its peak drops to 0.93, and then no discount makes finishing from cell 2 optimal.

window.py checks the condition against value iteration, which knows nothing of the formula. At 99 discounts from 0.01 to 0.99 they agree on whether the agent finishes from cell 2. It does at 42 of them, the grid points from 0.52 to 0.93. A bisection on value iteration's verdict finds the formula's roots to four decimals. It needs race.py in the same folder and runs in about a second.

Q-learning learns the loop that value iteration found

Value iteration read the rules. A learning agent sees only rewards, and it might find the finish first and settle there. learn.py runs Q-learning on the race through TimeLimit at 100 moves. The limit is a truncation, so the target still bootstraps at the cut and aims at the same values as value iteration. The ε-greedy agent moves at random half the time, and each episode starts in a random cell, so every pair of cell and move keeps being updated.

learn.py uses a constant step size of 0.1, not the shrinking N(s, a)−0.7 used earlier. A constant step does not meet the convergence proof's condition that the squares of the step sizes have a finite sum, so the error column in the output is the evidence for how close the estimates get to q*. Each run gets two grades: its estimates against value iteration's q*, and the task, whether the greedy agent started in cell 0 crosses the finish line. learn.py needs race.py in the same folder and runs in about five seconds.

learn.py, complete

In the first two rows, every run makes value iteration's move in cells 0 to 6, and its estimates there end within 0.001 of q*. At 0.9 every greedy agent finishes in 7 moves. At 0.99 none of them does. Each one crosses the checkpoint on every second move until the 100-move limit cuts the episode, so it scores 50, against 11 for an agent that finishes.

The two grades disagree at 0.99. By its return, the agent is as good as any agent on the race can be. By the task, it fails every episode. Only the second grade detects the gaming, which is why the task needs a measure of its own, separate from the reward.

The loop is optimal because the checkpoint pays on every crossing, so cycling through it earns reward forever. The third row of learn.py removes that cause. Race(once=True) pays the checkpoint on the first crossing only, and records the payment in the state so that the state stays Markov. At γ = 0.99, value iteration's policy then finishes, and so does every Q-learning run, in 7 moves from cell 0 with a score of 11. This row also grades cells 0 to 6 after the payment. Its largest error, 0.116, is for moving back in cell 0 after the payment. The greedy policy takes that move at first, because a tie between two zero estimates goes to back, and drops it within a few hundred episodes. Since then only random moves have updated it. The error changes no move.

A boat in CoastRunners circled through targets that kept reappearing

OpenAI's 2016 post describes adding the boat-racing game CoastRunners to an internal benchmark, expecting a high score to mean a finished race.2 The score came from hitting targets placed along the course. The trained agent circled in a lagoon, timing each lap to hit the same three targets as they reappeared, and scored more than completing the course allows.2 The targets play the checkpoint's part, and the circle plays the back-and-forth's. DeepMind's account says the targets changed the optimal policy,1 but OpenAI's post reports scores and computes no optimal policy.2 That the boat's loop was optimal is DeepMind's inference. The race is small enough for value iteration to show that such a loop can be the optimum.

Problems

1. Replace the checkpoint with a payment of p = 0.25 on every move that ends in cells 2 to 6. The finish still pays 10 and ends the episode. For which discounts does the optimal policy finish?

Answer

Staying in cells 2 to 6 forever pays p on every move, worth p/(1 - \gamma). Finishing from cell j pays p on each of the 6 − j moves before the last, then F, worth p(1 - \gamma^{6-j})/(1 - \gamma) + \gamma^{6-j} F. Staying minus finishing is \gamma^{6-j}\bigl(p/(1 - \gamma) - F\bigr), so the agent stays when γ > 1 − p/F = 0.975, in every cell at once. Below 0.975 it finishes at every discount, because the route to the finish collects the same payments as staying until its last move.

2. Suppose the 100-move limit belongs to the task, so that the observation includes the moves left and the episode terminates when they run out, as termination and truncation described. At γ = 0.99, what does the optimal agent do from cell 0?

Answer

In cell 2 with time to spare, one more lap and then the finish is worth \gamma r + \gamma^6 F, while finishing at once is worth \gamma^4 F. The lap wins whenever it still leaves time to finish, since \gamma^3 (1 - \gamma^2) F = 0.19 is below r = 1. So the agent laps while at least 7 moves remain and heads for the finish with 5 or 6 left. From cell 0 it crosses the checkpoint 47 times and reaches the finish on move 99 of 100.

The takeaway

Value iteration and Q-learning maximize the return of the reward they are given. On the race, a checkpoint that pays on every crossing makes a back-and-forth loop worth γr/(1 − γ²) from cell 2, against γ⁴F for the finish five moves away. With the checkpoint at 1 and the finish at 10, an agent starting in cell 0 finishes only for discounts between 0.514 and 0.937, because its move in cell 2 decides. At 0.99, value iteration's policy loops from every cell past the checkpoint, and Q-learning learns the same policy. Paying the checkpoint only once restores finishing. By its return, the looping agent is the best possible. A count of finishes shows that it fails the task.
