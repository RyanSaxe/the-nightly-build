Reinforcement Learning

The best number of torque levels for balancing a pendulum rises with the learning budget

On Gymnasium's pendulum with torque a thousand times dearer, Q-learning does best with 3 levels after 60,000 steps and 9 after 600,000, since each added level is one more estimate per cell.

20 min read2026-11-16

Why this matters

A Q-learning table needs a finite list of actions, and many environments offer a continuous one, such as a torque anywhere between −2 and 2. Offering the agent k evenly spaced torques is a trade. More levels bring the best policy over them closer to what any torque could do. They also give every row of the table more action values to estimate from the same experience. Choosing the levels is action discretization, and k is its one setting. On a pendulum balanced near upright, you will bound the first effect from the torque cost and the spacing alone. You will then measure the second with Q-learning at three budgets, and test whether the fine sets fall behind for lack of updates or for how they explore.

With only full torque, holding the rod still means pushing both ways

Gymnasium's Pendulum-v1 is a rod on a pivot (Fig. 1). Its state is the angle θ from upright and the turning speed θ̇. The agent observes cos θ, sin θ and θ̇, and each step it applies a torque u between −2 and 2 for 0.05 s.1 Gravity adds 15 sin θ to the acceleration and the torque adds 3u, so full torque can hold the rod still only within 23.6° of upright. Episodes are cut off after 200 steps.2

We change two things, both through Gymnasium's own Pendulum-v1. A wrapper charges 1.0·u² for torque, a thousand times the environment's 0.001·u². Every episode starts within 0.3 rad of upright and turns at most 0.5 rad/s, through the reset options Pendulum-v1 already takes.1 The dearer torque is what makes the number of levels matter, as the next section derives. The near-upright starts keep every episode in the small region the learner's table covers, since a table a laptop can fill learns the full swing-up nowhere near its ceiling. The agent picks from k evenly spaced torques from −2 to 2, both ends included.

Start the rod tilted 0.25 rad, at rest. With 65 levels the best policy pushes back at −2 for two steps, then eases the torque toward zero as the rod comes up. Its episode returns −17.0. With three levels it can only push or coast. It pushes at −2 for five steps, coasts at 0, and kicks at −2 again whenever the rod drifts, for −61.0. With two levels it can't coast at all. To keep the rod up it alternates −2 and 2, step after step, and every step pays 4 for torque. That is 800 over the episode, and the solve returns −801.3.

Alternating between neighbouring levels costs at most C(Δ/2)² a step

In Fig. 1 the levels cost return only where the best torque lies between two of them. To see how that cost grows with the spacing, write C for the torque-cost coefficient, 1.0 here. Follow the best policy with any torque allowed, and let u* be the torque it applies at one step. Evenly spaced levels have spacing Δ = 4/(k − 1), so u* lies between two neighbouring levels, a below and a + Δ above.

A policy over the levels can match u* on average by alternating between those two. It applies a + Δ a fraction p = (u* − a)/Δ of the time and a otherwise, each step picking whichever brings the running total of its torque errors back toward zero. Torque enters the step linearly, so the rod follows the best policy's path with a small wobble. Fig. 1 shows this twice: two levels alternate evenly around u* = 0, and three levels mostly coast at 0 with a rare kick at −2. The torque cost does not average out, because it is a square.

A best torque of exactly ±2 costs nothing extra, since both ends are in every set. The wobble costs little. The running total of torque errors stays within Δ/2, so the speed stays within about 0.15·Δ/2 of the best path's. The reward's 0.1·θ̇² term charges at most about 0.002·(Δ/2)² a step for that. The best policy over the levels does at least as well as the alternation, so over 200 steps

The bound is reached when the best torque sits midway between two levels for the whole episode. That is the two-level case of Fig. 1: at the top u* = 0, midway between −2 and 2, and the alternation is the back-and-forth, with the bound's 800. Each halving of the spacing cuts the bound to a quarter. With Pendulum-v1's C = 0.001 the extra torque costs at most 0.8 per episode even for two levels, and the wobble about as much again. Seyde and colleagues show why two levels suffice when the action enters the dynamics linearly, as torque does here, and costs nothing. The best action then sits at one limit or the other wherever it changes the return to first order. Where it doesn't, as while the rod is held still, switching quickly between the limits does the same job.3

Solving backward from the last step gives each set's ceiling

The bound needs no solve, but the true shortfall needs the best policy over each set. Value iteration's sweep finds it. Start from V200 = 0 and back up one step at a time, with no discount.

The state is continuous, so ceiling.py keeps each Vt on a grid of 361 angles by 321 speeds and reads the value of a next state off the four grid points around it. It then plays the policy that picks each step's level by those values, in Pendulum-v1's exact dynamics, from 1000 starts drawn the way the episodes start. These are returns the levels really reach, so they can only understate a set's ceiling. Running pendulum.py on its own checks its copy of the dynamics against env.step. The solve took under a minute here.

pendulum.py and ceiling.py, complete

Every shortfall sits under the bound. Two levels come within 9 of it, because they push back and forth all episode. From 3 to 5 to 9 levels the shortfall falls by about four at each halving of the spacing, from 45 to 11.6 to 2.9, as the bound's (Δ/2)² predicts. Seventeen levels fall short by 1.65, more than a fourfold drop would leave, but there the shortfall is down to the size of the solve's grid error. For 3 to 9 levels the bound runs about four times the shortfall, because the best torque sits midway between levels only during the catch.

Q-learning's best set moves to finer levels as the budget grows

Now let an agent learn. learn.py runs Q-learning on the dearer pendulum through Gymnasium's API. Its table has one row per cell of a 15 × 15 grid over angles within 0.6 rad of upright and speeds within 2 rad/s, and one column per level. Each row averages over the states in its cell. The step size is 0.3, the exploration rate ε is 0.1, and the discount is 0.95, a horizon of about 20 steps. Pendulum-v1 never terminates, so the target bootstraps at every step, including the truncated 200th. After 60,000, 200,000 and 600,000 steps, each run's greedy policy is scored from ceiling.py's 1000 starts. Each set gets five seeds, and a call took about two minutes on four cores here.

learn.py, complete

The best set changes with the budget: three levels after 60,000 steps, five after 200,000, nine after 600,000 (Fig. 2). Five other seeds, 5 to 9, pick the same three sets. At 600,000 steps nine levels beat five by less than the spread between seeds, though, so the third pick is the least certain. Two levels sit at their ceiling of about −802 from 200,000 steps on. The agent has found the back-and-forth, and no budget can do better with ±2. Seventeen levels have the highest ceiling and come last at 60,000 steps, at −1288, behind even two levels.

Equal steps per level remove most of the fine sets' deficit

Count the estimates. With 225 rows, seventeen levels make 3,825 action values and three levels make 675. After 60,000 steps that is 16 updates per action value on average with seventeen levels, and 89 with three. Fig. 3 shows what the difference leaves after 200,000 steps.

Five levels have learned the solve's diagonal band near the centre, where every episode starts. Seventeen levels show little of it. More levels also change how the agent explores. A random move from two levels always pushes at full torque. Seyde and colleagues find that such exploration covers more of the state space, because every random action has full magnitude.3 If that drove the result, the fine sets would catch up once their random moves pushed as hard. The flag --explore-ends restricts every set's random moves to −2 and 2, which every set has.

At 60,000 steps the fine sets' deficit survives intact: seventeen levels score −1279. At the larger budgets full-torque random moves help nine and seventeen levels somewhat, so exploration explains part of the later gaps but none of the early one. The removal test for updates gives every set the same steps per level instead. The flag --per-level runs 30,000 steps per level, 60,000 for two levels and 510,000 for seventeen.

With equal steps per level, nine levels match five, and seventeen levels rise from −1288 at 60,000 steps to −119. They stay behind nine, and their spread of 111 comes mostly from one run that still drops the rod. Most of the fine sets' deficit at a fixed budget is the lack of updates per action value.

Steps per level turn a budget into a prediction. A set of k levels after N steps has as many updates per action value as a set of k′ levels after N·k′/k steps. A finer set wins when the ceiling it adds, which the solve or the bound gives before any run, exceeds what it loses by learning from fewer updates. Nine levels add 8.6 to five levels' ceiling. At 200,000 steps they have the updates per action value that five levels had at 111,000, when five levels were still far from done, so nine levels lose. At 600,000 steps they have what five had at 333,000, after five had stopped improving, and the 8.6 decides it.

Problems

1. Gymnasium's DiscretizeAction wrapper offers the centres of k equal bins, which never reach ±2 and for even k leave out zero.4 With two bins that is −1 and 1. On the dearer pendulum, what does holding the rod upright cost per episode with them, against −2 and 2? From which starts can they hold the rod at all?

Answer

At the top u* = 0 sits midway between −1 and 1, so alternating pays an extra 1·1² = 1 a step, about 200 an episode. That is the bound with Δ/2 = 1, and a quarter of what −2 and 2 pay. But a torque of 1 holds the rod still only where 15·|sin θ| ≤ 3, within 11.5° (0.2 rad) of upright. A start tilted further falls unless it is already turning back toward upright, and a fall costs far more than 200. The wrapper's sets trade reach against spacing.

2. When the best torque sits exactly on a level, how much less is the next level worth? Compare 3 and 17 levels at C = 1, and say what the lesson on action frequency implies for estimates that must rank them.

Answer

The action value's slope in u is zero at u*, so the neighbour, Δ away, is worth less by a constant times Δ². The torque term alone gives C·Δ², 4 with three levels (Δ = 2) and 0.0625 with seventeen (Δ = 0.25), and near the top steering the pushed rod back adds more of the same form. The action gap between neighbours shrinks 64-fold, while the noise in a learned estimate doesn't. As with short time steps, the agent needs far more updates to rank neighbouring levels correctly. Ranking them wrong also costs only that small gap.

The takeaway

Discretizing a continuous action trades a ceiling against experience. Alternating between the two levels either side of the best torque pays at most C(Δ/2)² a step in extra torque, so evenly spaced levels with spacing Δ fall short of continuous torque by at most about 200·C(Δ/2)² over an episode. The solve's shortfall falls about fourfold per halving of the spacing. Each added level is also one more action value per cell to learn from the same steps. On the dearer pendulum Q-learning did best with 3 levels after 60,000 steps, 5 after 200,000 and 9 after 600,000. Equal steps per level removed most of the fine sets' early deficit, and full-torque exploration removed none of it.
