Per-component Q-tables settle wrong only on the rover's steep ledge

Summing one table per component swaps a maximum over 729 joint actions for six maxima over three levels each, and the sum picks the optimum wherever no component's best level depends on the others.

20 min read2026-11-27

Why this matters

A Q-table keeps one value for every action in every state. When an action has several components, such as how a rover drives and sets each instrument, every combination of levels is a separate action, and three levels for each of six components make 729. Writing the action value as a sum of one term per component, a factored action value, splits Q-learning's maximum into one maximum per component. You will derive when components chosen one at a time make the best combination. You will then train a joint and a factored Q-learner on a rover whose action grows from one component to six, and find the one ledge where the factored learner settles on a combination no joint learner chooses.

In cell 3 the best pair is the best row with the best column

A rover drives along a track from cell 0 to a lander in cell 7, where the episode ends (Fig. 1). Each step it sets three components. The drive goes back, stops or goes forward one cell. The arm is stowed, half out or extended, and the mast is down, half up or up. Every step costs 1, and each instrument costs 1 for each level it sits away from its best level in the current cell. On two ledges, extending the arm while the mast is up tips the rover, for 45 more on the steep ledge in cell 2 and 6 more on the shallow one in cell 5. Every reward carries Gaussian noise of standard deviation 1.

In Gymnasium the action space is MultiDiscrete([3, 3, 3]), the cartesian product of three Discrete spaces.1 An action such as [2, 2, 0] means forward, arm extended, mast down. A Q-table over this space holds 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 values per cell. Stable-Baselines3's DQN refuses the space.2

Value iteration over the 27 joint actions gives Q*, the optimal action values (Fig. 2). Both learners below are tabular, so that each of their choices can be checked against Q* cell by cell.

In cell 3 each entry is −3.44 plus an arm term and a mast term. Extended is the best row whichever column you take, and up the best column whichever row, so the best pair is the best row with the best column. Cell 2 breaks the pattern in one entry. Its best pair is still extended with the mast down, but with the mast up the best arm level is half.

Finding one takes Σ nd comparisons instead of Π nd. Storing the values takes Σ nd entries per state instead of Π nd, 9 instead of 27 on the rover and 18 instead of 729 with six components.

Every table takes the same TD error

The factored learner stores one table per component and uses their sum as its action value. After each step it computes one TD error against the target above and adds the same correction to the entry each table used:

Its target is the Q-learning target applied to the summed values, so both learners estimate the value of acting greedily at s′. The update is semi-gradient Q-learning with one feature for each state, component and level: the sum's gradient is 1 for the D entries in use and 0 elsewhere. Tang and colleagues use this sum for one agent's factored action,3 as value-decomposition networks do for teams of agents.4 Tavakoli and colleagues' action branching instead fits each branch on its own to a target that averages the branches' greedy values.5

One update moves the sum for the action taken by Dαδ, which stays below δ with α = 0.1 and at most six components. Tabular Q-learning over joint actions converges to Q* if every pair keeps being updated and the steps shrink suitably. The sum has no such guarantee, since it combines function approximation, bootstrapping and off-policy learning, the deadly triad. The runs below are the only evidence that it settles here.

In learn.py both learners let each component keep its greedy level, except that with probability ε it takes a random level, independently of the others.

Each table ranks its levels by an average over the others

Where do the tables come to rest? Hold the exploration fixed: in state s the agent tries level b of component e with probability μe(b), independently across components. Let y(s, a) be the target's expected value with the next state's entries held fixed. The entry Qd(s, b) stops moving on average when the TD error averages to zero over the steps that use level b:

Component d therefore ranks its levels by the expected target averaged over the other components' levels, weighted by how often the agent tries each.

The ranking gives a sufficient condition. If component d has a level that is best whatever the other components do, every weighted average keeps that level on top. If every component has such a level, their tuple is the joint optimum, and the per-component argmaxes find it. A sum, as in cell 3, is the simplest case.

Cell 2 fails the condition, because the arm's best level is half when the mast is up. With ε = 0.1 the mast stays at its greedy level, down, except when it explores, so it is up on 1 visit in 30. At those rates the average column of Fig. 2 puts extended 0.5 behind half: it saves 1 on the arm's cost and loses 45/30 = 1.5 to tipping. The factored learner should therefore settle on forward, half and down, which Q* puts 1 below the optimum and a joint learner never picks. In cell 5 extended loses only 6/30 = 0.2 and stays 0.8 ahead. Q* is not a sum there, and extended is not best with the mast up, yet the per-component choice stays optimal.

The next cell depends on the drive alone, so the γV*(s′) part of Q*(s, a) is a term in the drive, and Q* is a sum wherever the reward is. rover.py's output shows a gap of 0.00 everywhere but the ledges. A ledge's estimate can change the factored learner's choices in other cells only through that drive term.

The joint learner needs over ten times the steps at six components and the same α

On flat ground, without the tipping penalty, Q* is a sum in every cell, so both learners can end with the optimal action everywhere. scale.py adds instruments one at a time, from the drive alone (D = 1) to five instruments (D = 6), and counts each learner's steps until its greedy action is optimal in all seven cells. It runs 10 seeds with α = 0.1 and ε = 0.1, in about a minute.

scale.py, complete

The joint learner's steps grew 1.8 to 2.5 times with each added component, and the factored learner's far more slowly, to 61,000 against 4,250 at six. With the drive alone the two learners make the same updates. Beyond one component, each joint value changes only on steps that take its joint action, while each factored entry changes on every step that uses its level. From four components up, the slowest factored run beat the fastest joint run, on these seeds and on ten fresh ones.

A stronger joint learner narrows the gap. In a check with steps that shrink as N(s, a)−0.7, the SARSA and Q-learning lesson's rule, it kept pace up to four components and still needed more than twice the factored learner's steps at six.

On the steep ledge alone, the factored learner's arm settles at half

ledges.py trains each learner with the drive, the arm and the mast for 100,000 steps on 20 seeds. A constant step keeps every estimate wandering around its resting point, so ledges.py reads each learner's choice off its tables averaged over the second half. Its --flat flag removes the tipping penalty. A run takes about two minutes.

ledges.py, complete

The joint learner's settled choice is optimal in every cell of every run. The factored learner's is optimal everywhere except the steep ledge, where all 20 runs settle on forward, half and down, as the average predicted. On the shallow ledge every run keeps the arm extended. With --flat no run departs anywhere. Twenty fresh seeds gave the same counts.

The derivation holds the exploration rates fixed. At ε = 0.05 the fixed-rate average puts extended 0.25 ahead on the steep ledge, yet every run still settled on half, and these runs do not show why. Read at the last step instead of averaged, the factored tables also departed in cells 0 and 1 in some runs, more often than the joint tables did.

Problems

1. Suppose a third ledge where the arm's best level is extended, the mast's best is up, and the tipping penalty of 45 falls on that very pair. Which joint actions are optimal there, and where can the factored learner come to rest with ε = 0.1?

Answer

Extended with the mast at half, and half with the mast up, each cost 1. Neither is a resting point. From extended and half, the mast is up on 1 visit in 30, so extended scores 1 − 45/30 = −0.5 against half and the arm moves to half. From half and up, the mast's table puts up 0.5 behind half in the same way. Half with half agrees with both averages and costs 2, worse than either optimum.

2. The joint learner explores the same way, so it also tries extended with the mast up on the steep ledge. Why does its settled choice stay optimal there?

Answer

A joint entry moves only on steps that take its own joint action, so tipping lowers only the entries with the arm extended and the mast up. Forward, extended and down still rests at its Q*, −4.10, the largest in the cell. As in the lesson on Q-learning, the policy that makes the moves decides how often each entry is updated, not where it rests.

The takeaway

With six components on flat ground, the factored learner needed 4,250 steps to the joint learner's 61,000. Its action value sums one table per component, so Q-learning's maximum splits into one maximum per table and each state needs Σ nd entries instead of Π nd. Trained on one shared TD error, each table ranks its levels by the target averaged over the other components' levels, weighted by how often the agent tries them. The per-component choice is optimal wherever each component has a level that is best whatever the others do. On the steep ledge that average put half ahead of extended, and the factored learner settled on a pair no joint learner chose.
