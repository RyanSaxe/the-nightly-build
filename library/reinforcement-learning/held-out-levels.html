Held-out levels catch a grid agent that memorized its training levels

A Q-learning agent that sees nearby walls and the goal's direction matches its training return on 500 new levels after training on 64, while a position-only agent closes the gap by failing its training levels.

18 min read2026-11-22

Why this matters

A Q-learning agent trained on one seven-by-seven grid reaches the goal on it every time, by a shortest route. Its return there cannot say whether it learned to find goals or learned this grid's route, since both policies earn it. So the separate evaluation environment has to hold new levels, drawn from the same generator with their seeds kept out of training. The difference between the return on training levels and on these held-out levels is called the generalization gap. What the agent observes decides whether held-out return can rise to meet a high training return. You will key a Gymnasium environment's levels to seeds, split the seeds, report both returns, and judge from an observation alone whether a policy acting on it could solve levels it has not seen.

Both agents solve their one training level and few held-out ones

LevelGrid is a seven-by-seven grid built for this lesson. The agent starts in the top-left cell, (0, 0) as (row, column), and moves up, right, down or left. A move into a wall or the edge leaves it in place. Reaching the goal pays 1 and ends the episode. Every other move pays 0, and a time limit truncates the episode after 40 moves. A level's seed decides its goal and up to six walls.

We train two Q-learning agents on level 1000, the level whose seed is 1000, and they differ only in what they observe. The position agent sees its row and column. The view agent sees six numbers. The first four describe the cells up, right, down and left of it, 1 for a wall or the edge and 0 for an open cell. The last two say whether the goal is above, in the same row or below, and to the left, in the same column or to the right. Each agent trains for 3,000 episodes with ε = 0.1, a step size of 0.1 and γ = 0.9. Played greedily, both reach level 1000's goal at (2, 6) by a shortest route (Fig. 1, first panel).

We score an episode by its undiscounted return, 1 if the agent reaches the goal within 40 moves and 0 if not, so an average over levels is the share solved. Then we play both agents on 500 held-out levels, built from seeds that no training run uses. Over ten runs, each trained on one level of its own, the position agents solve 0.04 of them and the view agents 0.05.

The position agent plays level 1000's route on every level. On level 100066 the goal happens to lie on that route. On level 100005 a wall at (1, 3) blocks its fourth move, and since a blocked move leaves it in place, it repeats that move until the limit. On level 100008 it finishes the route at (2, 6), where level 1000's goal was, and moves back and forth beside it.

The view agent stops at views it never met. On level 100005 it reaches (0, 3), where the wall below and the open cell to the right make a view that level 1000 never showed it. Its table has no entry for that view, so all four estimates are 0 and argmax takes the first move, up, into the edge. Both agents memorized level 1000, one as a route and one as a table of that level's views, and only held-out levels could show it.

Zhang and colleagues measured memorization at its extreme: agents reached near-optimal training reward on small sets of mazes whose rewards were randomized per level, where good test reward was impossible.1

Each reset draws a level seed from a list the environment holds

make_level seeds a NumPy generator with a level's seed and draws the goal and the walls from it, so a seed always gives the same grid. Building levels with a program from seeds is called procedural generation, and Cobbe and colleagues built each level of their CoinRun game this way.2

Which level an episode plays is a second random choice. LevelGrid receives a list of level seeds when it is created, and each reset draws one with the environment's own generator, self.np_random. The same group's Procgen benchmark also uses two generators: one picks a level seed from the range its start_level and num_levels options set, and the other builds the level.3 Run k trains on seeds 1000k to 1000k + n − 1 for n levels and is scored on seeds 100000 to 100499. The lists share no seed, as in Zhang and colleagues' split.1

Gymnasium advises seeding reset once, right after the environment is created, not every episode,4 and Stable-Baselines3's vector environments reset each finished episode without a seed.5 With the list inside the environment, the one seed at the first reset fixes every level a run plays, and an evaluation names a level through reset's options.

Run on its own, levels.py passes both observations through check_env and prints what the next section uses.

The position agent starts at [0 0] on both levels. The view agent sees a wall below it on level 1000 and an open cell there on level 100008.

A policy of the position plays one route on every level

The position agent's failure follows from its observation. Its greedy policy is one table from cells to moves, whatever the level. From (0, 0) it makes the same first move on every level, and until a wall stops it, it visits the same cells in the same order. So it solves a held-out level exactly when that level's goal lies on its route before any wall, a rule that predicts every held-out result of run 1's position agent.

A stochastic policy follows no single route, but the same reasoning bounds it. Take any policy π that acts on the current observation, write Jℓ(π) for its expected return on level ℓ, and 𝔼ℓ for an average over levels from the generator. Held-out levels estimate the left side.

The first inequality holds because π is one of the policies in the maximum, and the second because choosing for each level separately does at least as well as one choice for all. The middle term depends only on the generator and the observation. More training levels change which π the agent ends with, but cannot lift its held-out return above that term.

For the position, the right side is the best return each level allows, because within one level the position is the whole state. The middle and right terms are equal only if one table of moves is optimal on almost every level. The inequalities hold for any return. For the shortfall, take the discounted return the agents learn from, under which optimal moves follow a shortest route. No table does that on two levels with goals at (6, 0) and (0, 6), since both show [0 0] at the start, where one needs down and the other right. No number of training levels closes that difference.

The same test works on any observation: look for two levels that show the same observation where they need different moves. For the view, toward_goal shows there are none on these levels. It moves toward the goal on whichever side is open, and it solves all 500 held-out levels by a shortest route. make_level keeps walls apart and out of the goal's row and column so that such a move always exists. In a maze with dead ends a policy of the current view can loop. Cobbe and colleagues gave their maze agent an LSTM network, “as memory is necessary for the agent to explore optimally.”2

The bound covers policies of the current observation. An agent that remembered past positions could notice a failed move and learn the walls within an episode, as a later stage's recurrent policies do.

With 64 training levels the view agent's held-out return meets its training return

How many training levels does the view agent need? heldout.py trains both agents on 1, 4, 16, 64 and 256 levels, ten runs per count and 3,000 episodes whatever the count. It scores each run on its training levels and on the 500 held-out levels, and prints the generalization gap, training return minus held-out return (Fig. 2).

heldout.py, complete

The view agent's held-out return rises from 0.05 at one level to 0.97 at 64, within 0.01 of its training return. Its table holds an entry only for a view the agent has updated. One level shows it few of the views that held-out levels contain, and each added level fills in more. After 256 levels run 1's view agent takes a shortest route on both levels of Fig. 3.

The last column of the output is the removal test. It hands each view missing from the table to toward_goal, leaves every learned entry alone, and plays the held-out levels again. For agents trained on one level, held-out return rises from 0.05 to 0.86, so missing views cause most of the shortfall. The lowest run reaches only 0.26, so there the entries it did learn, on its one level, also lead it astray. By 64 levels, filling changes nothing.

A few runs at 16 and 64 levels fail some of their own training levels, and their held-out return falls with them. Filling barely lifts these runs, so entries they learned are at fault, for reasons this lesson leaves untraced.

The position agent's gap closes because its training return falls

The position agent's held-out return stays near 0.04 at every count, while its training return falls from 1.00 at one level to 0.05 at 256: one table of moves cannot fit many training levels either. At 256 levels its gap is 0.01, as small as the view agent's at 64, for an agent that solves almost nothing.

Cobbe and colleagues treat the gap as the measurement, since “Train and test levels are drawn from the same distribution, so the gap between train and test performance determines the extent of overfitting.”2 Their own CoinRun gap with the Nature-CNN network shrinks from 32.66 points at 100 levels to 0.83 with unbounded levels, partly because training success falls from 99.45% to 90.87%.2 A random policy has a gap near zero.6 So a gap shows generalization only when the training return beside it is high.

The observation and the level count are necessary but do not decide the gap alone. At 4,000 CoinRun levels Cobbe and colleagues measured a gap of 11.83 points with the Nature-CNN and 1.83 with a different network, the IMPALA-CNN.2 The next lesson varies an environment's physical parameters instead of its layout, which is called domain randomization.

Problems

1. A version of the view keeps the four wall flags and drops the goal's direction. Can a policy that acts on it reach the goal by a shortest route on every level, given enough training levels?

Answer

No. Take two levels with no wall beside the start and goals at (6, 0) and (0, 6). Both show the flags [1 0 0 1] at the start, but the only shortest route to (6, 0) begins with down and the only one to (0, 6) with right. A policy of the flags makes the same choice on both, so by the bound no training set removes the shortfall.

2. Give the position agent its last two positions as one observation, the frame stack that gave Q-learning a rolling ball's speed. What does the stack add, and does the bound still apply?

Answer

Two equal positions show that the last move was blocked, so a policy of the stack can learn to turn after hitting a wall. That is layout information, but only after a failed move, and nothing about the goal. At the start every level shows [0 0] twice, so the levels of Problem 1 still need different first moves from one observation, and the bound applies.

The takeaway

Return on training levels cannot show whether an agent learned the task: position and view agents that solved their one training level solved 0.04 and 0.05 of held-out levels. Return on held-out levels from the same generator, with seeds kept out of training, can show it when the training return is read beside it. No policy of the current observation beats the best single policy for all levels. One table of positions cannot follow every level's route, so the position agent's gap closed only as its training return fell. The view supports one rule for every level, and after 64 training levels the view agent's held-out return came within 0.01 of its training return.
