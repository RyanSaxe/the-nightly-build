Reinforcement Learning

A cart that can't see its load brakes for the heaviest one

Domain randomization over Dock's load costs the 1 kg cart 0.22 s, all of it from a dock buffer that stops heavier loads only at lower speeds.

19 min read2026-11-23

Why this matters

A simulator's physical parameters are estimates, and a policy tuned to one guess can fail at the real value. One answer is to train on a range. Each reset draws the parameter from the range and holds it for the episode, as each reset drew a training level in the held-out levels lesson. This is called domain randomization. If the observation leaves the parameter out, a policy that acts on it makes one choice per observation for the whole range, so its best return is the middle term of that lesson's bound. That policy gives up return at a value of the parameter only where the best move depends on it. On a Dock with a random load, you solve for that policy exactly, find which loads pay and why, and check that Q-learning learns it.

At one state, a light load and a heavy load need opposite pushes

LoadedDock is Dock, the cart from the action-frequency lesson, carrying a load. Each reset draws the load's mass, 1, 2, 3 or 4 kg with equal chances, and keeps it for the episode. The drive holds every push at 1 m/s² whatever the load, so the mass never changes how the cart moves. It matters at the dock, whose buffer stops the cart only if its kinetic energy ½mv² is at most 0.5 J. That allows 1 m/s for a 1 kg load and 0.5 m/s for a 4 kg load. A faster arrival is a crash and pays −2 more, as rolling off the back end does.

Each decision now lasts 0.1 s, and every episode starts with the cart at rest at x = 0.2. The return drops the discount, so that every cost below reads in seconds. It is minus the seconds until the episode ends, minus 2 for a crash or a roll-off.

Take the 1 kg load first. Its best policy pushes right for eleven decisions, reaching 1.1 m/s, then brakes by pushing left for two and docks at 0.91 m/s after 1.29 s (Fig. 1). The 4 kg load has to arrive at 0.5 m/s or less. Its best policy pushes right for nine decisions and brakes for four, then pushes once more and brakes again to dock at 0.49 m/s after 1.51 s. Both paths reach x = 0.605 m at 0.9 m/s, and there they part. The 1 kg load's best move there is to push, and the 4 kg load's is to brake.

An agent that observes x and v sees the same state with every load, so its policy makes one move there for all four. A push leaves the cart at 1 m/s with 0.3 m to go. Braking would need 0.33 m to reach the 3 kg load's limit of 0.58 m/s, so the 3 kg and 4 kg loads crash, an expected cost of 1. Braking and then following the 4 kg load's path costs no load more than 0.22 s, since none docks sooner than the 1 kg load's 1.29 s. Crashing only the 4 kg load would still cost a quarter of 2, 0.5 on average. Both exceed 0.22 s, so the policy brakes for the heaviest load.

loads.py builds LoadedDock on dock.py from the action-frequency lesson. A push held for 0.1 s changes the speed by 0.1 m/s and moves the cart a whole number of 5 mm steps, so from a start on that grid every state stays on it. The observation reports the exact state as two integers, because qlearn.py's key() applies int(), which would cut a float position to 0 or 1. With integers its table gets one entry per (x, v), with nothing behind it but the load. Reset draws the mass with self.np_random, so the seed of a run's first reset fixes every load the run draws.1

Tobin and colleagues named domain randomization while training an object detector on randomized renders,2 after EPOpt and CAD2RL had trained RL policies in randomized simulators.34 Peng and colleagues drew a robot arm's dynamics parameters at the start of each episode, as LoadedDock does, and transferred the policy from simulation to the real arm.5

Averaging the dock's reward over the loads leaves one Dock to solve

A policy of (x, v) moves the cart the same way with every load, so only the reward depends on the mass, and only on the move that reaches the dock. Write rm(s, a) for the reward with load m, Jm(π) for policy π's expected return with that load, and p(m) for the chance of drawing m. The paths π produces have the same probabilities with every load, so the average over loads moves inside the sum over the path.

The right side is π's return in one ordinary Dock whose reward is r̄. Call it the averaged Dock. Arriving at 0.6 m/s pays −1 there on top of the time, since the 3 kg and 4 kg loads would crash. Both sides are equal for every π, so the policy of (x, v) with the best return averaged over the four loads is the averaged Dock's optimal policy.

The averaged Dock's best return is the middle term of the bound from the held-out levels lesson, with loads in place of levels.

A policy that also observes m can reach the right-hand term. Its state (x, v, m) never changes its m, so its Dock splits into one Dock per load, and its best policy plays each load's own best. The cost of hiding the mass at load m is that load's own best return minus the averaged Dock policy's return. It is zero at every load exactly when a single policy is optimal for each load separately.

exact.py finds that policy and each load's own by value iteration. It needs dock.py and loads.py and runs in about a second. Its list of the states the cart can reach is finite, since building it ends, and solve() sweeps until no value changes. It stops on every run, so its values solve the Bellman optimality equation.

The SARSA and Q-learning lesson showed that equation has one solution because the backup shrinks gaps by γ. With no discount that argument is gone. In any solution, a value equals its best move's reward plus the next value. Every move that doesn't end the episode pays −0.1, so values rise by 0.1 along best moves and never return to a state. Among finitely many states, the best moves must then end the episode, so each value is one finished path's return. Adding V(s) ≥ r + V(s′) along any other finished path puts the value at or above that path's return. So the only solution is the optimal values, and through Q(s, a) = r + V(s′) the optimal action values.

The light loads pay for docking at the 4 kg load's speed

The averaged Dock's policy follows the 4 kg load's own path and docks every load at 0.49 m/s after 1.51 s (Fig. 2). From this start, the cost of hiding the mass grows toward the light end of the range, from nothing at 4 kg to 0.22 s at 1 kg. A policy trained on the 1 kg load alone would do worse: it arrives at 0.91 m/s and would crash every heavier load.

As a check on the code, uv run exact.py --no-limit removes the buffer's rule, the mass's only effect. Every load's best policy then docks after 1.26 s, and hiding the mass costs nothing.

Q-learning with a load drawn every episode ends on the averaged Dock's policy

An agent that learns from episodes never sees r̄. Each episode shows it one load's reward. Equation (4) of the frame-stacking lesson says where its estimates rest anyway. Each entry Q(X, a) averages its target over the hidden states behind the observation X, weighted by each one's share of the visits to X. Behind each (x, v) here sit the four states (x, v, m). The load doesn't change where the cart goes, so under any policy every share is the draw's own ¼.

The second line is the averaged Dock's Bellman optimality equation, and no policy can move its shares, as ε moved the slow balls' share in Pitch. To this agent LoadedDock is an ordinary Dock with a random reward at the dock, so its estimates can rest only at the averaged Dock's optimal action values, the equation's one solution.

train.py runs q_learning from the frame-stacking lesson's qlearn.py, unchanged: no discount, ε = 0.1, and a step size of N(x, a)−0.7. The agent that can't see the mass trains for 20,000 episodes. The agent that sees it has four times as many entries, each visited with one load in four, so it trains for 80,000. train.py needs dock.py, qlearn.py, loads.py and exact.py, and ten runs of each agent took about four minutes here.

train.py, complete

Every run of the agent that can't see the mass docks every load after 1.51 s, the averaged Dock's policy. Every run of the agent that sees it earns each load's own best return. At x = 0.605 m and 0.9 m/s, every run ranks the two moves as exact.py does (Fig. 3). Two estimates for pushing have not reached exact.py's values in these runs: the hidden agent's is 0.04 too low and the 4 kg entry's 0.09 too high. The runs don't show why, and neither gap changes which move is greedy.

The hidden agent's entry is not the average of the four per-load entries. After either move it follows the averaged Dock's policy with every load, so its value for braking, −0.61, is the 4 kg load's, while each per-load entry follows that load's own best. As the frame-stacking lesson found, equation (4) does not give the share-weighted average of the hidden states' values.

Problems

1. Start the cart at x = 0.8 m, moving toward the dock at 1 m/s. Braking at every decision from there brings it in at about 0.77 m/s. Which loads can any policy dock, and which loads pay for hiding the mass?

Answer

Only the 1 kg load. Braking all the way arrives above the 2 kg load's limit of 0.71 m/s, so the heavier loads crash whatever the agent does. The averaged Dock's policy brakes just enough for the 1 kg load, which loses nothing. With no discount, each doomed load's own best is to crash as early as possible, so braking costs each of them 0.03 s. Here the cost falls on the heavy end of the range. uv run exact.py --start 0.8 1.0 prints the rows.

2. Suppose a push accelerated the cart by 1 m/s² divided by the load's mass in kg, so that a 4 kg load speeds up a quarter as fast as a 1 kg load. With the mass hidden, would every share in equation (4) of the frame-stacking lesson still be ¼?

Answer

No. One push from rest leaves a 1 kg load at 0.1 m/s and a 4 kg load at 0.025 m/s, so the loads pass through different states. How often each load reaches a state then depends on the policy, as in Pitch. The average over loads no longer moves inside the sum over the path, so the argument for the averaged Dock fails, and the speed itself carries information about the mass.

The takeaway

When the observation leaves out a parameter drawn at every reset, one policy has to serve every value of it. In LoadedDock the load changes only the dock's reward, so the best such policy is the optimum of one averaged Dock. Q-learning finds it, because the load never changes which states the cart visits. That policy loses return only where the best move depends on the mass. Because the buffer stops heavier loads only at lower speeds, every load braked for the heaviest, costing the 1 kg load 0.22 s. Observing the mass recovered each load's own best.
