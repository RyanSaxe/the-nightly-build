Reinforcement Learning

A progress reward paid as a change in potential leaves the optimal policy alone

Value iteration's arrows on the race do not move, and Q-learning under a 10-move time limit learns them only if the cell where the limit cuts each episode keeps its potential.

19 min read2026-11-20

Why this matters

In the race from specification gaming, a checkpoint that paid on every forward crossing made an endless loop optimal at γ = 0.99. Without the checkpoint, the agent has no reward before its first finish. A progress reward that also charges for lost progress can avoid the loop. Give each state a potential Φ and add γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) to the reward of each move from s to s′. Over an episode these shaping rewards sum to the last state's discounted potential minus the first state's. If the last state's potential is 0, or the episode never ends, every policy's value from a state shifts by the same amount and the optimal policy stays. Ng, Harada and Russell called this potential-based shaping. From that sum you can tell whether a progress reward keeps the optimal policy, and what potential it needs where an episode terminates and where a time limit cuts it.

The loop and the finish both collect 6 in shaping

Take the race from specification gaming at γ = 0.99. Keep its finish, which pays 10 and ends the episode, and remove the checkpoint that made looping optimal. In its place, give each cell a potential Φ equal to minus the moves left to the finish: −7 in cell 0, −1 in cell 6 and 0 at the finish. Each move from s to s′ now also pays γΦ(s′) − Φ(s), a shaping reward on top of the race's own.

A move forward from cell 1 pays 0.99 × (−5) − (−6) = 1.05, close to the old checkpoint's 1. The move back now costs something. From cell 2 to cell 1 it pays 0.99 × (−6) − (−5) = −0.94, so the agent hands back most of what it collected (Fig. 1).

One lap of the old loop from cell 1 collects 1.05 + 0.99 × (−0.94) = 0.12 in discounted shaping, so the loop seems to pay still. But the laps form a geometric series in γ², whose sum is 0.1194/(1 − 0.99²) = 6.00. The route straight from cell 1 to the finish also collects 6.00 in shaping, besides the finish's 10.

Both routes collect 6, which is minus cell 1's potential. Shaping adds the same amount to looping and to finishing, so finishing stays ahead by what the finish alone was worth.

The shaping rewards of an episode cancel in pairs

The two sums agree for a reason that holds on every route. Ng, Harada and Russell call a shaping reward of this form potential-based.1 Writing out the shaping rewards of an episode S0, S1, …, SN, each discounted as it enters the return, shows all but two of its potentials cancelling.

The move into each middle state St adds γtΦ(St), and the move out of it subtracts the same amount. Only the first and last potentials survive. Adding the sum to G0, the return under the race's own reward, gives the shaped return.

On the race the last term is always 0. An episode that finishes stops at the finish, whose potential is 0. One that never finishes has no last state, and γNΦ(SN) shrinks to 0 because every potential lies between −7 and 0. So every shaped return is the unshaped return minus Φ(S0). A value is an average of returns, and an action value only fixes the first move, so for every policy π:

The shift depends on neither π nor the move, so the best policy and the greedy move in each cell stay the same. That is Ng, Harada and Russell's theorem: under potential-based shaping, every optimal policy of the shaped task is optimal in the original, and the reverse.1 They prove it through the Bellman optimality equation, and they state the identity for every policy only in a remark, without proof.1 The cancellation proves it wherever the last term vanishes.

A lap starts and stops in one cell, so its shaping is -(1 - \gamma^n)\,\Phi(s) for n moves from s. That is positive on the race, where every potential before the finish is negative. At γ = 1, where Ng and colleagues first argue for a difference of potentials, every lap's shaping is 0.1 At 0.99 it is not, but an endless loop is one more episode whose own return is 0, so its shaped return is −Φ(S0), the same shift as every other episode's.

Without the charge for moving back, the loop returns

shaping.py checks the derivation with value iteration. shaped_rule adds the shaping reward to the reward of rule from race.py with the checkpoint at 0, and solve sweeps the Bellman optimality backup over it. The flag charge_back=False pays shaping on forward moves only, so the shaping rewards no longer cancel in pairs. Shaped, a Gymnasium wrapper for the next run, adds the shaping reward to each step. uv run shaping.py needs race.py in the same folder and runs in under a second.

With the charge, the optimal moves are the finish-only race's, all forward, and each value is the finish-only value minus the cell's potential, to within 4 × 10−15 (Fig. 2). Cell 0 rises by its 7 moves left, from 9.41 to 16.41.

Without the charge, the agent never finishes. A forward move still pays about 1 and a move back costs nothing, so the agent goes forward to cell 6 and then crosses between cells 5 and 6 forever, one move short of the finish.

Ng and colleagues also prove a converse: a shaping reward that is not potential-based changes the optimal policy of some task, though they note this says little when the task's rules are known.1 On the race the forward-only reward is one such reward, and Problem 1 below finds another.

The cut cell keeps its potential when the target bootstraps

The last term vanished because the finish has potential 0. A potential c there would add γNc to every episode that finishes on move N, an amount that depends on when the agent finishes and whether it does (Problem 2). The term vanishes in both kinds of task that Ng and colleagues' theorem covers. Their undiscounted tasks end in one absorbing state, whose potential can be shifted to 0, and their discounted tasks never end.1 For a discounted task that ends, like the race, Grześ shows that every terminal state needs potential 0.2

A time limit ends an episode differently. Termination and truncation showed that a cell where TimeLimit cuts an episode keeps its value, so the target bootstraps from it. The cut episode's shaping rewards still sum to γTΦ(ST) − Φ(S0), and Φ(ST) is not 0. Grześ, and Müller and Kudenko after him, require potential 0 in a state where an episode is cut after a fixed number of steps.23 Both treat the cut as a termination, with nothing after it.

With bootstrapping, the agent adds γT times its value for the cut cell, which under shaping is vπ(ST) − Φ(ST). Split the return at the cut, as that lesson did, and the cut cell's potential appears twice.

The two γTΦ(ST) terms cancel, leaving that lesson's unshaped target minus Φ(S0), the same shift as before. An agent that bootstraps through the cut therefore needs the cut cell's ordinary potential. Setting it to 0 drops the first term and leaves −γTΦ(ST) uncancelled. With Φ equal to minus the moves left, each cut then pays an extra 0.99 times the cut cell's moves left.

Zeroing the cut cell's potential pays the agent to stay back

limit.py tests the cut rule with Q-learning under a 10-move limit. The limit cuts a quarter of the episodes even in runs that learn to finish, since each episode starts in a random cell and half its moves are random. The other settings are those of learn.py in specification gaming: ε = 0.5, a constant step size of 0.1, 2,000 episodes and five runs per row. Each run is graded against the finish-only race's q*, minus Φ when shaped. The flag zero_at_cut sets the cut cell's potential to 0. limit.py needs race.py and shaping.py in the same folder and runs in about a second.

limit.py, complete

With the potential kept, every run ends within 0.001 of q* − Φ, and every greedy agent started in cell 0 finishes. With the potential set to 0, no greedy agent finishes. Cells 1 to 6 point back in all five runs, and the mean estimates run from 97 to 109, against 16.41 for the best policy from cell 0 (Fig. 3).

A cut in cell 0 now pays an extra 6.93, more than two thirds of the finish. An agent that stays near the start runs every episode to the limit, and 86% of episodes end in a cut. Since the target bootstraps through each cut, an estimate adds up the payments of all later cuts and climbs past any return the race can pay. Under a limit long enough that a trained agent is almost never cut, the two rules ended at nearly the same estimates in our checks.

Shaped Q-learning is Q-learning started from Φ

The kept-potential runs land on q* − Φ for a reason Wiewiora proved for tabular Q-learning.4 Tabular Q-learning that receives the shaping rewards from a starting table Q0 makes the same updates as unshaped Q-learning from Q0 + Φ, given the same experience. Under ε-greedy the two also choose their moves alike.4 Unshaped Q-learning settles at q* when it bootstraps through cuts, so the shaped agent settles at q* − Φ.

Shaping a tabular Q-learning agent therefore changes only its starting table, here minus the moves left, which lies below every optimal value on the race. The guarantee says nothing about how fast the agent gets there. Wiewiora, drawing on Koenig and Simmons, warns that an agent whose table starts below the optimal values can need learning time exponential in the size of the state and action spaces before it first finds the goal.4 The runs here did not measure speed.

Problems

1. The pay-once checkpoint in specification gaming pays 1 on the first crossing from cell 1 to cell 2 and records the payment in the state. Is it potential-based? At γ = 0.99, from which unpaid cells does the optimal agent turn back?

Answer

No. Staying in cell 0 pays 0, which forces Φ(0) = 0, and each move along the lane that pays 0 then forces the next cell's potential to 0, in both copies of the lane. The crossing would then pay 0, not 1.

From an unpaid cell j of 2 or more, turning back collects 1 on move j and finishes 5 moves later, worth \gamma^{j-1} + 10\gamma^{j+4}, against 10\gamma^{6-j} for going on. Turning back wins for j = 2 to 5 and loses at 6, by 9.995 to 10, and value_iteration(0.99, once=True) in race.py gives the same moves.

2. Give the finish a potential c in place of 0 and keep every other potential. For which c does the optimal agent at γ = 0.99 stop finishing?

Answer

From cell s, finishing on move N has shaped return 10\gamma^{N-1} - \Phi(s) + \gamma^N c, and never finishing has −Φ(s). Finishing wins when \gamma^N (10/\gamma + c) > 0, so for c > −10/γ ≈ −10.1 the agent finishes, and as soon as it can. Below that, every finishing route loses to never finishing.

The takeaway

A shaping reward γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) telescopes. An episode's shaped return is its return minus the first state's potential, plus γN times the potential where the return stops. When that term vanishes, every policy's value shifts by −Φ(s) and the optimal policy stays. On the race, with Φ equal to minus the moves left, the optimal moves match the finish-only race's, and paying for progress without charging for lost progress brings the loop back. A bootstrapped return does not stop at a time-limit cut, so the cut cell keeps its potential. Setting it to 0 stopped every Q-learning run under a 10-move limit from finishing.
