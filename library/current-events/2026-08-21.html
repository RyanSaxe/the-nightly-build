$40 trillion in federal debt arrives five months early

The federal debt hit $40 trillion five months ahead of pace, as this year's tariff refunds left customs receipts negative for a third straight month.

6 min read2026-08-21
National debt

$40 trillion in federal debt arrives five months early1

Treasury's daily count put total public debt at $40.047 trillion on August 18, up $3.8 trillion since Trump's second term began nineteen months ago and more than double the $19.95 trillion the government owed when he first took office in 2017.12

The pace outruns the round number. Debt held below $39 trillion as recently as March 17, so the most recent trillion arrived in five months. July's deficit alone ran $432 billion, and customs receipts have come in negative for three straight months. That means the government is refunding more in tariffs than it is collecting, the bill from the tariff program the Supreme Court struck down as unauthorized in February. Interest payments have grown to $1.1 trillion a year, now the second-largest line in the federal budget, behind Social Security.2

Immigration

A shield from ICE, but only for the plaintiffs3

Six Quaker meetings, a Sikh temple in Sacramento, and a network of 1,400 Baptist congregations just won something DHS's own policy said they couldn't have: a court order limiting immigration arrests at their doors. The Fourth Circuit ruled August 18 that DHS's 2025 policy, which replaced Biden-era limits on enforcement at "sensitive locations" with agents' own "common sense," likely violates the Religious Freedom Restoration Act by burdening the plaintiffs' ability to worship communally. One Baptist congregation's English classes lost 66 percent of their students after the policy took effect; DHS's own release announcing the change said the point was that "criminals will no longer be able to hide in America's schools and churches to avoid arrest."34

The government lost this case less on the facts than on its own silence. It never told the district court why letting agents work near pews serves a compelling government interest, so the appeals court held the argument waived. That leaves the underlying policy untouched everywhere else. The injunction covers only the plaintiffs' listed addresses, still allows warrant-authorized arrests, and says nothing about schools or hospitals, the other "sensitive locations" the old policy also protected. The next congregation that wants the same shield has to sue for it and build the same record.3

Second Amendment

A federal judge voids the ATF's ghost-gun rule, for four products5

Four years ago, the ATF redefined what counts as a gun. A year ago, the Supreme Court upheld its authority to do so. On August 17, a federal judge in Fort Worth ruled that the definition itself doesn't survive the Constitution. Judge Reed O'Connor found the rule's test for when a piece of metal or plastic becomes a regulated "frame or receiver," whether it can "readily" be finished, gives buyers and sellers no fair notice of where the line falls. He also found the colonial gun-safety laws the government cited as historical precedent addressed exploding barrels and stored gunpowder, not crime tracing, so they don't support treating unfinished parts as firearms.5

The ruling's declarations are broad; its relief isn't. O'Connor struck down the two regulations that define "frame or receiver" outright, but the injunction that follows protects only Defense Distributed, the Second Amendment Foundation's members, and four of Defense Distributed's own products by name. The ATF's rule stays in force against every other seller and buyer of unfinished gun kits in the country unless an appeals court says otherwise. The Justice Department can appeal to the Fifth Circuit, the court that mostly upheld O'Connor's earlier version of this ruling before the Supreme Court reversed it last year.56

Reproductive rights

What Indiana couldn't stop Planned Parenthood from telling minors7

Indiana's 2017 aid-or-assist law treated telling a pregnant minor about a legal abortion option the same as helping her get one: before a physician could even name a lawful out-of-state clinic, the law required the same notarized parental consent and government ID that Indiana requires to authorize the abortion itself. The Seventh Circuit ruled August 18 that the two aren't the same, and only one of them can be conditioned on a parent's notarized consent. Since Indiana bans nearly all abortions after Dobbs, minors seeking one already have to leave the state; the law would have made leaving contingent on first satisfying the very process a minor might be trying to avoid.7

The three-judge panel agreed on the outcome and split on why. The majority treated this as a speech case. The information is a phone search away, the law doesn't distinguish minors with parental support from those without it, and Indiana never showed the restriction protects the relationships it claims to protect. Judge Scudder's concurrence would uphold nearly all of the law, faulting only its notary requirement as one bureaucratic step too far, while preserving Indiana's underlying authority over what physicians tell minors about abortion. That gap outlasts this case. Indiana's law is one version of the "abortion trafficking" restrictions several states have adopted since Dobbs, and the next one a federal appeals court hears will land on a fracture already on the record.78

Elections

A Census Bureau fraud finding, unsigned9

The Census Bureau published a seven-page brief this week claiming "high confidence" that more than 24,000 noncitizens voted in the 2020 election, and Trump promoted it on social media as proof he won that race, saying the count would "explode" once the Bureau finishes checking 32 million more records. The brief carries no author's name, unusual for the agency. It isn't a vote count: it matches a commercial voter file called DataClear against federal immigration and Social Security records, then counts anyone whose citizenship it can't confirm as a noncitizen.910

The report's own numbers show the soft spot. Its source voter file matched only 91.7 percent of the ballots states certified to the federal government for 2020, as low as 74.5 percent in Utah. That means the underlying match already misses or misassigns real voters at a rate many times the fraud it claims to have found. Its rule for clearing someone as a citizen requires the match to turn up a dated naturalization oath, birth record, or passport; anyone whose citizenship exists but didn't surface in that search defaults into the noncitizen count. Twenty-four thousand voters out of 160 million works out to 0.015 percent, well within range for a matching process with an 8-to-25 percent miss rate to produce on its own, with nobody's vote at fault.9
