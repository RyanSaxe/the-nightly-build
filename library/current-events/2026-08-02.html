Trump pauses a planned Iran strike over a deal that only he has confirmed

Trump credits an unconfirmed deal for holding off an Iran strike, as the State Department tells Americans in ten Middle Eastern countries to consider leaving.

5 min read2026-08-02
Foreign Policy

Trump says Iran asked him to pause a strike in exchange for reopening the Strait of Hormuz1

Days after American and Iranian forces began striking each other directly, Trump posted on Truth Social Saturday night, Aug. 1 at 10:05 p.m., that Iran and "other Middle Eastern countries" had asked him to hold off because the "perimeters of a deal" were agreed. He said he was canceling the attack "subject to being able to rapidly make a DEAL." The terms he described: Iran fully reopens the Strait of Hormuz and ends its "nuclear threat," with Israel joining the commitment.1 The State Department separately urged Americans in ten countries, including Israel, Saudi Arabia and Qatar, to consider leaving the region.2 No Iranian or Israeli government has confirmed Trump's claimed deal. The closest independent corroboration is a request, not an agreement: Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman privately urged Trump to delay strikes, according to Axios and CNN reporting cited by Townhall.3 Trump has made this specific claim before. The Washington Post has reported he has "insisted countless times" that the Strait of Hormuz was already open "despite evidence to the contrary," the same claim he is now citing as the deal's centerpiece.4

Immigration

Judge Burroughs stays DHS's Somalia TPS termination for a second time5

U.S. District Judge Allison D. Burroughs, an Obama appointee in Massachusetts, issued a new administrative stay Aug. 1 blocking DHS from ending Temporary Protected Status for Somalia, after plaintiffs refiled their case as a Fifth Amendment equal-protection claim.5 DHS General Counsel James Percival called the repeated stays "defiance, obstruction, and delay."56 DHS's original notice, signed by Secretary Kristi Noem, had set the termination for March 17 and estimated 1,082 approved beneficiaries and 1,383 pending applications.7 The Supreme Court's June ruling in Mullin v. Doe closed off procedural challenges to TPS terminations under the Administrative Procedure Act. It left constitutional claims like this one open to judicial review, which is why the amended complaint survived where the earlier one hadn't.8

Health

The Americas region, not the U.S., lost measles-elimination status in November9

Measles cases in the United States reached 2,371 this year as of July 30, spread across 37 outbreaks, with 94% of cases, 2,219 of 2,371, tied to those outbreaks, according to the CDC.10 Kindergarten MMR vaccination coverage has fallen to 92.5% in the 2024–2025 school year from 95.2% in 2019–2020, below the roughly 95% coverage the CDC treats as necessary for community immunity.10 Coverage of the surge often treats the U.S. as having already lost its elimination status. It hasn't. The Americas region lost that status in November 2025, but the Pan American Health Organization attributes the loss to Canada's own re-established endemic transmission, not the United States.9 PAHO's Regional Verification Commission, the body that actually certifies elimination, will review the U.S.'s own national status separately, with a decision due in November 2026.9 Elimination requires no domestic transmission chain lasting 12 months or longer within a single virus genotype and lineage. The CDC first certified the U.S. met that standard in 2000 and re-verified it in 2012. PAHO is now applying the same standard to the current outbreak's yearlong clock, which started Jan. 20, 2025.911

Elections

UnidosUS and three allied groups build shared voter infrastructure for the first time12

UnidosUS, LULAC Institute, Mi Familia en Acción and Latino Victory Foundation launched a joint 2026 voter-mobilization campaign July 30, the first time the four have combined operations rather than run parallel ones. "For the first time, the nation's largest Latino organizations are building civic infrastructure together," the groups said.12 Their chief executives lead the coalition: Janet Murguía at UnidosUS, Juan Proaño at LULAC Institute, Hector Sanchez Barba at Mi Familia en Acción and Katharine Pichardo at Latino Victory Foundation.12 The coalition aims to contact more than 5 million Latino voters, register 250,000 new ones, and run 3.5 million door-knocks and one-on-one conversations toward a turnout increase of at least 5%.12 CBS reports the campaign will target nine states, including Arizona, Nevada, Pennsylvania and Texas, among the 36 million Latinos now eligible to vote nationally.13 Trump won 48% of the Latino vote in 2024, a 12-point gain from his prior share, but CBS cites polling showing roughly a quarter of those voters are now unwilling to back him again.13 "Our national organizations have always been aligned in working towards civic engagement, but we've never come together and formally partnered," Murguía told CBS.13
