Current Events

U.S. and Iran are now striking each other directly

The Middle East conflict the Federal Reserve cited as it held rates over three dissents has become an open exchange of fire, over a strained economy that just absorbed the end of a cap on seniors' drug premiums.

3 min read2026-07-30
National Security

U.S. forces strike dozens of IRGC sites after Iran fires on American troops1

U.S. Central Command finished a heavy wave of strikes late on July 29. It hit dozens of Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps targets across Iran, from command centers to missile and drone plants to coastal and maritime sites.1 The strikes came a day after Iranian ballistic missiles aimed at American forces in the region were intercepted.1 This is direct fire between the two states, not the proxy skirmishing of recent months. CENTCOM tied its purpose to protecting "American forces, commercial shipping, and neighboring Gulf countries," a reach toward the Strait of Hormuz and the oil that moves through it.1 Independent reporting confirmed the barrage and the scale of the response.2 CENTCOM gave no count of targets destroyed, and the fighting now shadows the day's economic news.1

Monetary Policy

The Fed holds rates, but three governors voted to raise them3

The Federal Open Market Committee left its benchmark range at 3.5 to 3.75 percent on July 29.3 The hold reads hawkish once the vote is counted. Three members dissented in favor of a quarter-point hike: Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari and Lorie Logan.3 Three votes to tighten is not a move toward rate cuts. It is the committee holding the line against inflation that has run above its 2 percent target for more than five years.3 Chair Kevin Warsh said the Fed has "no tolerance" for elevated inflation, and that five years of it "cannot be cured in nine weeks."4 The statement attributed part of the "elevated uncertainty" to the conflict in the Middle East.3

Health Policy

Medicare ends the subsidy that held down drug-plan premiums5

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services said on July 28 it will end a temporary subsidy after this year.5 The program had capped premium increases for stand-alone Medicare prescription-drug plans. Dropping it returns that market to "traditional market conditions" in 2027.5 The subsidy was not small. It cut the average enrollee's monthly premium by about $26 in 2025 and $16 in 2026, at a cost of roughly $9.8 billion, across the nearly 25 million people in stand-alone Part D plans.6 CMS set the 2027 national base beneficiary premium at $41.33 and says sponsors can now price without help.5 The independent read is that some enrollees face a larger premium increase next year than they have seen in a while.6 The per-plan figure is not yet set. Final 2027 premiums arrive in September.6

Household Economy

Consumers' view of the economy right now fell for a third straight month7

The Conference Board's headline Consumer Confidence Index slipped just 1.4 points to 90.8 in July.7 The small move understates the shift. The Present Situation Index, which rates business and job conditions today, dropped 3.6 points to 114.9, its third straight monthly decline.7 The Expectations Index held at 74.7, below the 80 mark the Board treats as a recession warning.78 The survey ran July 1 to 22, spanning the Middle East conflict and still-high grocery and gas prices.8 Missing a 92.3 forecast, it shows households growing warier of the economy in front of them, not only the one ahead.8
