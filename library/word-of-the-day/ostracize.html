Word of the Day

Athens exiled its powerful men by scratching names on broken pottery

Once a year the Athenian assembly could send a man into exile for ten years with no accusation and no trial, and the verb ostracize is what that ballot left in the language.

3 min read2026-08-28
ostracize verb /ˈä-strə-ˌsīz/

To exclude someone from a group or society by the common consent of its members.1

The ballot was a broken pot

Ostracize traces back to a potsherd. The Greek verb ostrakizein meant to banish a man by a vote written on potsherds, and beneath it sits ostrakon, an earthen vessel or a piece of one broken off.1 The Athenians named the act for its ballot, a shard of broken pottery scratched with a single name.1

Ten years gone, property kept

Ostracism ran to a fixed calendar. Once a year the Athenian assembly put a single question to a vote: should an ostracism be held?2 If the answer was yes, a day was set, usually about two months later.3 On the day, citizens filled the agora, each scratched a name onto an ostrakon, and dropped it inside a railed enclosure.4 The man named on the most sherds had to leave Attica for ten years.4 He was charged with nothing, kept his property and its income, and could be recalled early.43 The law was Cleisthenes', and the Athenians first used it around 488 BCE, two years after Marathon, against Hipparchus son of Charmus, a relative of the former tyrants.5

The sherds, and one good story

Little of this depends on trust, because the ballots survive. Excavators have pulled inscribed ostraka from the ground by the thousand: some 8,500 from a single dump tied to a vote around 471 BCE, and about 9,000 catalogued from the Kerameikos cemetery alone.6 The names scratched into them read like a register of famous Athenians: Themistocles, Aristides, Cimon, Pericles.3 Plutarch says the Athenians moved against Themistocles as they habitually did against anyone they judged too powerful.7 One find is more unsettling: a cache of ballots against Themistocles, from a well on the Acropolis's north slope, had been prepared in advance and never used, a sign the vote could be worked from behind the scenes.6

The most repeated story rests on one late witness. Plutarch tells it of Aristides, the statesman called "the Just": on voting day an illiterate citizen who did not recognize him asked him to scratch "Aristides" onto a sherd. Aristides asked what wrong the man had suffered at his hands.

Plutarch's anecdote
None whatever, I don't even know the fellow, but I am tired of hearing him everywhere called "The Just."4 the voter's reply, in Plutarch, Life of Aristides 7

Aristides, the story goes, wrote his own name and handed the sherd back.4 No fifth-century source records the exchange. Plutarch set it down around 100 CE, six centuries after the ostracisms of the 480s, in a Life written to improve its readers.4 Even the rule that made a vote valid is unsettled. Plutarch says 6,000 sherds had to be cast in all, or the vote did not count.4 The earlier Athenian historian Philochorus says instead that 6,000 had to fall against the single man.3 The excavated sherds are firm ground. The anecdote is a good story, attested late.

Why the word outlived the vote

Ostracism was a fifth-century Athenian affair, and might have stayed one.1 Instead the word traveled. Ostracism entered English by 1588 and the verb ostracize by 1649,81 carrying at first the narrow Athenian sense, then the broad one it holds now: to shut someone out of a group by common consent, no assembly required.1 The procedure fell away and left the act it had formalized.

Today the word works far from any agora. A 2020 study of people frozen out of a group opens plainly: "Being ignored and excluded is an intensely painful experience."9 That is ostracize with the potsherds gone: the act the Athenians surrounded with a yearly vote, and the rest of us manage without one.
