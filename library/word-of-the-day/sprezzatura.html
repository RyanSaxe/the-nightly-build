Word of the Day

Castiglione's word for effortless mastery diagnoses its own fakers

The same test that caught a Renaissance dancer trying too hard now grades figure skaters for looking like they aren't.

4 min read2026-08-02

A word invented against trying too hard

sprezzatura Italian noun /sprāt-tsä-ˈtü-rä/

Studied nonchalance: graceful conduct or performance without apparent effort.1

At the court of Urbino, in the dialogue Baldassare Castiglione set on four evenings in 1507, Count Lodovico da Canossa warns the room away from affectation.2 Short of a word for it, Castiglione gives him one, in Leonard Eckstein Opdycke's 1903 translation. Affectation is to be avoided, he says, “to the uttermost and as it were a very sharp and dangerous rock.” And, “to use possibly a new word,” he tells them “to practise in everything a certain nonchalance that shall conceal design and show that what is done and said is done without effort and almost without thought.”3 In Castiglione's own Italian: “una certa sprezzatura, che nasconda l'arte,” a certain sprezzatura that conceals the art.4

Castiglione names his invention sprezzatura, coined as a cure, not a compliment, for affettazione: affectation, the visible strain of trying too hard. Four centuries on, Merriam-Webster still tags it an Italian noun, not a naturalized English headword.1 His own dialogue shows how quickly the cure curdles back into the disease.

The trap built into the cure

Three paragraphs after coining the word, Castiglione's dialogue turns it on two dancers. One, Pierpaolo, dances with “legs stiff to the toe and head motionless, as if he were a stick,” so carefully that he seems to be counting his own steps.3 The other, Messer Roberto, overcorrects: nonchalance so theatrical the room can see him working at it. The Count refuses to let either pass as sprezzatura. “Do you not perceive that what you call nonchalance in messer Roberto, is really affectation?” he asks. “For it is clearly seen that he is striving with all his might to seem to be taking no thought, and this is taking too much thought.”3 Sprezzatura sits in the gap between the two dancers, and the gap is narrow. Prove you did not try, and you have proven that you did.

An idea older than the word

Castiglione's speaker has a model in mind, though he never names it: “I remember having once read that there were several very excellent orators of antiquity, who among their other devices strove to make everyone believe that they had no knowledge of letters.” Hiding their knowledge, he says, “they pretended that their orations were composed very simply and as if springing rather from nature and truth than from study and art.”3 Cicero had already written the specific case: in De Oratore, the Roman orators Crassus and Antonius each cultivate the impression that they never studied rhetoric at all, so their eloquence looks natural rather than trained.5 Ovid gives the idea its tag, in a scene Castiglione never mentions: carving the ivory statue that becomes Pygmalion's living wife, so lifelike that “ars adeo latet arte sua,” so does art conceal art.6 Castiglione cites none of them. The current he draws on was already old by 1507.

Still being judged for it

The pattern is still scored. Writing in The Philosopher in January 2024, nearly five hundred years after Castiglione published the book, philosopher Helen De Cruz applies sprezzatura to lute and harpsichord performance, where visible strain breaks a piece as surely as a wrong note.7 She also points to figure skating's International Judging System, which rewards “the use of effortless power to accelerate and vary speed.”7

Sprezzatura comes from sprezzare, to disdain, itself rooted in the Latin pretium, worth: literally, to devalue.8 The term for making mastery look free was built from the verb for stripping value away. A judge scoring a skater for effortless power and a Renaissance count docking a dancer for trying are running the same arithmetic, four centuries apart. The performance is worth more exactly when it looks like it cost nothing at all.
