A priest, not Parnell, coined the word ‘boycott’

Captain Boycott's neighbors in County Mayo withdrew their labor, service, and trade so completely in the autumn of 1880 that it cost the government some £10,000 to bring in the harvest they left standing.

3 min read2026-08-17
boycott verb /ˈbɔɪkɒt/

To take part in a concerted refusal to deal with a person, store, or organization, usually to express disapproval or force acceptance of certain conditions.1

The agent no one on Lough Mask would serve

Charles Cunningham Boycott collected the rents on Lord Erne's estate at Lough Mask in County Mayo, running some 1,500 acres for a tenth of the roughly £500 a year in rent.2 In September 1880 he issued eviction processes against eleven tenants who had fallen behind, and the Irish National Land League met it with an organized withdrawal of labor, service, and trade.2 His workers left, the shops would not serve him, and his harvest stood uncut.

Parnell supplied the tactic, a priest the word

The method had a text, weeks before it had a name. At Ennis on 19 September 1880, Charles Stewart Parnell told a crowd how to treat a man who took a farm cleared by eviction. Shun him "in the streets of the town" and "in the shop," he said, and put him "into a moral Coventry ... as if he were the leper of old."3 The speech gave the campaign its tactic. It did not give it a word.

That came days later, in conversation. James Redpath, an American journalist, wanted a term the country people would use. "Ostracism," he complained, would mean nothing to them.2 Fr John O'Malley, a priest in the district, supplied the answer.

The coinage
"How would it do to call it to Boycott him?"2 Fr John O'Malley to James Redpath, late September 1880

O'Malley is credited with the coinage, offered because "ostracise" was a word people would not remember.4 Redpath carried it into print, first in the Chicago Inter-Ocean that October.2 The accounts split on whether the dinner fell on 23 or 25 September, but none of them places the word in Parnell's mouth.2

Ten thousand pounds for a harvest worth a few hundred

Boycott's crops still stood rotting, so the government brought them in. About fifty Orangemen from Cavan and Monaghan did the digging that November, guarded by roughly a thousand troops and police.42

The expedition cost the public purse near £10,000 to save a crop the sources value between £350 and £500.42 Parnell jeered that the state had paid "one shilling for every turnip dug from Boycott's land."2 The surname moved faster than the men who made it. It was quickly taken up by newspapers in languages as far off as Japanese, where it became boikotto.5

A refusal that has to come from below

The word kept a narrow shape. Britannica calls a boycott a collective and organized ostracism against conduct regarded as unfair.6 What the definition leaves implicit the Land League made plain: pressure from below, people choosing together to withhold their custom. That line separates a boycott from an embargo or sanction, imposed from above, and from a simple ban.

The scholarship keeps that actor in view. Defining the consumer boycott in 1991, Monroe Friedman called it "an attempt by one or more parties to achieve certain objectives by urging individual consumers to refrain from making selected purchases in the marketplace."7 The urging, and the individual left free to heed it or not, are what separate a boycott from an order. The word still works on those terms: since 2012 Ethical Consumer has called for a boycott of Amazon over its tax avoidance and asks shoppers to pledge to join.8

The boundary shows most clearly where the word is declined. The largest American consumer action of 2025, a one-day withdrawal on 28 February, called itself an "economic blackout," and its organizers held to that phrase even as reporters reached for "buying boycott."9 A blackout can be switched on from a center. A boycott is the thing people have to be talked into joining.
