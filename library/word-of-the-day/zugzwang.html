Word of the Day

Zugzwang means having to move is the disadvantage

An anonymous Belfast newspaper writer used the English word in 1894, eleven years before the citation historians usually credit.

4 min read2026-08-01

A Belfast newspaper, 1894

zugzwang noun /ˈzʌɡzwæŋ/, /ˈzuːɡzwæŋ/

The necessity of moving in chess when it is to one's disadvantage.1

An unsigned notice in Belfast's Ulster Echo used the German word in English print on 19 December 1894: “The position is a perfect example of what the Germans call Zugzwang.”2 No earlier English use has been found. The citation usually credited as the first, Emanuel Lasker's own chess magazine, is dated more than a decade later, in February 1905.3

The word before the citation

The word itself is older than either citation. In September 1858, an unsigned article titled “Zugzwang, Zugwahl und Privilegien” ran across six pages of the Deutsche Schachzeitung, the leading German chess magazine of the period.3 German chess writers used it routinely within decades: reporting on the 1896 world championship match between Wilhelm Steinitz and Emanuel Lasker, the same magazine said flatly that White had “died of Zugzwang.”3

English took longer to adopt it, and even the 1905 citation still treats it as foreign. Lasker's Chess Magazine, describing a Marshall–Janowsky game that February, printed the word inside quotation marks: “White has struggled bravely and only loses by ‘Zugzwang.’”3 The word did not fully naturalize into English chess writing until 1929, when the translator of Aron Nimzowitsch's strategy manual My System left it untranslated rather than force it into an awkward English phrase.3

The compulsion, not the choice

Casual usage treats zugzwang as a synonym for a hard choice, or having no good options. Nimzowitsch's own strategy manual shows what that reading misses. He described a player's position degrading step by step until, forced by “the necessity of making a move,” the player must weaken a key point and the game collapses.4 The position is not yet lost while the compulsion is only building. It collapses once a move is forced. If the rules let the player pass, the same position holds indefinitely. The disadvantage was never one of the available moves. It was the requirement to make one at all.

Not every dictionary states this as precisely. Oxford Learner's Dictionaries defines zugzwang as being “forced to move a piece into a bad position, especially one from which the piece will be lost.”5 That definition describes a common symptom. Plenty of true zugzwang losses cost no piece at all, just a king or pawn shuffled onto a worse square. Merriam-Webster's phrasing holds up better: the compulsion to move is itself what is “to one's disadvantage.”1

Chess authorities do not fully agree on how strict the label should be, either. The critic Wolfgang Heidenfeld argued in 1972 that true zugzwang requires the compulsion to flip the result, turning a draw or a loss into a win, not just make a bad position worse. On that stricter test he rejected the most cited example in English chess writing, the 1923 Sämisch–Nimzowitsch game still nicknamed “the Immortal Zugzwang Game,” calling the label “nonsense.”3 The broader definition, the one the dictionaries and Nimzowitsch's own book use, is the one that travels beyond chess.

The same test off the board

The word now turns up regularly outside chess, and the accurate uses keep the same structure intact. The war-studies scholar Lawrence Freedman used it in 2023 to describe a position in the Russia–Ukraine war: “Legal moves are available and so one must be chosen, but they are all bad.”6 He located the strain exactly where the chess definition puts it: in the running-out of options that forces a commitment, committing reserves, exposing a flank, before the front line visibly moves at all.6

A 2015 analysis from the Finnish Institute of International Affairs used the term the same way, and made the same distinction the chess definition makes. Russia's position, the authors argued, was not a stalemate, a static deadlock where nothing forces anyone's hand, but a “zugzwang”: a worsening compulsion to act.7 The test for whether the word applies anywhere is simple: whether standing still has stopped being one of the options.
