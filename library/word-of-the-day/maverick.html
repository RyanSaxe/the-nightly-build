Neglect explains why Samuel Maverick's cattle went unbranded

The record doesn't even agree on how Maverick got the cattle, and the principled-refusal version most people repeat traces to a single 2008 broadcast.

3 min read2026-08-14
maverick noun /ˈma-v(ə-)rik/

An unbranded range animal, especially a motherless calf.1

A peninsula farm that came with a brand already on it

In February 1847, Samuel Augustus Maverick, a San Antonio lawyer, bought a farm on Texas's Matagorda Peninsula from Charles Tilton. The farm came with roughly four hundred longhorn cattle, already wearing Tilton's brand.2 Maverick left the herd's day-to-day care to Jack, a nineteen-year-old enslaved man, while he pursued land surveys and, soon after, a legislative seat. Jack asked for help managing the animals. None came.2 Few of the new calves were ever branded, and neighbors began reporting losses while Maverick, busy surveying and legislating, paid the herd little attention.2

From an absentee owner's herd to everyone's stray

By 1854, seven years of neglect had reduced the free-ranging herd to about 250 head, and Maverick had his sons drive what was left two hundred miles to a new ranch.2 Two years later he sold the herd outright to Augustine Toutant-Beauregard, who sent his own cowboys through the surrounding counties to gather up every unbranded animal they could find and call it a maverick. That roundup, not anything Samuel Maverick did himself, is what put the word into ranch talk and then into newspapers.2 By 1872, Maximilian Schele de Vere's dictionary of Americanisms had made the link formal, tying the word in print to Maverick's own name.2

A humane motive that traces to one 2008 broadcast

The story most people repeat today is different: that Maverick refused to brand his cattle on principle. A 2008 NPR segment quoting the language columnist William Safire attributes a specific version to Maverick himself: he "didn't want to be cruel to animals." No source is named for it.3 The same segment also reports a competing rumor from Maverick's own neighbors: that he left the cattle unbranded on purpose to claim any stray on the range as his own.3 Two independent scholarly accounts reject that version too. The South Carolina Encyclopedia calls it "legend" and says Maverick was simply "an indifferent cattleman" who was out of the cattle business by the mid-1850s.4 A Texas Standard piece by UT Rio Grande Valley professor W. F. Strong calls the same story "not true": Maverick, he writes, "was not all that interested in ranching. He was a land baron, a real estate investor."5 None of the three sources that examine his motives in any depth mention a humane reason.

The record does not even agree on how Maverick acquired the cattle in the first place. Merriam-Webster's own retelling has a client settling a legal debt with "about 450" head; the Historical Association's biographers, drawing on a 2017 biography, say he simply bought a peninsula farm the cattle came with.12 Neither version is footnoted to a deed, a letter, or the family's own papers. Only one of them is footnoted to a book.

Never claimed, not merely departed

By the century's end, Merriam-Webster's own word-history essay says, the word had already generalized past cattle, describing people "like cattle left to their own devices" who "blaze their own trails."6 Wiktionary's citation record dates the standalone figurative sense to the 1880s and preserves a line printed in Collier's in 1924, putting the new sense in the first person: "As a voter, I'm a maverick, don't belong to any party."7

The distinction is grammatical. The speaker doesn't say he left a party. He says he was never in one. The literal sense works the same way: an unbranded calf isn't an animal that broke away from a herd it belonged to. It is one that was never claimed, so it belongs to no one until whoever finds it brands it.2 That is a different image than "rebel," which needs a structure to rebel against, or "nonconformist," which needs a norm it is refusing. Maverick is the one of the three that was simply never claimed. That is why the same unbranded calf can read two ways at once: an admirable holdout, or an unclaimed stray up for grabs, depending on who is doing the branding.
