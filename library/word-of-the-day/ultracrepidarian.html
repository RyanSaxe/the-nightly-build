Word of the Day

The critic Hazlitt called ultracrepidarian had once been a shoemaker

It enters the printed record in 1819, carrying a Greek painter's rebuke to a cobbler who faulted a sandal and then presumed to judge the leg.

3 min read2026-08-05
ultracrepidarian adjective · noun /uhl-truh-krep-i-DAIR-ee-uhn/

Offering opinions or criticism on matters beyond one's knowledge or competence, or a person who does so.1

Above the sandal

A shoemaker once looked at a painting and was right. The sandal in it was missing a loop, he pointed out, and the painter fixed it. Emboldened, he came back the next day and started in on the leg. This time the painter put his head out from behind the panel and told him a cobbler should not judge above the sandal. The painter was Apelles, the most celebrated of the Greeks, and Pliny the Elder set the scene down in his Natural History, noting that the rebuke itself passed into a proverb.2

The proverb
Ne supra crepidam sutor iudicaret.2 A shoemaker, in his criticism, must not go beyond the sandal.3 Pliny, Natural History 35.85

Hazlitt hands it to Gifford

Seventeen centuries later, in another language, the proverb came back as a name for a person. By then Pliny's supra crepidam, “above the sandal,” had hardened into the tag later Latin carried as ultra crepidam, “beyond” it, and it was that sharper, later form the English word took its prefix from.1 In 1819 William Hazlitt used it, in A Letter to William Gifford, Esq., a book-length attack on Gifford, the editor of the Quarterly Review. One sentence carried the charge: “You have been well called an Ultra-Crepidarian critic.”4 Gifford had been apprenticed to a shoemaker in his youth, which gave the sandal a second edge.5

Hazlitt wrote well called, not I call you, presenting the epithet as one already applied to Gifford, and a later editor of Hazlitt's works proposed that Charles Lamb had coined it instead.6 So 1819 fixes the word's first appearance in the printed record, not the moment anyone invented it.5 The barb stuck to its target either way: Leigh Hunt aimed it at Gifford again in 1823, in a verse satire he titled Ultra-Crepidarius.6

The overreach it names

It is tempting to file ultracrepidarian with dilettante or presumptuous, as a word for anyone who holds forth past what they know. The cobbler draws a narrower line. He was genuinely competent. He knew sandals, and his first correction was correct. His fault was carrying that standing into the leg, where his trade gave him no claim. The word names that exact move: not ignorance in general, but competence in one place spent as though it covered the next.

The distinction still earns its keep. Dictionary.com illustrates the current sense with a line from a 2023 Salon essay, a writer who “toss[es] the phrase ultracrepidarian at them because they don't know what they're talking about” — them being the online personalities who narrate every subject at once.1 Apelles had to step out from behind his painting to deliver the rebuke. Now the whole of it fits in a single word, and anyone can throw it.
