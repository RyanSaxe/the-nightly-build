A minister of austerity gave his name to a fashion for portraits

Étienne de Silhouette ran Louis XV's finances for eight months in 1759, and no one can prove why his name settled on the cut-paper portrait rather than on the austerity that first made it a joke.

3 min read2026-08-03
silhouette noun \ˌsi-lə-ˈwet\

The image of a figure or object reduced to its flat outline and filled in solid, most familiar as a profile cut from black paper and mounted on a light ground.1

The minister who left no portrait

The word belongs to a person. Étienne de Silhouette (1709–1767) was controller-general of finances to Louis XV.2 Merriam-Webster traces the noun straight back to him.1 He held the office for roughly eight months in 1759, long enough for his surname to become a common noun and not much longer.3 No authenticated portrait of the man survives, not even a silhouette.2

Taxing doors and melting silver

Silhouette owed the post to Madame de Pompadour's patronage, and he inherited a treasury deep in the Seven Years' War.3 Income of 286 million livres stood against 503 million in expenses, including at least 94 million in debt service.2 His remedy, a "general subvention," taxed the visible signs of wealth: doors and windows, farms, servants, luxury goods, and profits.2 On 26 October 1759 he ordered gold and silver plate melted down.2 The cuts reached the court and the aristocracy, and they ended him. Even Voltaire objected, and he was dismissed on 21 November.23 What stayed behind was the adjective: anything skimped or done on the cheap was "à la Silhouette."4

A profile reported before the ministry

Why the name fastened to the shadow-profile is genuinely unsettled. Three accounts compete, and no contemporary source decides among them. The first is ridicule. His critics branded him a skinflint, and cheap, pocketless breeches were dubbed "culottes à la Silhouette." Austere fashions and arts were mockingly tagged the same way, a dig at his economies.34 The second is his own pastime. Writing for Oxford, Simon Kemp describes Silhouette as an enthusiast who would "sit guests ... in front of a blank canvas, before using a special lamp to project their shadow onto it for him to draw around."3 The third is his tenure: Merriam-Webster derives the word "perhaps from his ephemeral tenure" in office.1

The dating problem

Word Histories, citing Henri-Joseph Dulaurens's 1763 L'Arétin moderne, reports that "portraits à la Silhouëtte" were already being made in 1758, before Silhouette took office in March 1759. If that date holds, the name cannot have begun as a jibe at a 1759 austerity program, and the hobby account gains ground. The Dulaurens text could not be examined here.5

Word Histories, able to read the French sources, judges the hobby account the most persuasive of the three while conceding that none is proven.5

The word outlives its minister

The sense migrated the way the fashion did. The portrait sense appears in English by 1792, the general sense of any dark outline in profile by 1843, and a garment's contour by 1901.4 Even the word's English debut is disputed: Merriam-Webster dates the noun to 1783, while the OED places it at 1798, in The Monthly Review.15 The cut-paper portrait itself peaked from the later 1700s into the early 1800s.6

By the time Arthur Conan Doyle reached for the word, the man was gone and only the shape was left. In 1891 Watson sees Holmes "pass twice in a dark silhouette against the blind," the plain outline-against-light sense.7 In 1903 the older meaning still showed through: a wax bust of Holmes in profile resembles "those black silhouettes which our grandparents loved to frame."8 Neither passage explains the name, because by then the word no longer needed the man. The minister who taxed windows to close a deficit is forgotten. The shape he may or may not have traced by lamplight is what anyone now means by a figure standing dark against the light.7
