Word of the Day

Anosognosia marks the difference between not admitting a loss and not registering it

Joseph Babinski named it in June 1914 for stroke patients who insisted their paralyzed arms still worked, and it now marks why many people with schizophrenia never accept they are ill.

3 min read2026-08-08
anosognosia noun \ˌa-nō-ˌsäg-ˈnō-zh(ē-)ə\

An inability or refusal to recognize a defect or disorder that is clinically evident.1

The paralysis the patient did not notice

In June 1914 the neurologist Joseph Babinski reported to the Société de Neurologie de Paris on patients whose strokes had paralyzed one side of the body and who did not know they were paralyzed.2 They were lucid about most things. Asked to move the dead arm, they denied there was anything wrong with it.2 Babinski proposed a name for the sign, assembled from the Greek a-, nosos, and gnosis: a not-knowing of one's own disease.3 In the same report he set apart a milder companion condition, anosodiaphoria, for patients who granted the paralysis but met it with indifference.2

The coinage
I have observed in patients with organic hemiplegia an absence of awareness of their paralysis, which I propose to designate by the term anosognosia.2 Joseph Babinski, Revue Neurologique, 1914 (English translation)

Anton saw it first, without the word

The behavior was older than Babinski's term for it. In the 1890s the Austrian neuropsychiatrist Gabriel Anton had described patients who could not see or could not hear yet insisted their sight and hearing were intact.4 Anton had the phenomenon. Babinski supplied the word, and coined it for paralysis alone.5 When later neurologists recognized the same denial running through Anton's blindness cases and Babinski's hemiplegia, the unawareness of cortical blindness kept a joined name: Anton-Babinski syndrome.4

The line between refusing and not registering

This is the distinction the word protects. English already has denial, and from the outside the two behave alike: a person faced with an obvious impairment who carries on as if it were not there. In the clinic they are not the same thing. Denial is a psychological defense, in which a person can register the deficit and works to minimize it.6 Anosognosia is the deficit reaching its own detector: the injury that took the function also took the capacity to notice the function is gone.6 The standard clinical view keeps the two apart even while granting that they are hard to fully separate.6 It also marks the word off from the Dunning-Kruger effect, which is a metacognitive miscalibration in cognitively healthy people rather than an organic unawareness produced by brain injury.6

Why the word outlived the stroke ward

Babinski's stroke sign has since widened into a general term for unawareness of illness, and it does its heaviest work in psychiatry. StatPearls reports that 50 to 90 percent of people with schizophrenia and about 40 percent of people with bipolar disorder show anosognosia or a severe lack of insight.6 Read this way, a patient's refusal of treatment need not be stubbornness: the illness has concealed itself from the one person living inside it, and that unawareness is itself a leading reason people stop taking their medication.6

That is the sense worth keeping. Ordinary language has many words for people who will not see what is wrong with them and none for people who cannot. The pattern is not confined to the clinic. Most of us are, on some subject, unequipped to notice the gap where a faculty should sit. Anosognosia is the exact name for that failure, which is why a term born on a Paris stroke ward now earns its place in a psychiatry clinic and in plainer speech beyond it.
